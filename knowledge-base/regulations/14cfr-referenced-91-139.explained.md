This section sets up how the FAA tells pilots about emergency air traffic rules, and it names who may issue those notices. Here is the chain it describes. If the Administrator decides an emergency condition exists, or will exist, affecting the FAA's ability to run the air traffic control system — a condition under which normal flight operations cannot be conducted with the required levels of safety and efficiency — then the Administrator issues an air traffic rule or regulation that takes effect immediately. The Administrator or the Associate Administrator for Air Traffic may then use the Notice to Airmen (NOTAM) system to announce that the rule was issued. Those NOTAMs carry the substance: what rules govern flight operations, how navigation facilities may be used, and which airspace the rules apply in. Paragraph (c) is the part that binds you. Once a NOTAM has been issued under this section, no person may operate an aircraft, or any other device the regulation covers, inside the designated airspace unless they follow the authorizations, terms, and conditions written into the regulation the NOTAM covers. Note the breadth of "other device governed by the regulation concerned" — the prohibition is not limited to crewed aircraft. The practical takeaway for a remote pilot is that checking NOTAMs is how you find out an emergency rule exists at all, because these rules are effective immediately and are announced rather than published in advance.

Illustration: A remote pilot plans an early-morning mapping flight over a business park. During preflight planning she checks NOTAMs and finds one issued under the emergency air traffic rules provision: a regional ATC facility has suffered a failure, an immediately effective air traffic rule is in place, and the NOTAM designates the airspace it covers and the conditions for operating there. Her flight area falls inside that designated airspace, and the conditions in the regulation do not cover what she intends to do. She cancels the flight and reschedules. Her reason: paragraph (c) says no person may operate an aircraft or other device governed by the regulation inside the designated airspace except in accordance with the authorizations, terms, and conditions the regulation prescribes — so with no applicable authorization, the flight is simply not permitted.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people assume an emergency air traffic rule only restricts crewed airliners and airport traffic, since it stems from an air traffic control system problem. The text reaches any "aircraft, or other device governed by the regulation concerned," so whether a small unmanned aircraft is covered depends on the regulation the NOTAM covers, not on size. Second, people read paragraph (c) as a total ban on flying in the designated airspace. It is not a flat prohibition — it is a requirement to comply with the authorizations, terms, and conditions in the underlying regulation. If those terms permit your operation, you may operate under them; if they are silent or exclude you, you may not.
