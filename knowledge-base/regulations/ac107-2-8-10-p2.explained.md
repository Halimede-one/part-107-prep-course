This part of the advisory circular (AC) describes the duties of a manufacturer that holds an FAA-accepted Declaration of Compliance (DOC) for a small unmanned aircraft approved to fly over people. The duties fall into a few pieces. First, if the aircraft is no longer eligible for one or more of the specified categories of operations over people, the holder notifies the public, using one of the forms described in paragraph 8.10.2.1 (see that paragraph), and the notice should tell remote pilots the aircraft is no longer eligible. The FAA expects the holder to use due diligence so the intended audience actually gets word of potential unsafe conditions, and expects applicants to build a system for communicating with owners, because owners and operators need to know their aircraft may have a safety defect. Second, a DOC holder must notify the FAA of any safety issue it identifies. Manufacturers should maintain and review information that could reveal a safety issue leading to noncompliance for operations over people — including, but not limited to, consumer complaints, warranty returns, insurance claims or payments, product liability lawsuits, reports of production problems, product testing, or other critical analyses. Reporting does not automatically mean the FAA will find the aircraft ineligible or require corrective action; the FAA evaluates the report and works with the holder to decide whether corrective action is appropriate. Reports may be submitted through the FAA website. The notification should describe the nature of the safety issue and how the manufacturer, the remote pilot, or another party will fix it. Once fixed, the applicant submits a DOC to the FAA for acceptance, and once accepted, remote pilots may again operate that aircraft over people in the declared category or categories. If the holder is unable or unwilling to address the issue, the FAA may begin the rescission process described in paragraph 8.9.5 (see that paragraph). Importantly, a safety issue does not necessarily make the aircraft unusable under part 107 at all — it only makes it ineligible for Category 2 or 3 operations over people without a waiver, exemption, or an FAA-accepted corrective action. If the holder identifies a corrective action, it tells owners, operators, and the FAA how the issue can be resolved; whoever implements that corrective action must use an FAA-accepted Means of Compliance (MOC) to show the aircraft meets the safety requirements for Category 2 or 3 operations over people. Finally, for building many aircraft of the same make, model, and series, the FAA encourages manufacturers to keep a production quality system or design configuration control system so each unit repeatably matches what the DOC describes; with such a system an applicant can avoid testing every unit it builds. The FAA may request access to facilities to validate compliance with applicable industry consensus standards and FAA regulations, and by submitting the DOC the applicant agrees to allow unrestricted access to its facilities on FAA request.

Illustration: You fly a Category 2 aircraft for a real estate company and routinely cross over sidewalks with pedestrians on them. One morning the manufacturer emails registered owners: a batch of propeller hubs can crack, so the model is no longer eligible for operations over people until a replacement part is installed. That notice is exactly the public notification this paragraph describes. Your decision: stop the over-people portion of your flights. You can still fly the aircraft under part 107 — a safety issue like this does not necessarily ground the aircraft entirely — but until the fix is made and a new DOC is accepted by the FAA, Category 2 operations over people need a waiver or exemption. After you install the replacement hubs and the manufacturer's corrected DOC is accepted, you may again fly over people in the declared category.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read a safety issue as grounding the aircraft completely; the chunk says the opposite — it would only render the aircraft ineligible for Category 2 or 3 operations over people without a waiver, exemption, or accepted corrective action. Second, people assume reporting a safety issue to the FAA automatically triggers loss of eligibility or mandatory corrective action; the chunk says it does not, and the FAA evaluates the report and works with the DOC holder to decide. Also note the reporting duty here sits with the DOC holder, the manufacturer, not with the remote pilot — but the remote pilot is the one who must stop flying over people once notified.
