This part of the advisory circular describes what the manufacturer-side party — the FAA calls it the "applicant," the one who declared the aircraft compliant — must hand over with a small unmanned aircraft that is eligible for Category 2 or Category 3 operations over people. The applicant must provide remote pilot operating instructions when the aircraft is sold or transferred, or when someone other than the applicant uses it, and should keep those instructions up to date as the aircraft changes.

The instructions must contain at least three things. First, general information, a system description, and system limitations, including which category or categories of operations over people the aircraft is eligible for. If a specific component has to be on the aircraft for it to meet the performance-based safety requirements of Category 2 or Category 3 — a parachute system or similar device supplied separately, for example — the instructions must clearly identify that component, and must also list components that are eligible or necessary for inclusion on the aircraft. Second, a statement about allowable modifications. If modifications are allowed, the instructions must fully describe the modifications the applicant determined do not change eligibility, covering both the aircraft itself and any payload anyone might add. Those described modifications must be consistent with the basis on which the FAA accepted the Declaration of Compliance (DOC). Anyone who modifies the aircraft in a way that affects its eligibility to operate over people under Category 2 or Category 3 must submit a new DOC for FAA acceptance before the aircraft is eligible to operate over people. Third, a statement saying whether the aircraft has variable modes or configurations. If it does, the instructions must explain how a remote pilot can verify which mode or configuration the aircraft is in and how to switch between them, and must let a pilot who wants a different category — or a mode allowed under part 107 but not allowed over people — work out what is needed. The pilot should not be able to change the mode or configuration by accident.

The instructions must be specific to that particular aircraft design. The applicant can either update its existing manual to add the required information or write a separate set of instructions for operations over people. There is no required format: packaging insert, electronic file, or any other means is acceptable. Whatever the method, the applicant should keep them current, and remote pilots in command should be able to tell clearly which version of the instructions is in effect at the time of the planned operation over people.

Illustration: You buy a used quadcopter from another operator, and the seller tells you it is good for Category 2 flights over people. Nothing comes with it but the airframe and batteries. Because the aircraft is being transferred, the applicant's remote pilot operating instructions should be available to you, so you find them on the manufacturer's website. They state that the aircraft only meets the Category 2 performance-based safety requirements with the manufacturer's parachute module attached, and that the module ships separately. They also describe a "sport" mode that is permitted under part 107 but not permitted for operations over people, along with how to check which mode the aircraft is in. Your decision: do not fly over people until the parachute module is installed and you have confirmed the aircraft is in the correct mode, because the instructions identify that component and that mode as conditions of the Category 2 eligibility.

Common misreading: People read this as a rule they themselves must satisfy before flying, and look for a pilot checklist item. The obligations here fall on the applicant that declared compliance — producing the instructions, keeping them current, making them design-specific. The pilot's stake is different: the instructions are how you learn what component must be attached, what modifications are allowed, and which mode the aircraft must be in. A second misreading is thinking that because an applicant lists allowable modifications, any modification is fine. Only the modifications the applicant describes as not changing eligibility are covered; a modification that affects Category 2 or Category 3 eligibility requires a new DOC accepted by the FAA before the aircraft may operate over people.
