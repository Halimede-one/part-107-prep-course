This section is a rule for manufacturers, not for pilots. It applies to anyone producing an unmanned aircraft under a design approval or production approval issued under part 21 of the regulations — the same approval process used for type-certificated aircraft. After September 16, 2022, a producer may not build such an aircraft for operation in United States airspace unless two things are true. First, all the applicable part 21 requirements are met. Second, the aircraft either (1) is designed and produced to meet the minimum performance requirements for a standard remote identification unmanned aircraft set out in § 89.310, following a means of compliance the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) has accepted, or (2) carries Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast (ADS-B) Out equipment meeting § 91.225. Those two options are alternatives — meeting either one satisfies paragraph (b). For the details of what § 89.310 and § 91.225 require, see those sections.

Illustration: A company is building a large cargo drone under a part 21 production approval and plans to deliver the first units in 2024. Because the date is after September 16, 2022, the company cannot simply build the airframe and ship it. It must satisfy part 21 and then choose a path for identification: design the aircraft to the § 89.310 standard remote identification performance requirements using an FAA-accepted means of compliance, or install ADS-B Out equipment that meets § 91.225. The engineering team picks ADS-B Out because the aircraft already operates in controlled airspace and carries a transponder, so paragraph (b)(2) is the cheaper route for them.

Common misreading: Pilots sometimes read this as the rule that governs their own hobby or commercial drone. It is not. This section only reaches aircraft produced under a part 21 design or production approval, and it places the duty on the producer. It also does not say every drone must have remote identification built in — ADS-B Out equipment meeting § 91.225 is an accepted alternative here.
