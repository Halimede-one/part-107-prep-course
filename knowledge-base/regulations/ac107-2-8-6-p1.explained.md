This part of the advisory circular (AC) describes a means of compliance (MOC) that the FAA has already accepted, so an applicant does not have to invent one. It applies to the impact kinetic energy and exposed rotating parts requirements at 107.120(a)(1) and (2) for Category 2 and 107.130(a)(1) and (2) for Category 3 — the categories for operating a small unmanned aircraft over people. Applicants are still free to propose a different MOC and the FAA may accept it; this one is simply available. The FAA-provided MOC is a calculation, not a crash simulation: it does not account for impact dynamics or other factors, and consists only of using the formula the FAA describes to calculate the aircraft's maximum kinetic energy. Under this MOC the applicant confirms three things: (1) the impact does not exceed a certain kinetic energy limit, (2) the aircraft has no exposed rotating parts, and (3) it has no safety defects.

Section 8.6.1 lays out how to get the speeds that feed the calculation. First, find the maximum forward airspeed the aircraft can reach at full power in level flight, using a reliable and accurate airspeed measurement method under typical environmental conditions — a Global Positioning System (GPS) groundspeed indicator, a radar gun, or a tape measure and stopwatch are all offered as examples. Second (8.6.1.1), find the ground impact speed from an unpowered free fall starting at the highest altitude the aircraft can reach at full power, which can be measured by a drop test using a reliable and accurate vertical speed measurement method. If a drop test from the highest attainable altitude is unreasonable, 8.6.1.2 allows a drop test from a lower altitude, as long as that altitude is high enough to determine the aircraft's free-fall aerodynamic characteristics — such as coefficient of drag and terminal velocity — well enough to calculate the impact speed from the highest attainable altitude accurately. The manufacturer states the environmental conditions under which the maximum speeds were determined in the supporting data submitted under 107.155, and describes any unique test conditions for both the level flight and free-fall scenarios.

Section 8.6.1.3 explains the limits of this approach. The tests cover speeds reachable before or during a typical failure, such as losing power and falling with both a vertical and horizontal speed component. They do not cover failure modes or pilot actions that would push the aircraft past those speeds — a powered descent that impacts faster than an unpowered free fall is the example given. The FAA assumes such events are not typical and, while possible, are unlikely. But if an applicant concludes that such a failure or pilot action could typically occur and produce higher speeds, the applicant should use the higher speeds in the kinetic energy calculation. Then, per 8.6.1.4, the applicant takes the horizontal and vertical maximum speeds and determines the highest combination the aircraft could reach from a typical failure, which yields the maximum impact kinetic energy. The applicant should identify and assess typical failures from system or equipment loss of function or malfunction, and also those that could be caused by pilot error.

Two part 107 reminders are embedded in the text: aircraft operated under part 107 may not exceed the speed limitations in part 107 unless authorized by a Certificate of Waiver (CoW) or an exemption (107.51(a)), and may not exceed the altitude limitations of that rule unless authorized by a CoW or an exemption (107.51(b)). The specific numeric limits are in 107.51 — see that section.

Illustration: A small company has built a 1.3-pound quadcopter and wants to declare it eligible for Category 2 so customers can fly it over people. Rather than develop its own means of compliance, the company uses the FAA-provided MOC. An engineer flies the aircraft at full power in level flight across an open field and records 44 miles per hour peak groundspeed on the GPS indicator, logging the temperature, pressure, and wind for the record. The team decides a drop test from the aircraft's full ceiling is impractical at their site, so under 8.6.1.2 they drop it from a lower altitude, measure the descent profile with a vertical speed sensor, derive the coefficient of drag and terminal velocity, and calculate the free-fall impact speed from the highest attainable altitude. They then review failure modes: loss of a motor, loss of link, and likely pilot errors. They find nothing that would typically produce a faster impact than the combined horizontal and vertical speeds they measured, so they combine those speeds into the worst typical case and run the FAA's formula to get maximum impact kinetic energy. They also confirm the propellers are shrouded, so there are no exposed rotating parts, and that there are no safety defects. All of this, including the environmental conditions and the unique test setup, goes into the supporting data submitted under 107.155.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, remote pilots sometimes think these tests are something they must perform. They are not — this MOC is for the applicant, typically the manufacturer, seeking a Category 2 or Category 3 declaration. A pilot's job is to fly an aircraft that already carries the right labeling. Second, people read the measurement steps as permission to fly fast and high: the aircraft's maximum attainable speed and altitude are what get measured for the calculation, but the AC states plainly that part 107 operations may not exceed part 107's speed and altitude limits without a Certificate of Waiver or an exemption. A third, subtler misreading is treating the FAA-provided MOC as a full safety analysis — the chunk says it does not account for impact dynamics or other factors, and that if atypical failures or pilot actions could realistically produce higher speeds, the higher speeds must be used.
