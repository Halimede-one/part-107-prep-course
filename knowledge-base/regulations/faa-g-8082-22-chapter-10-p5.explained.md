This part of Chapter 10 covers three related ideas: a hazard-spotting checklist, two styles of decision-making, and the traps and stress that degrade both.

The PAVE checklist is the first step of the 3P model. Its goal is building situational awareness by perceiving hazards — present events, objects, or circumstances that could contribute to an undesired future event. The pilot systematically identifies and lists hazards in four areas: Pilot, Aircraft, enVironment, and External pressures. All four combine and interact, so every flight is a unique situation. The study guide singles out the pilot-aircraft combination for special attention: ask whether the combined "pilot-aircraft team" can handle the mission. A very experienced, proficient pilot still has limited weather flying ability in an unfamiliar aircraft; conversely, a pilot may have a new technically advanced aircraft they have flown for a considerable amount of time.

Analytical decision-making uses models such as the 5 Ps, 3P, and DECIDE. Good decisions come from gathering all available information, reviewing it, analyzing options, rating options, selecting a course of action, and then evaluating that course of action for correctness. But time is not always available. The guide uses a quarterback as the example: he intends to execute a plan, but new circumstances force decisions on the fly. That is automatic or naturalistic decision-making. Research over several decades shows that experts pressed for time first assess whether a situation strikes them as familiar, then quickly imagine how one or a few courses of action will play out, rather than weighing pros and cons of many options. They take the first workable option they find — not necessarily the best possible choice, but one that often yields remarkably good results. This holds across experts from firefighters to chess players, and appears to hinge on recognizing patterns and consistencies that clarify options in complex situations. Experts make provisional sense of a situation without actually reaching a decision, launching experience-based actions that then trigger creative revisions. It is reflexive, anchored in training and experience, and used most often in emergencies when there is no time for analytical decision-making. It improves with training and experience, and most pilots end up using a mix of tools matched to their own experience and training.

Operational pitfalls are the flip side of experience. More experienced pilots make more automatic decisions, but experience also brings tendencies — classic behavioral traps. As a rule, more experienced pilots try to complete a flight as planned, and that desire to meet the goal can hurt safety and lead to an unrealistic assessment of piloting skills. These patterns must be identified and eliminated.

On stress: everyone is stressed to some degree almost all of the time, and some stress is good because it keeps a person alert and prevents complacency. Effects of stress are cumulative and, if not handled appropriately, can add up to an intolerable burden. Performance generally rises with the onset of stress, peaks, then falls off rapidly once stress exceeds the person's ability to cope. Stress can impair in-flight decision-making. The two categories are acute and chronic, both explained in Chapter 9, "Physiological Factors (Including Drugs and Alcohol) Affecting Pilot Performance" — see that chapter for the details.

Illustration: A remote pilot is hired to photograph a construction site and runs PAVE the morning of the flight. Pilot: he slept poorly and is three weeks out from his last flight. Aircraft: it is a new, larger quadcopter he bought last week and has flown twice. Environment: gusty wind, cranes and a partly built steel frame around the subject. External pressures: the client is flying in that afternoon and wants the images before he leaves. Looking at the pilot-aircraft pair specifically, he realizes that his general proficiency does not carry over — he has little feel for how this unfamiliar airframe handles gusts near obstacles, and he will be relying on automatic, reflexive reactions he has not yet built for this aircraft. He also recognizes the external pressure as the classic trap of pushing to complete the flight as planned. He reschedules for the calm early-morning window the next day and spends the afternoon flying the new aircraft in open ground to build familiarity. Reason: the hazards stacked in all four PAVE areas at once, and his automatic decision-making had no pattern library for this aircraft to fall back on if something went wrong.

Common misreading: Two common ones. First, people read automatic decision-making as a shortcut they can choose instead of the analytical models. The chunk describes it as reflexive, anchored in training and experience, and used most often in emergencies when there is no time for analytical decision-making — it is what experience produces under time pressure, not a method a new pilot can select. Second, people assume stress is simply bad for performance. The chunk says some stress is good because it keeps a person alert and prevents complacency; performance increases with the onset of stress and peaks before falling off rapidly once stress exceeds the ability to cope.
