This is the opening of Appendix A of Advisory Circular (AC) 107-2A, which the FAA offers as guidance, not as a rule. It presents aeronautical decision-making (ADM), Crew Resource Management (CRM), and one example of a risk assessment process for finding hazards and sorting how much risk each could bring to an operation. It also gives examples of criteria a remote pilot in command (PIC) of a small unmanned aircraft might use to judge how severe a consequence would be and how likely it is to happen.

The ADM steps listed are: (1) Identify personal attitudes hazardous to safe flight. The chunk says unawareness of these hazards can lead to getting behind the aircraft or situation, operating without adequate fuel or battery reserve, losing positional or situational awareness, operating outside the envelope, and failing to finish flight planning, preflight inspections, and checklists; operational pressure feeds these pitfalls. (2) Learn behavior modification techniques — keep using risk assessment procedures, and run an attitude assessment to spot a hazardous attitude. (3) Learn to recognize and cope with stress. Small unmanned aircraft system (small UAS) work can bring stressors unlike those in manned aviation, such as working with an inexperienced crewmember, lack of standard crewmember training, dealing with the public and city officials, and understanding new regulatory requirements; good planning can reduce or remove that stress. (4) Develop risk assessment skills — identify hazards first, then analyze likelihood and severity to establish the probability of risk, then mitigate or even eliminate it. The chunk names visual observers (VO), a thorough preflight inspection, weather planning, familiarity with the airspace and operational area, proper aircraft loading, and performance planning as mitigations. Figure A-1 is offered as one example tool, and the chunk says other tools are also available. (5) Use all available resources when there is more than one crewmember, which is CRM. CRM means an environment where open communication is encouraged and expected and the whole crew is involved to maximize team performance. Crewmembers such as VOs and other ground crew can supply information on traffic, airspace, weather, equipment, and aircraft loading and performance, and during operations over people or moving vehicles can report on people not directly participating.

Three CRM examples follow. Communication procedures: the VO keeps eyes on the aircraft and stays aware of the surrounding airspace and operational area, then passes flight status and hazards to the remote PIC and the person manipulating the controls so action can be taken; as conditions change, the remote PIC should brief the crew on the changes and the adjustments needed. Communication methods: the remote PIC, person manipulating the controls, and VO must work out a method such as a handheld radio or other effective means that does not create a distraction and lets them understand each other, and the remote PIC should pick the method before flight. Task management: tasks vary with the complexity of the operation, more crewmembers may be needed depending on the area, and the remote PIC should use enough crewmembers that nobody becomes overloaded, because an overworked team member raises the chance of an incident or accident.

Illustration: A remote PIC is hired to film a downtown street festival from above a closed block. She works the risk assessment in the order the appendix describes. Hazards first: crowds at the edge of the closed block, tall buildings that could cut the control link, and a VO she has never flown with. Likelihood and severity next, then mitigations drawn from the list in A.2.4 — she adds a second VO so one can watch the aircraft while the other watches for people wandering past the barricades, and she reviews the airspace and the building heights beforehand. For communication method, handheld radios beat shouting across a noisy festival, so she issues radios and tests them before launch, settling the method before flight as A.2.5.2 says to. Midway through, the wind picks up and a side street opens to foot traffic. Because conditions have changed, she briefs both VOs on the new traffic flow and the shortened orbit she will fly. Her reason for every one of these choices is the same: she identified a hazard, judged the risk, and took an action that lowered it rather than flying and hoping.

Common misreading: People read this appendix as a mandatory checklist and assume a VO, a handheld radio, or Figure A-1 is required equipment. The chunk presents all of it as an example process — it says Figure A-1 is 'an example of a risk assessment tool' and that 'others are also available for use.' The one piece of firm language here is A.2.5.2's statement that the remote PIC, person manipulating the controls, and VO must work out a method of communication — which applies when you are actually using those crewmembers, not a mandate to staff a crew. The flip side of the misreading is just as common: treating the appendix as optional reading and skipping risk assessment entirely. It is guidance on how to do something the FAA expects a remote PIC to do.
