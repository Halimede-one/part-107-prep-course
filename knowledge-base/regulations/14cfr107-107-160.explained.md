This section is about the paperwork a manufacturer (or other applicant) files to say its small unmanned aircraft qualifies for Category 2 or Category 3 operations over people. That filing is called a declaration of compliance. Paragraph (a) lists what must be in it: the applicant's name, physical address, and email address; the aircraft make and model name, and series if applicable; the serial number or range of serial numbers covered; whether this is an initial or amended declaration, and if amended, why it was re-submitted; which accepted means of compliance was used to meet § 107.120(a) or § 107.130(a) or both; a set of declarations by the applicant; and any other information the Administrator requires. The declarations in (a)(9) are that the applicant has shown the aircraft (or specific configurations of it) meets § 107.120(a) or § 107.130(a) or both through that accepted means of compliance, has verified the aircraft contains no safety defects, has satisfied § 107.120(b)(3) or § 107.130(b)(3) or both, and will let the Administrator inspect its facilities, technical data, and manufactured aircraft and witness tests needed to determine compliance. See those sections for what each requires. Under (b), the FAA notifies the applicant when it accepts the declaration — acceptance is an FAA action, not something the applicant grants itself.

The rest of the section covers taking that acceptance away. Before starting rescission proceedings under (d)(1) through (3), the FAA will notify the applicant that a safety issue has been identified. The FAA may rescind a declaration if the aircraft no longer complies with § 107.120(a) or § 107.130(a), if the declaration violates § 107.5(a), or if the Administrator determines an emergency exists related to safety under the authority in 49 U.S.C. 46105. The normal process: the FAA issues a notice proposing rescission, stating its basis, and the holder has 30 calendar days from the date of issuance to submit evidence refuting it and showing how the aircraft meets this subpart. If the FAA does not receive that information within those 30 calendar days, it issues a notice rescinding the declaration. In an emergency under (d)(2)(iii), the FAA uses 49 U.S.C. 46105(c) to issue a rescission order without going through that 30-day process at all.

The part that reaches remote pilots is (d)(1): no person may operate a small unmanned aircraft identified on a rescinded declaration of compliance while that declaration is rescinded. A holder hit with a rescission order under (d)(3) may petition the FAA to reconsider, in a manner specified by the Administrator, within 60 days of the date the rescission was issued. The petition must show at least one of three things: a material fact that was not in the original response to the safety-issue notification plus an explanation of why it was absent; a material factual error by the FAA in deciding to rescind; or that the FAA did not correctly interpret a law, regulation, or precedent. The FAA then issues a notice either affirming or withdrawing the rescission. Finally, part 13, subpart D of this chapter does not apply to the procedures in paragraphs (d) and (e).

Illustration: You fly real estate shoots and your aircraft is a Category 2 model you routinely fly over small groups of people in a parking lot. One morning you see a manufacturer bulletin and an FAA notice: the declaration of compliance covering your serial number range has been rescinded. Nothing is physically wrong with your aircraft — it flies fine and you have logged no malfunctions. You still cannot fly it over people under Category 2 that day. Paragraph (d)(1) says no person may operate an aircraft identified on a rescinded declaration while the rescission stands, and your serial number is in the covered range. You call the client, reschedule the over-people portion, and wait to see whether the manufacturer's petition to reconsider results in the FAA withdrawing the rescission.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, pilots skim this as pure manufacturer business and miss paragraph (d)(1), which binds the operator directly — the rescission does not just embarrass the manufacturer, it grounds the identified aircraft. Second, people read the 30 calendar days and the 60 days as deadlines that apply to them. They do not. Those clocks belong to the holder of the declaration of compliance: 30 calendar days to answer a proposed rescission, 60 days to petition for reconsideration. Nothing here gives a remote pilot a grace period to keep flying while the manufacturer argues its case. Also note the emergency path — if the Administrator determines a safety emergency exists, the rescission order can issue immediately with no 30-day window beforehand.
