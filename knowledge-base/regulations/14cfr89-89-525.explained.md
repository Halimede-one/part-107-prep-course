This section tells manufacturers how to mark what they build. It applies to the person producing the equipment, not to the pilot flying it.

Paragraph (a): nobody may produce a standard remote identification unmanned aircraft under § 89.515 unless the aircraft displays a label saying it meets the requirements of this part. Paragraph (b) says the same for a remote identification broadcast module produced under § 89.520 — the module itself must carry the label.

The label has four requirements in both cases, and they are identical: it must be in English, legible, prominent, and permanently affixed. "Permanently affixed" means it is part of the product, not a sticker in the box or a line in the manual. (For what § 89.515 and § 89.520 actually require of the equipment, see those sections.)

Illustration: A remote pilot is shopping for a broadcast module to add to an older drone that has no built-in remote identification. Two modules are on the table. One has a small plate on its underside, in English, stating that the equipment meets the requirements of 14 CFR part 89. The other has nothing on the housing — the seller points to a line on the cardboard packaging instead. Under this section, the label must be permanently affixed to the broadcast module, so the packaging claim does not satisfy what a compliant producer was required to do. The pilot buys the labeled one.

Common misreading: People read this as a duty on the pilot — as if you could be cited for flying a drone whose label has worn off, or as if you could bring a drone into compliance by printing and attaching your own label. Neither follows. The prohibition here runs against the person who produces the equipment. A label you make yourself does not make non-conforming equipment conforming; it only tells buyers something that may not be true.
