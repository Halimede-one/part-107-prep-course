This section sets the operating rules a remote pilot in command must follow to fly a Category 3 operation over people. There are two parts: the aircraft requirement and the flight-conduct requirement.

First, the aircraft. It must be eligible for Category 3 operations under § 107.130(a), it must appear on an FAA-accepted declaration of compliance as eligible for Category 3 in accordance with § 107.160, and it must be labeled as eligible for Category 3 in accordance with § 107.130(b)(1). All three apply together. See those sections for what each one actually requires.

Second, the flight itself. Category 3 flatly prohibits operating over open-air assemblies of human beings. Beyond that, you may fly above a human being only if one of two conditions is met. Condition one: the operation is within or over a closed- or restricted-access site, and everyone inside that site has been put on notice that a small unmanned aircraft may fly over them. Condition two: the aircraft does not maintain sustained flight over any human being — with two exceptions to that limit. Sustained flight over a person is allowed if the person is directly participating in the operation of the small unmanned aircraft, or if the person is under a covered structure or inside a stationary vehicle that can provide reasonable protection from a falling small unmanned aircraft. Note the wording: "covered structure" and "stationary vehicle." A moving vehicle does not satisfy this condition.

Illustration: A remote pilot is hired to shoot progress photos of a warehouse renovation. The aircraft is Category 3 eligible, is on an FAA-accepted declaration of compliance for Category 3, and carries the Category 3 label. The job site is fenced with controlled gates, so it is a restricted-access site. The pilot and the site superintendent brief the crew at the morning toolbox talk that a drone will be flying overhead that day, and the superintendent posts a notice at the gate. Because everyone inside the site is on notice, the pilot may fly above the workers under condition (c)(1) — no need to avoid sustained flight over them.

That afternoon the client asks for a shot of the public parking lot across the street, where shoppers are walking to their cars. The pilot declines that shot as flown. The parking lot is not a closed- or restricted-access site, those people are not on notice, and they are not participating in the operation or under a covered structure. The pilot could only transit over that area without maintaining sustained flight over anyone — and if a crowd had gathered, the open-air assembly prohibition in paragraph (b) would rule it out entirely.

Common misreading: Two common slips. First, people read the "covered structure or stationary vehicle" exception as a general permission to fly over cars and buildings, forgetting that the vehicle must be stationary — a car moving down a road does not count. Second, people assume that because a site is fenced, Category 3 lets them hover over workers. The closed- or restricted-access condition only works if everyone inside has been put on notice; a fence alone is not notice. And no notice, no fence, and no exception overrides paragraph (b) — open-air assemblies are off limits in Category 3 regardless of the other conditions.
