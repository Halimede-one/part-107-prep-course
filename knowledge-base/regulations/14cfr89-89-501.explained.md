This section sets the scope of a subpart of Part 89, the Remote Identification rule. Note what it governs: the design and production side, meaning the manufacturers and builders who make the equipment, not the day-to-day flying of it. Paragraph (a) lists four things the subpart covers: design and production requirements for unmanned aircraft that have remote identification built in and are produced for operation in United States airspace; design and production requirements for remote identification broadcast modules (add-on devices that broadcast identification); the procedures for submitting, accepting, and rescinding declarations of compliance; and the rules that apply to the persons who submit those declarations of compliance for Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) acceptance. Paragraph (b) then states the general reach: subject to the exceptions in paragraph (c), the subpart applies to the design and production of all unmanned aircraft operated in United States airspace. Paragraph (c) carves out four categories, but with an important condition attached: the carve-out does not apply if the aircraft is designed and produced to be a standard remote identification unmanned aircraft. For anything other than those, the subpart does not reach the design or production of home-built unmanned aircraft; unmanned aircraft of the United States Government; unmanned aircraft that weigh 0.55 pounds or less on takeoff, counting everything on board or otherwise attached to the aircraft; and unmanned aircraft designed or produced exclusively for aeronautical research or to show compliance with regulations. Remote pilots mostly encounter this section indirectly. It explains why a store-bought drone comes with remote identification capability already built in and why a scratch-built airframe does not.

Illustration: A remote pilot builds her own quadcopter from parts in her garage, intending to fly it for paid real estate photography. She reads this subpart and wonders whether she must meet the design and production requirements as the builder. Paragraph (c)(1) excludes home-built unmanned aircraft from the design and production requirements of this subpart, and she is not designing it to be a standard remote identification unmanned aircraft, so those production requirements do not fall on her. That settles only the question this section answers. Whether her flight itself must broadcast remote identification, and how she would satisfy that, is governed by other sections of Part 89 and Part 107, not by this applicability section, so she still has to go read those before she flies the job.

Common misreading: The biggest trap is reading paragraph (c) as a blanket exemption from remote identification. It is not. This section only limits the reach of this subpart, which is about design and production. Someone seeing 'unmanned aircraft that weigh 0.55 pounds or less' in (c)(3) may conclude that a sub-0.55-pound aircraft is free of remote identification obligations entirely. All this chunk says is that the design and production rules in this subpart do not apply to it. Operating requirements live elsewhere. The second trap is skipping the opening clause of (c): if an aircraft is designed and produced to be a standard remote identification unmanned aircraft, the four carve-outs do not shelter it, so a home-builder who sets out to produce a standard remote identification aircraft is back inside this subpart.
