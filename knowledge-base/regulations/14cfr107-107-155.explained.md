This section is written for manufacturers and others who want the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) to accept a "means of compliance" — the method used to show a small unmanned aircraft meets the requirements for Category 2 or Category 3 operations over people. It is not a rule a remote pilot performs in the field; it governs the paperwork behind the aircraft a pilot flies.

Paragraph (a) limits what a means of compliance can be built from: to meet the requirements of § 107.120(a) for Category 2, or § 107.130(a) for Category 3, the means of compliance must consist of test, analysis, or inspection. See those sections for what those requirements actually are.

Paragraph (b) lists what an applicant must send the FAA, in a manner the Administrator specifies: (1) a detailed description of the means of compliance, including the test, analysis, or inspection procedures that demonstrate the aircraft meets § 107.120(a) for Category 2 or § 107.130(a) for Category 3, and the description should include conditions, environments, and methods as applicable; and (2) an explanation of how applying that means of compliance actually fulfills those requirements.

Paragraph (c): if the FAA decides the applicant has demonstrated compliance with paragraphs (a) and (b), it notifies the applicant that the means of compliance is accepted.

Paragraph (d): acceptance is not permanent. The Administrator reviews a means of compliance on an ongoing basis and may rescind acceptance if it does not meet any or all of the requirements of this subpart. A notice of rescission is published in the Federal Register.

Paragraph (e) states that part 13, subpart D, of this chapter does not apply to the procedures of paragraph (a). This chunk does not say what part 13, subpart D, contains — see that subpart.

Illustration: A company designs a small quadcopter it wants to sell for flights over people. Before it can declare the aircraft eligible for Category 2, it has to show the FAA how it knows the aircraft meets § 107.120(a). The company builds a drop-test protocol: it specifies the test rig, the impact surface, the temperature range, and the measurement method, then documents results. It submits that protocol as its procedures under paragraph (b)(1), plus a written explanation under paragraph (b)(2) tying the test results to the § 107.120(a) requirement. Because the protocol is a test — one of the three things paragraph (a) allows — and the submission covers both required items, the FAA reviews it and notifies the company it has accepted the means of compliance. Two years later, if the FAA's ongoing review finds the protocol no longer meets the subpart's requirements, it can rescind acceptance and publish a notice of rescission in the Federal Register.

Common misreading: People read this as something a remote pilot has to do before flying over people. It is not. The remote pilot's obligations for operations over people live in other sections of this subpart; § 107.155 is the process by which the FAA accepts a manufacturer's or applicant's compliance method. A second misreading is treating an accepted means of compliance as settled forever — paragraph (d) makes it subject to ongoing review and rescission.
