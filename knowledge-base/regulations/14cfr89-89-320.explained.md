This section lists the design and performance standards a remote identification broadcast module must meet. A broadcast module is the add-on device you attach to a drone that does not have remote ID built in. These requirements fall on whoever designs and produces the module, not on the remote pilot, but they tell you what behavior to expect from a compliant module.

The module must be able to determine the take-off location of the unmanned aircraft. Its time mark message element must be synchronized with all the other message elements it sends.

For self-testing and monitoring: before take-off, the module must automatically test its own remote identification functionality and tell the person manipulating the flight controls what the test result was. From take-off to shutdown, it must keep monitoring that functionality continuously and notify the person at the controls of any malfunction or failure.

The module must be designed and produced in a way that reduces a person's ability to tamper with the remote identification functionality, and it must include error correction when broadcasting the message elements listed in § 89.315 (see that section for what those elements are).

On interference, the requirement runs both ways: the module must not interfere with other systems or equipment installed on compatible unmanned aircraft, and those other systems must not interfere with the remote identification equipment.

For message broadcast, the module must be able to broadcast the § 89.315 message elements using a non-proprietary broadcast specification and radio frequency spectrum compatible with personal wireless devices under 47 CFR part 15 (Federal Communications Commission rules), in a band where operations may occur without an FCC individual license. It must also be designed to maximize the range at which the broadcast can be received, while still complying with 47 CFR part 15 and any other laws applicable as of the date the declaration of compliance is submitted to the FAA for acceptance.

The accuracy and timing numbers are specific. Reported geometric position of the unmanned aircraft: accurate within 100 feet of true position, with 95 percent probability. Reported geometric altitude of the aircraft: within 150 feet of true geometric altitude, 95 percent probability. Reported geometric position of the take-off location: within 100 feet, 95 percent probability. Reported geometric altitude of the take-off location: within 150 feet, 95 percent probability. Latitude, longitude, and geometric altitude of the aircraft must be broadcast no later than 1.0 seconds from the time of measurement to the time of broadcast. And the module must broadcast the message elements at a rate of at least 1 message per second.

Note: the line "Subpart E—Means of Compliance" at the end is just the heading for the next subpart, with no rule text in this chunk.

Illustration: You bolt a broadcast module onto an older drone that has no built-in remote ID. You power up on a gravel pull-off and the module's app shows a self-test result before you launch — that notification is the requirement in paragraph (c)(1) doing its job. Twenty minutes into the flight the app pops an alert that the remote ID broadcast has failed. That alert exists because paragraph (c)(2) requires the module to monitor itself continuously from take-off to shutdown and notify the person at the controls. You now know the broadcast is down rather than finding out afterward, so you can land and stop flying instead of continuing without a working broadcast.

Common misreading: Pilots sometimes read the accuracy figures as tolerances they are personally responsible for meeting in flight — as if a position report off by more than 100 feet were a pilot violation. These are performance standards the module must be built to, demonstrated by the producer through the declaration of compliance process. The pilot's practical obligation from this section is to pay attention to the self-test result and the malfunction notifications the module is required to give.
