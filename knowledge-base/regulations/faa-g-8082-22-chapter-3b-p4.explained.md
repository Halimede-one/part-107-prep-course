This is the last stretch of the weather chapter, covering five topics.

Fronts. Air masses pick up the character of the region they form over. When a moving air mass meets another with different properties, the boundary zone between them is called a frontal zone, or front. Across that zone, temperature, humidity, and wind often change rapidly over short distances.

Mountain flying. Before a flight over mountainous terrain, gather as much preflight information as you can on cloud reports, wind direction, wind speed, and stability of the air. Satellites often help locate mountain waves. The guide warns that adequate information may not always be available, so stay alert for "signposts in the sky." It gives specific cues: wind at mountain top level in excess of 25 knots suggests some turbulence; wind in excess of 40 knots across a mountain barrier dictates caution. Stratified (layered) clouds mean stable air. Standing lenticular clouds and rotor clouds suggest a mountain wave — expect turbulence many miles to the lee (downwind side) of the mountains, with relatively smooth flight on the windward side. Convective clouds on the windward side mean unstable air — expect turbulence close to the mountain and on either side of it.

Structural icing. Ice forms on an aircraft in flight only when two conditions are both present: (1) the aircraft is flying through visible water, such as rain or cloud droplets, and (2) the temperature where that moisture strikes the aircraft is 0 degrees Celsius or colder. Aerodynamic cooling can drop the temperature of an airfoil to 0 degrees C even when the surrounding air is a few degrees warmer.

Thunderstorm life cycle. A thunderstorm cell passes through three stages: cumulus, mature, and dissipating. You cannot reliably see the moment one stage becomes the next — the transition is subtle and not abrupt — and a single thunderstorm may be a cluster of cells each in a different stage.

Cumulus stage: most cumulus clouds never become thunderstorms, but every thunderstorm starts as one. The defining feature is an updraft that extends from near the surface to the cloud top. The cloud's growth rate may exceed 3,000 feet per minute, which is why the guide says it is inadvisable to operate a small unmanned aircraft (UA) near rapidly building cumulus clouds. Droplets start small and grow to raindrop size; rising air carries liquid water above the freezing level, creating an icing hazard. When the drops get heavy enough to fall, the cold rain drags air down with it, and the cell has reached the mature stage.

Mature stage: precipitation beginning to fall from the cloud base is the signal that a downdraft has formed. The cold rain in the downdraft holds back compressional heating, so the downdraft stays cooler than the surrounding air, accelerates, and may exceed 2,500 feet per minute. At the surface that air spreads outward, producing strong gusty winds, a sharp temperature drop, and a rapid rise in pressure. That surface wind surge is called a "plow wind" and its leading edge is the "first gust." Updrafts at this point peak and may exceed 6,000 feet per minute. Updrafts and downdrafts side by side create strong vertical shear and a very turbulent environment. All thunderstorm hazards are at their greatest intensity in the mature stage.

Dissipating stage: downdrafts dominate and the storm dies rapidly. The stage is complete when rain has ended and downdrafts have abated. Once every cell has finished, only harmless cloud remnants remain.

Ceiling. For aviation purposes, the ceiling is the lowest cloud layer reported as broken or overcast, or the vertical visibility into an obscuration such as fog or haze. Broken means five-eighths to seven-eighths of the sky is covered with clouds. Overcast means the entire sky is covered. Current ceiling information comes from the aviation routine weather report (METAR) and from automated weather stations of various types.

Visibility. Visibility is the greatest horizontal distance at which prominent objects can be seen with the naked eye. Current visibility is reported in METAR and other aviation weather reports and by automated weather systems. Forecast visibility, as predicted by meteorologists, is available to a pilot during a preflight weather briefing.

Illustration: You plan an afternoon mapping flight on the downwind side of a ridge. Your preflight check shows winds at ridge-top level around 45 knots across the barrier, and the webcam shows a line of standing lenticular clouds parked over the ridge. Both signposts point the same way: wind in excess of 40 knots across a mountain barrier dictates caution, and standing lenticular clouds suggest a mountain wave with turbulence many miles to the lee. You are on the lee side — the worst place to be. You postpone. The next day, winds are 12 knots and the sky shows flat stratified layers, which mean stable air, so you fly. Later that week on a humid afternoon, you are set up for a tower inspection when a cumulus cloud overhead visibly towers upward in minutes and the first drops of rain hit the pavement. Rain falling from the cloud base is the signal the cell has entered the mature stage, when all thunderstorm hazards are at their greatest intensity, with gusty surface winds spreading out ahead of the storm. You land immediately and secure the aircraft rather than waiting for the gust front.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people treat the dissipating stage as a safe window. The guide says the storm dies rapidly, but the stage is not complete until rain has ended and downdrafts have abated — and a thunderstorm may be a cluster of cells in different stages, so one dying cell nearby tells you nothing about the cell behind it. Second, pilots assume ice cannot form when the thermometer reads a few degrees above freezing. The chunk says aerodynamic cooling can lower an airfoil to 0 degrees C even though the ambient temperature is a few degrees warmer, so flying through visible moisture in near-freezing air can still ice your aircraft.
