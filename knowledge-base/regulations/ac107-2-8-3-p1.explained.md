This guidance explains how the FAA reads the ban on flying over people in section 107.39. The default rule: you may not fly a small unmanned aircraft over a person who is not under safe cover, unless your operation fits one of the four categories in part 107 subpart D (see that subpart). Safe cover means a structure or a stationary vehicle that would protect the person if the aircraft hit it. Two groups are not a problem: people under safe cover, and people who are direct participants in the operation. Direct participants include the remote pilot in command (PIC), another person manipulating the controls, a visual observer (VO), or crewmembers needed for the safety of the operation. A direct participant should be directly involved in the flight, and the remote pilot assigns and briefs them before the flight. The guidance lists ways to comply: pick a site with no people and none expected for the whole operation; if people are present, have a plan to keep them clear, which may mean directing them to stay indoors or under safe cover; keep a safe distance from non-participants; and do not fly over moving vehicles. The FAA adds that you should think about the aircraft's course, speed, and trajectory, including the possibility of a failure, when judging whether the aircraft would go over or strike a non-participant.

On distance (8.3.1): part 107 does not set any specific stand-off distance from people. You may choose your own minimum, and in choosing it the guidance says to consider the aircraft's performance (course, speed, trajectory, maneuverability), environmental conditions such as wind including gusts, precipitation, and visibility, operational area conditions such as where people, vessels, or vehicles are and how they move, plus terrain and structures, probable failures and your ability to fly emergency maneuvers including emergency landings, and your own familiarity with the aircraft. You should re-evaluate and adjust that distance as conditions change during the flight.

On open-air assemblies (8.3.2): Category 1, 2, or 4 operations may not be flown in sustained flight over an open-air assembly unless the operation meets section 89.110 or section 89.115(a) (see those sections). That prohibition can be waived. "Sustained flight" does not include a brief, one-time transit over part of the gathering when the transit is merely incidental to a point-to-point flight unrelated to the assembly. Category 3 operations are not allowed over an open-air assembly at all.

On moving vehicles (8.3.3): part 107 permits flight over people inside moving vehicles with an aircraft eligible for a Category 1, 2, 3, or 4 operation, under one of two conditions. For Categories 1, 2, and 3, the flight must be within or over a closed- or restricted-access site, and anyone inside a moving vehicle at that site must be on notice that an aircraft may fly over them. If the flight is not within or over such a site, the aircraft must not maintain sustained flight over any moving vehicle. The note adds that Category 4 aircraft may be eligible to fly over moving vehicles as long as the operating limitations in the FAA-approved Flight Manual, or as otherwise specified by the Administrator, do not prohibit it.

Illustration: You are hired to photograph a suburban house. Your crew is you as remote pilot in command and one visual observer you briefed before launch. Flying over the two of you is fine because you are both direct participants. The homeowner wants to watch from the driveway, and a neighbor is mowing the lawn next door. Neither is a participant, so you ask the homeowner to watch from inside the house, which counts as safe cover, and you plan a flight path that stays off the neighbor's lawn. Part 107 gives you no required stand-off number, so you set your own: the breeze is gusty and the house has tall trees, so you decide to keep at least the length of the yard between the aircraft and the mower, and you plan your turns so that a motor failure would drop the aircraft onto the roof or the empty back yard rather than onto the neighbor. Mid-flight the mower moves closer, so you reposition and shorten the orbit. Reason: nobody outside your briefed crew is under your flight path, and your margins account for a failure.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read "no specific stand-off distance" as permission to fly close to bystanders. The rule is not a distance rule at all, it is an over-people rule, so a tight pass beside a crowd can be legal while a high pass directly over one person is not, and the FAA still expects you to pick and adjust your own margin using the listed factors. Second, people treat "not sustained flight" as a loophole for crossing crowds or traffic. Outside a closed- or restricted-access site, the moving-vehicle allowance only covers flight that does not maintain sustained flight over a vehicle, and over an open-air assembly the exception is limited to a brief, one-time transit that is merely incidental to a point-to-point flight unrelated to the assembly. Repeatedly circling back over the same crowd or the same highway is not incidental. Also note Category 3 gets no open-air-assembly exception at all.
