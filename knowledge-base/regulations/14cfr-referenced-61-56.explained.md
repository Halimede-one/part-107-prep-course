This is a part 61 rule about pilots of aircraft they fly themselves, and part 107 points to it by reference. It sets out what a flight review is and when a pilot needs one.

What a flight review is (paragraph (a)): at minimum 1 hour of flight training plus 1 hour of ground training. It must cover (1) the current general operating and flight rules of part 91, and (2) whatever maneuvers and procedures the person giving the review decides are needed for the pilot to show safe exercise of the privileges of that pilot certificate. Glider pilots may swap the 1 hour of flight training for at least three instructional flights in a glider, each including a flight to traffic pattern altitude.

When it is required (paragraph (c)): no person may act as pilot in command of an aircraft unless, since the beginning of the 24th calendar month before the month they act as pilot in command, they have both completed a flight review given by an authorized instructor in an aircraft for which the pilot is rated, and received a logbook endorsement from that instructor certifying satisfactory completion. Note this is counted in calendar months, not to the day.

Substitutes and exceptions, all measured over that same period:
- (d) A pilot proficiency check or practical test for a certificate, rating, or operating privilege, given by an examiner, an approved pilot check airman, or a U.S. Armed Force, counts instead. So does a practical test given by an examiner for a flight instructor certificate, an added rating on one, flight instructor recent experience under § 61.197(b)(1), or reinstatement of flight instructor privileges under § 61.199(b)(2) — see those sections.
- (e) Satisfactorily finishing one or more phases of an FAA-sponsored pilot proficiency program counts instead.
- (f) A flight instructor certificate holder can skip just the 1 hour of ground training if they met the recent experience requirements under § 61.197 or reinstated instructor privileges by completing an approved flight instructor refresher course under § 61.199(a)(1).
- (g) A student pilot training for a certificate who holds a current solo flight endorsement under § 61.87 does not need the flight review.

Two practical allowances: (h) the authorized instructor may combine this review with the requirements of § 61.57 and other recent experience requirements. (i) A flight simulator or flight training device may be used, but only within an approved course at a part 142 training center, only if it represents an aircraft or set of aircraft the pilot is rated for, and unless the simulator is approved for landings the pilot must still meet the takeoff and landing requirements of § 61.57(a) or § 61.57(b) — see that section.

Illustration: A pilot holds a private pilot certificate and completed a flight review with an authorized certificated flight instructor on March 12, 2024, with a logbook endorsement signed that day. In February 2026 she wants to fly an airplane as pilot in command. Counting back from February 2026, the beginning of the 24th calendar month before it is March 1, 2024. Her March 12, 2024 review falls inside that window, so she is current and may act as pilot in command. If instead she waited until April 2026, the window would start April 1, 2024, her March 2024 review would fall outside it, and she would need a new flight review — or one of the substitutes in paragraphs (d) or (e) — before acting as pilot in command.

Common misreading: Two trip-ups. First, people treat the deadline as a 24-month anniversary date, so a review on March 12, 2024 is read as good until March 12, 2026. The rule counts calendar months: currency runs through the end of the 24th calendar month, so that review covers flights through March 31, 2026. Second, people assume only a formal flight review counts. Paragraphs (d) and (e) let a qualifying proficiency check, practical test, or a phase of an FAA-sponsored pilot proficiency program satisfy the requirement instead.
