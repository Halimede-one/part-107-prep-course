This one sentence sets the scope of the subpart it introduces. It says the subpart applies to the operation of all civil small unmanned aircraft systems (small UAS, meaning the drone plus everything used to control it) that are subject to Part 107. Two words do the work here. "Civil" distinguishes these aircraft from public aircraft operations, such as those flown by a government entity. "Subject to this part" points back to the general applicability rules elsewhere in Part 107 — see those sections for which aircraft and operations fall under Part 107 in the first place. So this section does not itself decide whether Part 107 covers you; it says that once Part 107 does cover you and your operation is civil, every operating rule in this subpart applies to you. There is no carve-out in this sentence for small drones, hobby-sized aircraft, short flights, or operations on private property.

Illustration: A remote pilot is hired to photograph a church steeple. She wonders whether the operating rules in this subpart apply, since the flight is short, low, and over private land with the owner's permission. The answer is yes. Her flight is a civil operation and it falls under Part 107, so the whole subpart applies — nothing in this applicability statement shrinks the rules for small, brief, or private-property flights. She plans the flight to the full set of operating requirements.

Common misreading: People read "civil" as meaning "non-commercial" or "recreational," and conclude that paid work is excluded. It means the opposite kind of distinction: civil separates privately operated aircraft from public (government) aircraft operations, and paid commercial work is squarely civil.
