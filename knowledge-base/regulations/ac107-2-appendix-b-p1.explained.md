This is Appendix B of an advisory circular (AC) — FAA guidance, mostly phrased as what a remote pilot in command (remote PIC) "should" do rather than a rule. It covers four related preflight topics.

Performance data (B.1): the manufacturer may publish operational and performance information — takeoff, climb, range, endurance, descent, and landing. You have to understand what that data means to make practical use of the aircraft's capabilities and limitations. The AC warns that manufacturers' performance information is not standardized, so it won't look the same from brand to brand. If your manufacturer publishes none, the AC advises looking for performance data already determined and published by other users of the same small unmanned aircraft system (small UAS) make and model, and using that as a starting point.

Weight and balance (B.2): before any flight the remote PIC should verify the aircraft is correctly loaded by determining its weight and balance (W&B) condition, and should closely follow the W&B restrictions set by the manufacturer or builder. The remote PIC must consider the consequences of an overweight aircraft, which may create an unsafe condition. Even when a maximum gross takeoff weight is specified, the aircraft may not safely take off at that weight under all conditions: high elevations, high air temperatures, and high humidity (high density altitudes) may require reducing weight first. So may the length, surface, and slope of the runway or launch area, the surface wind, and nearby obstacles. Weight can also change in flight — fuel burn is the most common. Burning fuel makes the aircraft lighter and improves performance but can hurt balance. On unmanned aircraft, in-flight weight change can also come from expending items on board, such as a jettisonable load.

Balance and center of gravity (B.2.2): bad weight distribution can affect flight characteristics much the way excess weight does. The manufacturer may set limits on where the center of gravity (CG) can be. The CG is not a fixed point marked on the airframe — where it sits depends on how the aircraft's weight is distributed, so shifting or expending variable load items can move it. The remote PIC should work out how the CG will shift and what that does to the aircraft. If the CG is outside the allowable limits after loading, or won't stay inside them throughout the flight, weight has to be moved or removed before flight.

Weather sources (B.3): remote PICs are encouraged to get preflight weather from Flight Service at https://www.1800wxbrief.com, where a free account lets you use the briefing service. Flight Service also has a telephone-based service, but that one is intended for manned aircraft pilots only. The AC also encourages using the National Weather Service (NWS) Aviation Weather Center (AWC) at https://www.aviationweather.gov — free, no registration, and carrying the products that matter to a remote PIC, such as Aviation Routine Weather Reports (METAR) and Terminal Aerodrome Forecasts (TAF). Alongside the weather, the AC calls it critical to check for temporary flight restrictions (TFRs) at the FAA's TFR website, https://tfr.faa.gov.

Weather and performance (B.4): weather influences both aircraft performance and flying safety. The chunk names atmospheric pressure and density, wind, and uneven surface heating as factors affecting small unmanned aircraft performance that must be considered before flight.

Illustration: A remote pilot plans an afternoon mapping flight from a mountain meadow on a hot, humid August day, flying a multirotor at its rated maximum gross takeoff weight with a heavy camera and a spare battery strapped on. The manufacturer publishes almost no performance numbers, so the pilot searches an owners' forum and finds climb and endurance figures other operators of the same model have published, and uses those as a starting point. Reading them against the high elevation, high temperature, and high humidity — a high density altitude — the pilot decides the published maximum weight is not a safe takeoff weight today, leaves the spare battery on the ground, and reduces the load. The pilot also notices the launch spot slopes and sits near a stand of tall pines, which reinforces the decision. Before launching, the pilot pulls a briefing from a free 1800wxbrief.com account, cross-checks the METAR and TAF on aviationweather.gov, and checks tfr.faa.gov for any temporary flight restriction over the area.

Common misreading: The most common misreading is treating the manufacturer's maximum gross takeoff weight as an all-conditions guarantee — if it's under the limit, it's fine to fly. The chunk says the opposite: the aircraft may not always safely take off at that load under all conditions, and high density altitude, launch area length, surface, slope, surface wind, and obstacles may each require reducing weight before flight. A second one: people assume the Flight Service phone briefing is available to them. The chunk says the telephone-based service is intended for manned aircraft pilots only; remote pilots are pointed to the website. A third: assuming the CG is a marked spot on the airframe. It isn't — it moves as load is shifted or expended, so it has to be reasoned about for the whole flight, not just at loading.
