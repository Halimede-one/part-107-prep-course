This section covers how an individual names a U.S. agent for service — a person in the United States who can receive official FAA (Federal Aviation Administration) communications and legal notices on that individual's behalf. The designation goes to the FAA in writing, in the form and manner the Administrator prescribes. The person designating the agent has to make sure the agent actually understands what receiving FAA service on their behalf involves and is capable of doing it. The written designation must contain four items: the agent's full name, the agent's U.S. address, an email address, and the individual's certification that the agent has accepted that responsibility. A fax number and phone number may be included but are not required. If anything about the designation or the agent's contact information changes, the individual must tell the FAA within 30 days of the change. Two consequences are spelled out. First, no individual may exercise the privileges of any certificate, rating, or authorization issued under part 47, 61, 63, 65, 67, or 107 unless they have designated a U.S. agent as required under this subpart — and aircraft registration certificates issued to individuals who fail to do so are ineffective. Second, no individual will be issued a certificate, rating, or authorization under those same parts without that designation. Part 107 is the remote pilot rule, so a remote pilot certificate sits squarely inside both the no-flying and the no-issuance consequences. Note that the chunk does not state which individuals are required to make this designation; that condition lives elsewhere in this subpart — see that section. The trailing line about Subpart D is only a heading and source note, with no rule text in it.

Illustration: A remote pilot who holds a Part 107 certificate and falls under this subpart's designation requirement has a U.S. agent on file — a cousin in Ohio. The cousin moves and changes email addresses. The pilot has 30 days from the change to notify the FAA of the new address and email, in the form and manner the Administrator prescribes. The pilot has a commercial roof-inspection flight booked five weeks out and assumes the paperwork can wait until after the job. It cannot wait: the obligation is tied to the 30 days after the change, not to the flight schedule. And because paragraph (d) bars exercising the privileges of a Part 107 certificate unless the individual has designated a U.S. agent as required under this subpart, letting the designation fall out of compliance puts the flight itself at risk. The pilot files the update inside the 30-day window and then flies.

Common misreading: Two trip people up. First, assuming the 30-day notice applies only when you swap to a different agent — the chunk says any change to the designation or to the agent's contact information, so a new email or address for the same agent also starts the 30-day clock. Second, treating the agent as a convenience or a mailing drop. The individual must certify that the agent has accepted the responsibility and must ensure the agent understands and is competent to receive FAA service; naming a relative who has no idea they were named does not satisfy this section.
