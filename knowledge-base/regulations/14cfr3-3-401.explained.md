This section does two things: it sets the scope of the falsification subpart (subpart D of 14 CFR Part 3) and defines one term used in it.

On scope, paragraph (a) says the subpart applies to any person who is subject to the requirements in a listed set of subchapters of this chapter, each with its own carve-outs: subchapter A (except parts 1 and 3), subchapter C (except part 39), subchapter D, subchapter E (except parts 71 and 73), subchapter F (except parts 95 and 97), subchapter G (except part 110), subchapter H, and subchapter K (except parts 185, 187, 189, and 193). The chunk gives only this list; to know whether a particular rule you operate under sits inside one of those subchapters, see the subchapter listings themselves. Note that the trigger is being subject to requirements in those subchapters — not holding a particular certificate.

On definitions, paragraph (b) is broader than it first looks. For purposes of this part, “document in any format” covers documents that are electronic or physical, and it also covers other tangible items, such as data plates or marked parts. So a physical object that carries information counts as a “document” here.

Illustration: A remote pilot replaces a damaged component on an aircraft and finds the small identification plate on the new component is unreadable. Rather than leave it, the pilot stamps a different number onto the plate so the records will match. Even though no paperwork was touched, paragraph (b) means that plate counts as a “document in any format” under this part — data plates and marked parts are named in the definition. The pilot should instead correct the records through the proper route rather than re-marking the part, because altering the plate is treated the same as altering a document.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read “document” as meaning only forms, logbooks, and files, and assume that scratching a number onto a part or swapping a data plate falls outside the rule — paragraph (b) says the opposite. Second, the long list of parenthetical exceptions gets read as a general escape hatch; each “except” removes only the specific parts named inside its own parentheses, not the whole subchapter.
