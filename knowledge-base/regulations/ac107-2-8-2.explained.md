This chunk picks up mid-sentence (the first words continue a sentence about operations over people), and it describes the four categories Part 107 sets up for flying a small unmanned aircraft over people.

Category 1 is the weight-based path. The aircraft must weigh no more than 0.55 pounds, and that weight includes everything on board or otherwise attached to the aircraft at takeoff and for the whole operation — so payloads, props, guards, and any add-on count. On top of the weight limit, the aircraft must have no exposed rotating parts that would lacerate human skin on impact with a person.

Category 2 and Category 3 are not weight-based. Those operations may only be flown with small unmanned aircraft that meet performance-based safety requirements, which the chunk describes as limiting the risk and severity of injury based on potential hazards. The chunk does not spell out those requirements here.

Category 4 is the certificated-aircraft path. A small unmanned aircraft that has been issued an airworthiness certificate under 14 CFR part 21 may operate over people under part 107, as long as the operating limitations in the FAA-approved Flight Manual — or limitations otherwise specified by the Administrator — do not prohibit operations over people.

Illustration: You own a 0.49-pound camera drone and want to fly it above a crowd at a street festival. Bare weight is under the limit, but you clip on a 0.08-pound spare battery pack and a small light. Takeoff weight is now 0.57 pounds. That is over the 0.55-pound Category 1 limit, so Category 1 is off the table for this flight. You also check the props: they are unguarded and exposed, which raises the laceration question the chunk flags. Decision: strip the add-ons to get takeoff weight back under 0.55 pounds and confirm the aircraft has no exposed rotating parts that would lacerate skin — or don't fly over the crowd at all.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read the 0.55 pounds as the drone's listed or empty weight. The chunk says it includes everything on board or attached at takeoff and throughout the operation, so accessories push you over. Second, people assume a heavier drone can qualify for Category 2 or 3 just by being heavier. Those categories have nothing to do with weight — the aircraft must fulfill performance-based safety requirements, and this chunk does not list what those are.
