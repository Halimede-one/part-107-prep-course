This is part of a table in Appendix C of AC 107-2A listing conditions a remote pilot might notice on a small unmanned aircraft system (small UAS) and the action the FAA suggests taking. Appendix C is titled "best practices," so these are recommended steps rather than rule text. Five conditions appear in this portion. Items 4 through 6 cover signs of fire risk: a strong fuel smell, the smell of electrical burning or arcing, and visual signs of electrical burning or arcing such as black soot tracings or sparking. For all three, the suggested action is the same: inspect further to find the source, recognizing that a fuel leak may be escaping the aircraft or collecting inside a sealed compartment, and that either a fuel or electrical problem can bring a risk of fire or extreme heat that hurts the aircraft's structure, its performance characteristics, and how long it can fly. Then judge whether repairs are needed and how extensive they must be before flying safely again. Item 7 covers a noticeable change in sound level, measured in decibels, from the propulsion system during operation. The suggested action is to inspect the whole aircraft with extra attention to propulsion components, meaning motors and propellers, looking for damage or reduced performance, then assess repairs. Item 8 covers control inputs that are not synchronized or are delayed. This one is worded more strongly: discontinue flight and avoid further flight until the control link between the ground control unit and the aircraft has been inspected and tested. The reason given is that unreliable control communications can lead to loss of control, which risks a collision or a flyaway. Confirm the link is accurate and reliable before flying again, then assess repairs.

Illustration: You are flying a mapping grid and partway through the second pass you notice the aircraft's motors have taken on a distinctly louder, rougher pitch than at launch. You finish the pass, land, and set the aircraft aside rather than swapping batteries and continuing. Following item 7, you inspect the whole aircraft with emphasis on the motors and propellers, and you find a hairline crack near the hub of one propeller and grit in one motor bell. You replace the propeller, clean the motor, and only then decide whether the aircraft is fit to continue. The reason is that the sound change was the symptom; the propulsion damage behind it could have grown into a failure in flight.

Common misreading: People read these entries as a checklist that permits finishing the job first. Only item 8, control inputs not synchronized or delayed, actually says to discontinue flight and avoid further operations until the control link is inspected and tested. The fuel-smell, electrical-smell, electrical-burning, and sound-change entries say to inspect further and assess repairs, which is not the same as clearance to keep flying while you think about it. Also note this is Appendix C best-practice guidance in an advisory circular, not a regulation, so these specific actions are recommendations, not citable requirements.
