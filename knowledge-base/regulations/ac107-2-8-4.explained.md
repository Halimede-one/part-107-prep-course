This chunk explains who counts as an "applicant" under part 107 subpart D, the rules for flying small unmanned aircraft over people. An applicant is any person who produces, designs, or modifies a small unmanned aircraft that is eligible to fly over people in the United States. That covers a manufacturer selling many aircraft, a company selling build-it-yourself kits, and a person who modifies an aircraft in a way that changes which category of over-people operations it qualifies for.

The chunk breaks this into cases. (1) Kit sellers: if a kit contains every component and part needed to build an operable small unmanned aircraft, so the buyer needs nothing else, and the aircraft is meant for operations over people, the seller must meet part 107 subpart D. Before selling, the seller must confirm that the fully assembled aircraft — not just the separate parts — meets the performance-based safety requirements, using an FAA-accepted Means of Compliance (MOC), and then declare compliance. (2) Self-builders: someone who buys parts separately and builds an aircraft is an applicant, and must submit a Declaration of Compliance (DOC) to conduct Category 2 or Category 3 operations. (3) Modifiers: changing an aircraft that is already listed on a DOC can put it out of compliance with that declaration, meaning it is no longer in the configuration that was declared. If the change makes it ineligible for operations over people, a new DOC must be submitted before flying over people or moving vehicles in that category. Applicants should list allowable modifications in the remote pilot operating instructions (see paragraph 8.12 for more on those instructions) so pilots know what they may change. If an applicant later develops software or hardware updates, the manufacturer should make operating instructions for the new capabilities available. (4) Anyone who makes a modification the remote pilot operating instructions do not permit to a Category 2 or Category 3 eligible aircraft makes that aircraft ineligible. If that person still intends to fly Category 2 or Category 3 over people, they take on the applicant's responsibilities: show the modified aircraft meets the performance-based safety requirements for Category 2 or Category 3, or both, using an FAA-accepted MOC, and submit a new DOC. The same applies to someone who modifies an already ineligible aircraft intending to fly Category 2 or 3 over people. (5) Maintenance that does not change the aircraft's configuration or characteristics does not require a new DOC. Replacing propellers is fine without a new DOC if that replacement is listed as an allowed modification in the remote pilot operating instructions; replacing propellers with ones not covered by those instructions requires a new DOC for Category 2 or Category 3 operations over people. Illustration: A remote pilot flies a Category 2 eligible aircraft over crowds at outdoor events. One propeller cracks. She checks the remote pilot operating instructions, which list the manufacturer's part-number propellers as an allowed replacement, so she installs a new set of those and keeps flying over people with no new paperwork — the configuration still matches the declaration. Two months later she finds cheaper third-party propellers that are not mentioned anywhere in the operating instructions. If she installs those and still wants to fly over people, she has made a modification the instructions do not permit, so the aircraft is no longer eligible. To keep doing Category 2 work she would have to act as the applicant herself: show the modified aircraft meets the Category 2 performance-based safety requirements using an FAA-accepted Means of Compliance and submit a new Declaration of Compliance. She decides it is not worth it and orders the manufacturer's propellers instead.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people assume that only big manufacturers can be applicants. The chunk says plainly that a hobbyist who buys parts separately and builds an aircraft, or a pilot who makes an unapproved modification, becomes the applicant with all those responsibilities. Second, people assume routine maintenance always triggers a new Declaration of Compliance. It does not — maintenance that leaves the configuration and characteristics unchanged is fine. The dividing line is whether the operating instructions list the change as allowed, not whether you call it maintenance or a modification.
