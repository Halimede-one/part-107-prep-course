This is the self-grounding rule. There is no medical exam or medical certificate for small unmanned aircraft system (sUAS) operations, so the judgment falls on you. If you know, or have reason to know, that you have a physical or mental condition that would interfere with safely operating the sUAS, you may not take part in the flight. The ban covers four roles: manipulating the flight controls, acting as remote pilot in command (the person responsible for the operation), acting as visual observer (the person who watches the aircraft to help keep it in sight), and acting as any other direct participant in the operation. The standard is not 'diagnosed by a doctor.' It is what you know or have reason to know, which includes conditions a reasonable person in your position would recognize, such as effects you can feel coming on.

Illustration: You are the remote pilot in command for a roof inspection and you woke up with a migraine that is blurring the edges of your vision. Nobody has grounded you and there is no certificate to surrender. Under this section you still cannot fly, cannot hand the controls to yourself later in the day while the symptoms persist, and cannot step over to be the visual observer instead, because blurred vision would interfere with safe operation in that role too. The right call is to postpone or let a healthy crew member fly while you stay out of the direct participant roles.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read this as applying only to the person holding the controller. It names the remote pilot in command, the visual observer, and any direct participant as well, so you cannot sidestep it by switching jobs on the crew. Second, people treat 'knows' as requiring a formal diagnosis. The chunk also says 'has reason to know,' so symptoms you can plainly feel count even with no doctor involved.
