This section sets rules for the people who build drones, not the people who fly them. It applies to unmanned aircraft that do not have a design approval or production approval issued under part 21 of this chapter. After September 16, 2022, no person may produce an unmanned aircraft for operation in the airspace of the United States unless two things are true. First, the aircraft must be designed and produced to meet the minimum performance requirements for standard remote identification unmanned aircraft in § 89.310, and that must be done in accordance with a means of compliance the FAA has accepted. Second, all three of the conditions in paragraph (b) must be met: (1) the person responsible for production must, on request, let the Administrator inspect their facilities, technical data, and any standard remote identification unmanned aircraft they produce, and witness any tests needed to check compliance with this subpart; (2) that person must cause independent audits to be performed on a recurring basis, and also any time the FAA gives notice of noncompliance or potential noncompliance, to show the aircraft listed under a declaration of compliance meet this subpart, and must give the FAA the results of all those audits on request; and (3) that person must keep product support and notification procedures in place so the public and the FAA are told about any defect or condition that makes an aircraft stop meeting this subpart, within 15 calendar days of the date the person becomes aware of it. There is an exception for what is provided in § 89.510 — see that section.

Illustration: A remote pilot runs a small shop that assembles and sells quadcopters to local real estate photographers. The airframes have no part 21 design or production approval. Because she is producing aircraft for operation in United States airspace, she must build them to the standard remote identification performance requirements of § 89.310 using an FAA-accepted means of compliance, and she must also set up the three supporting obligations: be ready to open her shop, her technical data, and her finished aircraft to an FAA inspection on request; arrange recurring independent audits of the models listed under her declaration of compliance; and keep a notification procedure so that if she learns the Remote ID broadcast module in a batch is dropping its serial number, she tells the public and the FAA within 15 calendar days of becoming aware of it. If she only wants to fly her own builds and not produce for the airspace generally, she should check § 89.510 for the exception rather than assume this section applies or does not.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, pilots read the 15-calendar-day notification duty as something they owe when their own drone misbehaves. It is not a pilot reporting rule — it falls on the person responsible for production. Second, people read the audit requirement as the FAA auditing the manufacturer. The chunk puts the burden the other way: the producer must cause independent audits to be performed and hand the results to the FAA on request.
