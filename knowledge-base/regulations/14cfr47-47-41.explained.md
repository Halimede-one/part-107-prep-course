This section says how long a Certificate of Aircraft Registration lasts and what you owe the FAA (Federal Aviation Administration) when something changes.

Paragraph (a): a certificate the FAA issues under this subpart stays effective unless the registration has already ended because it was revoked, canceled, expired, or the aircraft's ownership was transferred. Apart from those, the certificate runs until one of seven listed events happens: (1) the aircraft is registered under the laws of a foreign country, subject to the Convention on the International Recognition of Rights in Aircraft where that applies; (2) the aircraft is totally destroyed or scrapped; (3) the certificate holder loses U.S. citizenship; (4) 30 days have passed since the certificate holder's death; (5) an individual owner who is not a U.S. citizen loses resident alien status, unless that person becomes a U.S. citizen at the same moment; (6) for an owner that is a corporation which is not a U.S.-citizen corporation, either the corporation stops being lawfully organized and doing business under U.S. or state law, or a period described in § 47.9(b) ends and the aircraft was not based and primarily used in the United States during that period (see that section for what the period is); and (7) for an aircraft registered in a trustee's name, the trustee loses U.S. citizenship, loses resident alien status without becoming a citizen at the same time, or stops acting as trustee in any way and is not immediately replaced by someone meeting § 47.7(c) (see that section for those requirements).

Paragraph (b): the FAA must be told about any of these status changes and the reason for it. If the certificate was issued electronically, notice goes by email or another means the Administrator accepts. If the certificate is on paper, you complete the reverse side and return the certificate to the Registry. The deadlines are: 21 days for foreign registration, submitted by the person who owned the aircraft before the foreign registration; 60 days after the certificate holder's death, submitted by the administrator or executor of the estate, or by the heir-at-law if no administrator or executor has been or will be appointed; and 21 days after termination of registration in all the other cases in paragraph (a). Expired certificates are the exception: the holder destroys an expired paper or printed certificate instead of returning it. If the certificate is not available to return, paragraph (b)(4) requires a statement to the Registry describing the aircraft and saying why the certificate is not available, within the same time limits.

Illustration: A remote pilot registers a drone under this subpart and later sells it to a buyer in Canada, who registers it under Canadian law. The pilot's registration is at an end, and because she was the owner before the foreign registration, she is the one who must notify the FAA. Her certificate was issued electronically, so she emails the Registry within 21 days, stating the reason for the change: the aircraft is now registered under the laws of a foreign country. If she had held a paper certificate, she would instead complete the reverse side and return it to the Registry in that same 21 days. Separately, if she had crashed the aircraft and it was totally destroyed, that too ends the registration, and the 21-day 'all other cases' deadline in paragraph (b)(3) would apply.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people treat this as a rule about an expiration date; it is not. Expiration is one of the ways registration can already have ended before these events. The seven events in paragraph (a) are separate endings that cut a certificate short. Second, people assume the FAA figures it out on its own when an aircraft is destroyed or sold abroad. Paragraph (b) puts the burden on a named person to give notice, with the reason, inside a stated deadline, and 'I can't find the certificate' is not an excuse: paragraph (b)(4) still requires a written statement to the Registry within the same time.
