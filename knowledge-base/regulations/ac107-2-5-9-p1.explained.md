This part of the advisory circular explains what visual line of sight (VLOS) means in practice. The remote pilot in command (remote PIC) and the person manipulating the controls must be able to see the small unmanned aircraft at all times during flight, under section 107.31. That means flying close enough that you can actually keep it in view. If a visual observer (VO) is used, the same visibility requirement applies to that person too.

The FAA accepts brief moments when you are not looking directly at the aircraft — glancing at the controller to check battery life or build operational awareness — as long as you retain the capability to see the aircraft or quickly maneuver it back into view. Scanning the airspace or briefly looking down at the control station (CS) is not prohibited. If VLOS is lost, you must regain it as soon as practicable. The FAA deliberately sets no specific time interval for how long an interruption may last, because a fixed allowance could permit a hazardous interruption. Throughout, the see-and-avoid responsibilities of sections 107.31 and 107.37 still apply. If VLOS cannot be regained, follow pre-determined loss-of-VLOS procedures — the aircraft's capabilities govern the choice, which might be landing immediately, entering hover mode, or using a return-to-home sequence.

VLOS must be maintained by unaided vision. The only correction allowed is eyeglasses (spectacles) or contact lenses. Binoculars and similar vision aids may be used only momentarily to enhance situational awareness — for example, to avoid inadvertently flying over people or conflicting with other aircraft. First person view (FPV) devices may be used during operations, but they do not satisfy the VLOS requirement.

Using a VO is optional. A VO supplements situational awareness and VLOS and frees the remote PIC and person manipulating the controls to handle other mission-critical duties such as checking displays — though those two people must still keep the capability to see the aircraft themselves. A VO must be able to communicate effectively about the aircraft's location, attitude, altitude, and direction of flight; the position of other aircraft or hazards in the airspace; and the determination that the aircraft does not endanger the life or property of another, per section 107.33.

The remote PIC must make sure the VO is positioned where the VO can see the aircraft well enough to maintain VLOS, and can do this by specifying the VO's location. Under section 107.33 the remote PIC and VO must coordinate to (1) scan the airspace where the aircraft is operating for any potential collision hazard, and (2) maintain awareness of the aircraft's position through direct visual observation. In practice the VO tells the remote PIC and person manipulating the controls the flight status and any collision hazards entering the area so the appropriate action can be taken. The remote PIC, person manipulating the controls, and VO must work out a method of effective communication that does not create a distraction — meaning a constant ability to understand one another. That method must be determined before the operation. Communication-assisting devices such as a handheld radio are permitted to make communication from a distance work.

Illustration: You are flying a roof inspection with one visual observer. Before launch you agree that the VO will stand at the northwest corner of the lot and you will use handheld radios on a set channel, with the VO calling out aircraft position and any manned traffic. Mid-flight you look down at your controller for about two seconds to check remaining battery — that is acceptable, because you still have the capability to see the aircraft and bring it back into view. A moment later the aircraft drifts behind a rooftop HVAC unit and you cannot see it at all. Your VO also cannot see it from the northwest corner. You do not wait out some allowance of time, because none exists; you immediately work to regain visual line of sight, and when you cannot, you follow the loss-of-VLOS procedure you briefed — in this case commanding a hover and then stepping sideways to re-acquire the aircraft, because this airframe holds position reliably and hovering keeps it clear of the structure.

Common misreading: Two traps here. First, people hear "brief moments" and assume there is a permitted number of seconds — three, five, ten. The chunk says explicitly that no specific time interval exists, because setting one could allow a hazardous interruption. Second, pilots assume a visual observer or an FPV feed substitutes for their own view. It does not: the remote PIC and person manipulating the controls must themselves retain the capability to see the aircraft, a VO only supplements that, and FPV devices may be used but do not satisfy the VLOS requirement. Binoculars are also not a workaround — they are for momentary situational awareness, not for extending how far out you may fly.
