This section just defines three terms used throughout Part 47, the aircraft registration rules. It sets no requirement by itself; it tells you what the words mean when other sections use them.

"Citizen of the United States" (or "U.S. citizen") covers three kinds of applicants. First, an individual who is a citizen of the United States or one of its possessions. Second, a partnership in which every single partner is an individual U.S. citizen. Third, a corporation or association organized under the laws of the United States, a State, the District of Columbia, or a U.S. territory or possession — but only if all of these hold true: the president and at least two-thirds of the board of directors and other managing officers are U.S. citizens, the entity is under the actual control of U.S. citizens, and at least 75 percent of the voting interest is owned or controlled by persons who are U.S. citizens.

"Registry" is shorthand for one specific office: the FAA, Civil Aviation Registry, Aircraft Registration Branch. When other sections of Part 47 say to send something to the Registry, that is the office they mean.

"Resident alien" means an individual who is a citizen of a foreign country and has been lawfully admitted to the United States for permanent residence as an immigrant, in conformity with Department of Homeland Security regulations (8 CFR Chapter 1).

Illustration: A drone services company is set up as a limited liability company in Colorado and wants to register an aircraft. The president and four of the six board members are U.S. citizens (that clears the two-thirds mark), and U.S. citizens hold 80 percent of the voting interest (above the 75 percent floor). But a foreign parent company holds contractual veto power over every operating decision, so the entity is not under the actual control of U.S. citizens. All three conditions in paragraph (3) have to be satisfied, not just two of them, so the company does not meet this definition of U.S. citizen. The owner would need to look at the eligibility provisions elsewhere in Part 47 — see those sections — to find the right path.

Common misreading: People treat the 75 percent voting-interest test as the whole test for a corporation. It is one of three separate conditions in paragraph (3), alongside the officer/director citizenship requirement and actual control by U.S. citizens. A second trap: the partnership definition in paragraph (2) requires every partner to be an individual U.S. citizen, so a partnership with a corporate partner does not qualify under that paragraph even if that corporation is U.S.-owned. Finally, "resident alien" is defined here as a separate term — it is not a subcategory of "U.S. citizen."
