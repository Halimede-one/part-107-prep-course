This passage explains what counts as night and what equipment and training you need to fly in the dark or near it. Night is defined in § 1.1 as the time between the end of evening civil twilight and the beginning of morning civil twilight, as published in The Air Almanac and converted to local time. In the continental United States, evening civil twilight runs from sunset until 30 minutes after sunset, and morning civil twilight runs from 30 minutes before sunrise until sunrise. Alaska uses a different definition, described in The Air Almanac. The Air Almanac has tables for sunrise and sunset at various latitudes, and those tables can be downloaded from the Naval Observatory and customized for a location.

For civil twilight operations, the small unmanned aircraft must have anti-collision lighting visible for at least 3 statute miles (sm). The remote pilot in command (remote PIC) may reduce the visible distance of that lighting to less than 3 sm during flight if he or she determines that doing so is in the interest of safety.

For operations at night, § 107.29 sets out two risk mitigation measures. First, the remote PIC must have completed either an initial knowledge test or recurrent training updated to include night operations — specifically covering night physiology and night visual illusions. Chapter 6 covers both paths; see that chapter. Second, the small unmanned aircraft must have lighted anti-collision lighting visible for at least 3 sm. You may rely on the manufacturer's statement that the lighting is visible for 3 sm, but you remain responsible for verifying that it actually works, is visible for 3 sm, and flashes fast enough to avoid a collision at your operating site.

At night, as in civil twilight, the remote PIC may reduce the intensity of the light when safety calls for it. The example given: a bright light or strobe very close to the remote pilot can wash out the pilot's ability to judge the aircraft's location, speed, attitude, or altitude accurately. That discretion ties back to § 107.19, which makes the remote PIC directly responsible for the operation and for complying with all of part 107 — including keeping the ability to visually observe the aircraft.

Section 107.29 does not require illuminated anti-collision lighting for daytime operations, because lighting generally does not do much to reduce collision risk in daylight. Remote pilots may still choose to run lights during all daytime flights.

Finally, a remote PIC or operator may request a waiver of the anti-collision lighting requirement for night and civil twilight operations. The request has two parts: fully describe the proposed operation, and establish that it can be conducted safely under the terms of a Certificate of Waiver (CoW). Paragraph 5.20 describes the application process; see that paragraph.

Illustration: You are hired to photograph a parking lot in Ohio with sunset at 7:12 p.m. You plan to start at 7:20 p.m. and finish by 8:00 p.m. Sunset to 30 minutes after sunset — 7:12 to 7:42 p.m. — is evening civil twilight. After 7:42 p.m. it is night. So the first part of your flight is civil twilight and the rest is night. Either way, the aircraft needs anti-collision lighting visible for at least 3 statute miles, and because part of the flight is at night, you need to have completed the knowledge test or recurrent training that covers night physiology and night visual illusions. Your strobe's spec sheet claims 3 sm visibility, which you may rely on, but you still walk out and confirm the strobe is actually lit and flashing before launch. Partway through, flying the aircraft close overhead, the strobe is blinding you and you can no longer judge its altitude. You dim the light, because § 107.19 makes you responsible for the operation and you must keep the ability to see the aircraft. That reduction is permitted when you determine it is in the interest of safety.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people treat the anti-collision lighting dimming allowance as a blanket excuse to fly with weak lights. The chunk says the aircraft must be equipped with lighting visible for at least 3 sm; the reduction is an in-flight judgment call made in the interest of safety, not a lower equipment standard. Second, people assume a remote pilot certificate by itself authorizes night flight. It does not — night privileges come only after a knowledge test containing questions on night physiology and night visual illusions, or after completing the recurrent training that covers those topics. Also note that civil twilight operations require the lighting but the chunk does not tie them to the night training requirement; the training requirement is stated for operations at night under § 107.29.
