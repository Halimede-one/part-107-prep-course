This part of the advisory circular lists the four flight limitations every small unmanned aircraft operation must stay inside, and then gives practical ways to show you are meeting each one. The limits: groundspeed cannot exceed 87 knots (100 miles per hour (mph)); altitude cannot exceed 400 feet above ground level (AGL), unless you are flying within a 400-foot radius of a structure, in which case you may not go higher than 400 feet above that structure's immediate uppermost limit; visibility, as seen from the location of the control station (CS), may not be less than 3 statute miles (sm); and the aircraft must stay at least 500 feet below a cloud and at least 2,000 feet horizontally from a cloud. The chunk cites § 107.51 for these limits. The FAA notes the purpose: these limits support the remote pilot's ability to spot hazards such as encroaching aircraft or people on the ground and react in time.

For groundspeed, the circular offers three example methods: a Global Positioning System (GPS) device on the aircraft that reports groundspeed, letting the pilot work out wind direction and speed and compute airspeed for a given heading; timing the aircraft between two or more fixed points while accounting for wind, then noting the power settings that keep it at or below 87 knots groundspeed; or relying on the manufacturer's design limitations, such as an installed groundspeed limiter.

For altitude, the examples are: a calibrated altitude reporting device that gives altitude above mean sea level (MSL), from which the pilot subtracts the MSL elevation of the control station to get AGL height above the terrain or structure; a GPS device able to report MSL altitude; having the remote pilot and visual observer (VO) pace off 400 feet from the aircraft while it sits on the ground to build a visual sense of that distance and then hold that perspective or closer in flight; or using the known height of local rising terrain or structures as a reference.

For weather, the remote pilot in command (PIC) must determine that visibility from the control station is at least 3 sm and that the aircraft keeps 500 feet below clouds and 2,000 feet horizontally from clouds. Local aviation weather reports with current and forecast conditions are one way to do this. If more than one reporting station is near the operating area, pick the closest one that best represents the terrain around where you are flying. If no local aviation weather reports are available, the remote PIC cannot fly until the required visibility and cloud clearances can be determined by other reliable means. Two absolute prohibitions close the paragraph: the aircraft cannot be operated above any cloud, and nothing may obstruct visibility between the aircraft and the remote PIC — the circular names smoke or a cloud as examples. The chunk cites § 107.39 at the end of this weather paragraph.

Illustration: You are hired to inspect the upper antenna array on a 600-foot broadcast tower. Standing with your control station at the base, you first check the weather: the nearest aviation reporting station, about four miles away and on similar terrain, reports 7 sm visibility and a broken ceiling at 2,500 feet. Visibility and cloud clearance are satisfied. Because you will stay within a 400-foot radius of the tower, you may climb above 400 feet AGL — but no higher than 400 feet above the tower's immediate uppermost limit, which is roughly 1,000 feet AGL in this case. To track that, your aircraft's GPS reports MSL altitude, and you subtract the control station's MSL elevation to read AGL. You also use the tower's known 600-foot height as a cross-check. You keep the orbit tight around the tower and stay well under the reported ceiling so you are never within 500 feet below the clouds and never above any cloud. The inspection proceeds.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, the structure exception is not a blanket permission to fly to 400 feet above anything tall nearby — you must be within a 400-foot radius of that structure, and your ceiling is 400 feet above that structure's immediate uppermost limit, not 400 feet above its base or 400 feet plus some general allowance. Second, the methods listed for groundspeed, altitude, visibility, and cloud clearance are examples of ways to comply, not a required checklist. The requirement is the limit itself; the GPS device, the pacing-off exercise, and the weather report are suggested means. But that cuts both ways on weather: if you have no local aviation weather reports and no other reliable means of determining visibility and cloud clearance, the circular says you cannot fly — guessing by eye is not offered as a substitute.
