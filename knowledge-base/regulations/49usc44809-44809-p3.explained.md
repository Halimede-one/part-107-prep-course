This chunk is mostly editorial material, and the first page or so is a fragment. It opens mid-sentence ("lows:") in the middle of the amendment notes that track how Congress rewrote 49 U.S.C. 44809 through Public Law 118-63 in 2024. Those notes list which subsections were added, struck, or renumbered; they are bookkeeping, not rules you fly by. One quoted phrase inside them is worth noticing because it is the replaced text of a condition: "In Class G airspace, the aircraft is flown from the surface to not more than 400 feet above ground level and complies with all airspace restrictions and prohibitions." The operative version of the conditions lives in the body of 44809 itself, so see that section.

The part with real teachable content is the statutory note titled "Use of Unmanned Aircraft Systems for Educational Purposes," from Public Law 115-254, division B, title III, section 350, as later amended. It answers a practical question: does flying a drone for school count as a "recreational purpose" under 44809? The note says yes, in four listed cases. A "recreational purpose" under subsection (a)(1) of 44809 includes an unmanned aircraft system: operated by an institution of higher education for educational or research purposes; operated by an elementary school, a secondary school, or an institution of higher education for educational or research purposes; flown as part of an established Junior Reserve Officers' Training Corps (JROTC) program for education or research purposes; or flown as part of an educational program chartered by a recognized community-based organization as defined in subsection (h) of 44809.

The note then defines its terms. "Education or research purposes" for a school or college includes instruction of students at the institution, academic or research related uses approved by the institution including Federal research, activities undertaken by the institution as part of research projects including federally sponsored ones, and other academic activities approved by the institution. "Institution of higher education" takes its meaning from section 101(a) of the Higher Education Act of 1965 (20 U.S.C. 1001(a)). "Elementary school" and "secondary school" take their meanings from section 8101 of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965 (20 U.S.C. 7801(19) and 7801(45)).

Two more pieces. Subsection (b) tells the Administrator what to weigh when updating an operational parameter under subsection (d)(1) of 44809 for higher-education flights: use of small unmanned aircraft systems and operations at an accredited institution as part of its curricula or research, development of streamlined risk-based operational approval, and the airspace and aircraft operators that may be affected. Subsection (c) says that not later than 270 days after enactment (October 5, 2018) the Administrator of the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) may establish regulations, procedures, and standards as necessary to facilitate safe school and college operations. Subsection (e) preserves FAA power: nothing here limits the Administrator's authority to pursue enforcement against a person operating any unmanned aircraft who endangers the safety of the national airspace system, and nothing here stops the Administrator from issuing rules or standards consistent with maintaining the safety and security of that system.

Illustration: A high school physics teacher wants her students to fly a small quadcopter over the athletic field to measure drop times from different heights. She holds no remote pilot certificate. Because the flight is operated by a secondary school for instruction of students, this statutory note treats it as a "recreational purpose" for the purposes of 44809, so she may fly under that exception rather than under part 107, provided she meets the conditions 44809 itself imposes (see that section). Her decision: run the lesson under 44809, and brief the students that the FAA can still take enforcement action if anyone flies in a way that endangers the national airspace system.

Common misreading: People read this note as a blanket "school drones are exempt" pass. It is narrower than that. It only reclassifies these flights as recreational for 44809 purposes, which means every condition in 44809 still applies, and it does not remove FAA enforcement authority or the agency's power to write further rules. It also does not cover work that is not educational or research, so a college that films a paid commercial advertisement with its drone is not covered by this note.
