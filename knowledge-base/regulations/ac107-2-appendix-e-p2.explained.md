This is the second half of the sample preflight assessment checklist in Appendix E of Advisory Circular (AC) 107-2A, followed by a short post-flight section. An advisory circular is FAA guidance, not a rule, and this appendix is offered as a sample — something you can adapt to your own aircraft and operation rather than a list the FAA checks off.

Items 21 through 31 cover the checks you make once the aircraft is assembled and powered, working from electronics outward to an actual low hover. They include confirming the control station (CS) and the small unmanned aircraft are talking to each other, and that the small Unmanned Aircraft System (small UAS) has acquired a Global Positioning System (GPS) location from the minimum number of satellites the manufacturer specifies. You check that avionics show correct indications — control link transceiver, communication and navigation equipment, and antennas — that the display panel works if you use one, and that ground support equipment such as takeoff and landing systems operates properly. You move the control surfaces from the CS and confirm they respond correctly, check the flight termination system if one is installed, and calibrate the compass if calibration is required before a flight. You verify controller operation for heading and altitude. For operations that will occur at civil twilight or night, you check that the anti-collision light works and can be seen for at least 3 statute miles (sm). Then you spin the propellers to listen and look for imbalance or irregular operation, and finally fly at a controlled low altitude, within range of any interference, and recheck all controls and stability.

The post-flight section has two items. First, evaluate the small UAS to decide whether repairs are needed before the next flight. Second, hold a review of the flight with any crewmembers you used, covering how the remote pilot and crewmembers performed their assigned duties, any equipment malfunctions and anomalies, whether the planned risk mitigation measures worked or need revision, unanticipated risks and hazards met during the flight and what was done about them, and crewmember concerns and feedback for making future flights safer.

The last page of the chunk is the AC feedback form — contact addresses and blanks for reporting an error or suggesting a new subject. It contains no operating guidance.

Illustration: You are set up for a roof inspection that will run into civil twilight. Working down the Appendix E list, the aircraft shows a GPS lock on more satellites than the manufacturer's minimum, the control link and antenna indications look right, and the gimbal and control surfaces respond from the control station. At item 27 you walk out and look back at the strobe: the manufacturer rates it for 3 sm, it flashes, and you can see it clearly from the far end of the property. You calibrate the compass because the app asked for it after driving to a new site, spin the props and hear one blade buzzing oddly, swap it, then hover at ten feet near the building to recheck stability where the steel structure might interfere with the signal. It holds position, so you proceed. After landing, you and your visual observer spend two minutes on the post-flight review and note that the observer lost sight of the aircraft behind a chimney — an unanticipated hazard — and agree to reposition the observer next time.

Common misreading: The 3 sm anti-collision light figure here is part of a sample checklist in guidance, and this chunk does not state the underlying rule or when a light is required — see the operating rules in part 107 for that. Also, nothing in this appendix obligates you to use these exact 31 items in this order; it is a model you tailor to your aircraft, and the FAA's own text calls it a sample. Finally, the post-flight crew review is described as a practice worth adopting, not as paperwork this chunk requires you to file anywhere.
