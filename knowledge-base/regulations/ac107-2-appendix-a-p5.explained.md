This part of Appendix A walks through the Federal Aviation Administration's (FAA) risk assessment tools using a first-person example, then starts the Personal Minimums (PAVE) Checklist. It is advisory material, not a rule. The example makes the point that a remote pilot in command (remote PIC) should think through hazards before flight, the same way pilots of manned aircraft follow preflight action under 14 CFR part 91, § 91.103 and remote pilots follow preflight familiarization, inspection, and aircraft operations under 14 CFR part 107, § 107.49 (see those sections for what they actually require). The simple process it describes: consider the hazards of this particular operation, judge how severe the risk is, then make a plan to lessen (mitigate) the risk to an acceptable level. Writing these down builds a personal library of safety practices for later flights.

PAVE stands for Personal, Aircraft, Environment, and the chunk covers the first three. Under Personal, the AC suggests setting personal minimums based on your own flight experience, health habits, and tolerance for stress, and using the IMSAFE checklist: Illness (any illness or symptom that might affect you in flight), Medication (any prescription or over-the-counter drugs), Stress (psychological or emotional factors affecting performance), Alcohol (consumed within the last 8 to 24 hours), Fatigue (enough sleep and rest recently), and Eating (sufficiently nourished). Under Aircraft, it asks whether you preflighted the small unmanned aircraft system (small UAS) — aircraft, control station (CS), takeoff and landing equipment, anti-collision light for night operations — whether it is in a condition for safe operation, and whether any payload is properly secured before flight. Under Environment, it asks about weather, your own comfort and experience in the forecast conditions, whether you have left yourself an "out," whether you have picked alternate landing spots for an emergency, and a set of night questions: how night changes your operation, what the added risks are, whether you and other aircraft in the area will be able to see the anti-collision light for at least 3 statute miles (sm) given weather and terrain, whether the flash rate is sufficient to avoid a collision, and whether you will be operating over people. The AC notes that fog, terrain features such as hills and mountains, and manmade structures can all cut down that 3 sm visibility of the light.

Illustration: You are hired to shoot aerial footage near a highway accident scene. There is one obvious clear patch of shoulder you plan to use for takeoff and landing. Before you launch, you run the hazard process: what if an emergency medical services (EMS) helicopter needs that same patch while you are airborne, and your battery is getting low? You decide the risk is acceptable only if you pick out an alternate landing site first, plus a couple of additional spots — a ditch, an empty lot — where you would be willing to sacrifice the aircraft rather than force it down near people or into the helicopter's path. You brief yourself on those spots, then fly. When the helicopter does come in, you already know where to go. Afterward you write the plan down so the next accident-scene job starts from a better baseline.

Common misreading: The IMSAFE alcohol question — "Have I consumed alcohol within the last 8 to 24 hours?" — is a self-assessment prompt in advisory guidance, not the legal alcohol limit for remote pilots. It is a window to think about, not a permission to fly at hour nine. The actual prohibition lives in the regulation itself; see that section. Likewise, the whole PAVE and IMSAFE discussion is a recommended method, so there is no violation for skipping it — but the underlying preflight requirement in § 107.49 is a rule, and these tools are just a structured way to satisfy it well.
