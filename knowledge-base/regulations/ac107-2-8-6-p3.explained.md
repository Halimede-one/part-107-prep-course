This is the tail end of the FAA's own suggested test method (means of compliance, or MOC) for the Category 2 and Category 3 injury limits — material aimed mainly at manufacturers and other applicants, not at the person flying. Two points are made.

First, on kinetic energy: the FAA-provided MOC is deliberately simple and conservative. It assumes the whole kinetic energy of the small unmanned aircraft passes into the person on impact, and it gives no credit for how the aircraft is built. The AC admits that is not what really happens — structural configuration, materials, energy-absorbing materials, or an energy-absorbing protective cage may cut how much energy actually transfers. An applicant who wants credit for that softness may instead establish the energy transferred based on the aircraft's impact-absorbing characteristics, and if the showing is that the aircraft does not transfer more than the kinetic energy limit to a person, the aircraft may be eligible for Category 2 or 3 operations over people. The catch: that kind of demonstration needs an MOC that this AC does not supply. The FAA says plainly that it knows its method is limited, that it expects industry — through a voluntary consensus standards body or otherwise — to develop more flexible methods that account for non-rigid structures, and it encourages that work. Paragraphs 8.7 and 8.8 cover other MOC; see those paragraphs.

Second, on exposed rotating parts: building the aircraft with no exposed rotating parts is one way to comply, but the AC states it is not the only way. The example given is a ducted fan, where the propellers that provide lift and thrust sit inside the aircraft and would not touch a person in a typical impact. Testing and analysis may be needed to confirm the parts cannot become exposed during a typical impact — if impact forces would likely cause structural failures that expose the rotating parts, that design does not satisfy the requirement.

Illustration: A remote pilot shopping for an aircraft to fly over a crowd at a town festival sees a model advertised with a soft foam shell and a propeller cage, and the seller's page says it is 'built to absorb impact.' The pilot asks the manufacturer which means of compliance was used. The answer is that the company ran the FAA-provided test from this AC. That test gives no credit at all for the foam or the cage — it assumes every bit of the aircraft's kinetic energy goes into the person. So the pilot cannot treat the soft construction as the thing that qualifies the aircraft for Category 2 or 3; what matters is the showing the manufacturer actually made. If the company instead wanted credit for its energy-absorbing design, it would have needed a different means of compliance, because this AC does not contain one.

Common misreading: Two traps. One: reading 'energy-absorbing materials may reduce the transfer of kinetic energy' as permission to subtract something from a kinetic energy calculation. Under the FAA-provided MOC you cannot — that method ignores construction entirely and assumes full transfer. Credit for absorption requires a different MOC that this AC does not give. Two: reading 'no exposed rotating parts' as the mandatory design. The chunk calls it one means, but not the only means, and a cage or duct that breaks open in a typical impact would not satisfy the requirement anyway.
