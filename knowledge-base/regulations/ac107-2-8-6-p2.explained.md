This part of the Advisory Circular (AC) is written for applicants — usually manufacturers — who are declaring that a small unmanned aircraft qualifies for a category of operations over people. It gives the FAA-provided Means of Compliance (MOC) for calculating how much energy the aircraft would transfer if it hit someone. The formula is KEimpact = 0.0155 x w x v-squared, where KEimpact is maximum impact kinetic energy in foot-pounds (ft-lbs), w is the aircraft weight in pounds, and v is maximum impact speed in feet per second (ft/s). The AC works two examples: a 1.0-pound aircraft at 26 ft/s yields 10.5 ft-lbs, and the same aircraft at 40 ft/s yields 24.8 ft-lbs. Tables 8-5 and 8-6 then list the maximum impact speeds, rounded to whole numbers, that keep an aircraft under the kinetic energy thresholds the tables name for each category: 11 ft-lbs for Category 2 and 25 ft-lbs for Category 3. In feet per second, those speeds run 26 and 40 at 1.0 lb, 22 and 33 at 1.5 lb, 19 and 28 at 2.0 lb, 17 and 25 at 2.5 lb, and 15 and 23 at 3.0 lb. In miles per hour (mph), they run 18 and 27 at 1.0 lb, 15 and 22 at 1.5 lb, 13 and 19 at 2.0 lb, 11 and 17 at 2.5 lb, and 10 and 16 at 3.0 lb. The AC notes these tables ignore any energy-absorbing features of the aircraft that might reduce energy actually transferred to a person. Two conditions follow. First, if the aircraft uses airspeed or altitude limiting systems to stay under a kinetic energy figure, those systems should be installed or enabled during the tests, and if compliance depends on them working properly, the applicant should explain their proper use and any restrictions in the remote pilot operating instructions. For a variable-mode aircraft that can fly with those systems on or off, the operating instructions should spell out the restrictions tied to each mode. Second, this FAA-provided MOC does not cover parachutes, ballistic recovery systems, or other deployable devices. Those features may genuinely lower impact energy, but they need separate FAA review, and an applicant who wants to rely on them must go outside this MOC and describe in a proposed MOC how the devices meet the safety requirements.

Illustration: A remote pilot buys a 2.0-pound drone the manufacturer declared for Category 2 operations over people. Reading the remote pilot operating instructions, she finds that the declaration depends on a software speed limiter, and that the aircraft also has a 'sport' mode in which the limiter is disabled. Before a job filming a crowded street fair from overhead, she confirms the aircraft is in the limited mode the instructions tie to the Category 2 declaration, and leaves sport mode alone. Her reason: the manufacturer's compliance rests on that limiting system functioning, and the operating instructions state the restriction for each mode, so flying in the unlimited mode would put her outside the basis on which the aircraft was declared.

Common misreading: Pilots read Tables 8-5 and 8-6 as speed limits the rules impose on their flying. They are not. They are test-and-calculation reference values an applicant may use to show an aircraft's maximum performance stays under a kinetic energy threshold. A pilot's real constraint comes from the aircraft's category declaration and the restrictions printed in the remote pilot operating instructions. A second misreading: fitting a parachute does not by itself move an aircraft into a lower-energy category under this MOC, because this MOC does not account for deployable devices at all — those require FAA review and a different compliance path.
