This is the definitions list for Advisory Circular (AC) 107-2A. It is vocabulary, not rules, but the wording matters because these terms carry through the rest of the document.

The terms about building and certifying aircraft: an **Applicant** is a person who submits a Declaration of Compliance (DOC) to the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) for review and acceptance, and can be anyone who designs, produces, or modifies a small unmanned aircraft. A **DOC** is the record submitted to the FAA certifying that the aircraft conforms to the Category 2 or Category 3 requirements under part 107 subpart D, described in Chapter 8 of this AC. A **Means of Compliance (MOC)** is the method the applicant uses to show its small UAS would not exceed the applicable injury severity limit upon impact with a human being, contains no exposed rotating parts that would cause lacerations, and has no safety defects. A **Voluntary Consensus Standards Body** is a domestic or international organization that plans, develops, establishes, or coordinates voluntary standards using agreed-upon procedures, observing principles such as openness, balance of interest, and due process; these can be nonprofits, industry associations, accredited standards developers, professional and technical societies, committees, task forces, or working groups.

The terms about people: the **Remote Pilot in Command (remote PIC, or remote pilot)** holds a Remote Pilot Certificate with a small UAS rating and has the final authority and responsibility for the operation and safety of a part 107 operation. A **Person Manipulating the Controls** is someone other than the remote PIC who flies the aircraft under the remote PIC's supervision. A **Visual Observer (VO)** is a person the remote PIC designates as a crewmember to assist the remote PIC and the person manipulating the controls in seeing and avoiding other air traffic or objects aloft or on the ground (§ 107.3). **Corrective Lenses** means spectacles or contact lenses.

The terms about equipment: a **Control Station (CS)** is the interface the remote pilot or person manipulating the controls uses to control the aircraft's flightpath. An **Unmanned Aircraft** is an aircraft operated without the possibility of direct human intervention from within or on the aircraft (§ 1.1). A **Small Unmanned Aircraft** is one weighing less than 55 pounds, including everything that is on board or otherwise attached to the aircraft, and flyable without the possibility of direct human intervention from within or on it. A **Small UAS** is the small unmanned aircraft plus its associated elements — including communication links and the components that control the aircraft — required for safe and efficient operation in the National Airspace System (NAS).

A footnote on the page summarizes a different legal path entirely: 49 U.S.C. § 44809(a), which lets a person operate without specific FAA certification or operating authority if the operation meets all eight listed limitations — recreational purpose only; flown per a community-based organization's FAA-coordinated safety guidelines; within visual line of sight (VLOS) of the operator or a co-located visual observer in direct communication; gives way to manned aircraft; prior authorization in Class B, C, or D airspace or within the lateral boundaries of the surface area of Class E airspace designated for an airport; in Class G, surface to not more than 400 feet above ground level (AGL); operator has passed the aeronautical knowledge and safety test under § 44809(g) and keeps proof available; and the aircraft is registered and marked under 49 U.S.C. chapter 441 with proof available on request. Illustration: You own a quadcopter that weighs 48 pounds empty. For a utility inspection you add a 4-pound sensor pod, a 2-pound spare battery strapped to the frame, and a 2-pound mount. Total: 56 pounds. Under 4.2.8, the 55-pound figure counts everything on board or otherwise attached, so this configuration is not a small unmanned aircraft and does not fit part 107 — you take off the spare battery and the mount and reconfigure to stay under 55 pounds. Separately, on that same job your trainee flies the aircraft while you watch over her shoulder: she is the person manipulating the controls (4.2.6), you remain the remote PIC with final authority and responsibility (4.2.7), and the coworker you station downrange watching for traffic is the visual observer (4.2.11).

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read the 55-pound figure as the airframe or manufacturer's listed weight — it includes the payload, batteries, gimbal, and anything strapped on. Second, the footnote's rules (400 feet AGL in Class G, community-based organization guidelines, the § 44809(g) test) are not part 107 rules. That footnote describes the separate recreational path under 49 U.S.C. § 44809, and it applies only to operations flown strictly for recreational purposes that meet all eight of its conditions.
