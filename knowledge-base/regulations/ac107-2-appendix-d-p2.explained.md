This part of Appendix D describes the two ways a Remote Pilot Certificate application gets submitted, and how the person accepting the application checks who you are.

Electronic route (D.6): Someone who meets the eligibility requirements registers as an applicant in IACRA (Integrated Airman Certification and Rating Application). IACRA holds FAA Form 8710-13 electronically until a certifying official opens it. The official finds the form by searching the applicant's unique FAA Tracking Number (FTN), which an FAA internal system assigns after the applicant completes the required items on the remote pilot application form. The certifying official verifies that the applicant meets the regulatory eligibility requirements, that the application was filled out properly, and that the applicant is who they say they are, and enters the photo identification data into IACRA when prompted. When the authorized individual finishes the application in IACRA, it goes electronically to AFB-700 for processing.

Paper route (D.7): Applicants may instead apply on a paper FAA Form 8710-13, so that everyone has an uninterrupted way to apply. The paper form captures the same information as IACRA. Once it is verified that the applicant meets the eligibility requirements of § 107.61 (see that section), the certifying official checks the "Application Accepted" box in the Submitting Official's Report section and selects the "Yes" box after "Applicant meets FAA Aviation English Language Proficiency." After the authorized individual confirms the form was completed per its instructions, the official sends it to their responsible Flight Standards office for data entry; addresses are at the FSDO (Flight Standards District Office) locator page listed in the chunk. After the data is captured, the FSDO mails the file to AFB-700 by first-class mail to: DOT/FAA, Airmen Certification Branch, AFB-720, P.O. Box 25082, Oklahoma City, OK 73125. The FAA notes that paper submittal may delay issuance of the certificate because of mailing time.

Identity checks (D.8 and D.9): The certifying official should review the photo identification presented at the time of application to confirm it is current and valid. The flight instructor should write down the type and number of the identification on the form's "Airman's Identification" section, with an expiration date — the chunk's example is a Virginia driver's license number A12345678 plus expiration date. The general review guidelines are: the applicant must present a government-issued photo identification; the official confirms the applicant matches the photo; the document must be valid, though some States do not print an expiration date on certain documents; the name must substantially match the name on the application, using best judgment because people reasonably use variations of legal, given, and nicknames (the chunk's examples: Robert Michael Smith Jr; Robert M Smith Jr; Robbie M Smith; Bob/Bobbie Smith), and a suffix such as JR, SR, II, or III may appear on the identification but not on the application; if the document shows a date of birth it must match the application; if it shows an address, compare it to the application and ask the applicant for a current address if they differ; and if it shows gender, it must match the application. The chunk refers to a "suggested list" of acceptable identification documents that is not included here.

Illustration: A sport pilot finishes the online recurrent-equivalent path and walks into a flight school to have a Certificated Flight Instructor process her Remote Pilot Certificate application. The instructor pulls up her FAA Tracking Number in IACRA, confirms the eligibility requirements are met and the form is complete, looks at her passport, compares the photo to her face, and types the identification data into IACRA when prompted. Her passport reads "Katherine Anne Doyle" but the application says "Katie A Doyle." Under D.9.1.4.1 the instructor uses best judgment: this is a recognizable variation of the same name, so it substantially matches and the application proceeds. Had the instructor instead used the paper FAA Form 8710-13, he would have checked "Application Accepted," marked "Yes" for English language proficiency, written "passport" plus the number and expiration date in the Airman's Identification section, and mailed it to the responsible Flight Standards office — and Katie's certificate would likely take longer, since the FAA notes paper adds mailing time to AFB-700.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read the paper FAA Form 8710-13 as obsolete or discouraged; the chunk says it exists specifically so all applicants have an uninterrupted ability to apply, and it captures the same information as IACRA — it is just slower. Second, people read the name-matching guideline as demanding a character-for-character match. The chunk says "substantially match" and tells the official to use best judgment about nicknames and missing suffixes. On the other hand, date of birth and gender are not treated that way: if those fields appear on the document, the chunk says they must match the application, and a differing address triggers a request for a current one.
