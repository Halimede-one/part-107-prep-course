This part of the advisory circular explains who may fly a small unmanned aircraft under Part 107 and what each person's role is. The remote pilot in command (remote PIC) is directly responsible for and is the final authority for the safe operation of the small unmanned aircraft (§ 107.19). A person acting as remote PIC must first hold a Remote Pilot Certificate with a small UAS (unmanned aircraft system) rating issued by the FAA (§ 107.12), and must keep that certificate plus personal identification in physical possession and readily accessible during flight operations (§§ 107.7 and 107.67(b)(1) through (3)). No one may operate or act as a remote PIC or visual observer (VO) for more than one small unmanned aircraft at the same time (§ 107.35).

Two or more certificated remote pilots may hand the remote PIC designation back and forth during a flight, but only if both are capable of maintaining visual line of sight (VLOS) of the aircraft without loss of control (LOC), and only by positively communicating the transfer. Whoever assumes control takes on responsibility for safe operation.

Someone who holds no Remote Pilot Certificate — or a remote pilot who has not met Part 107's recurrent training requirements — may still manipulate the flight controls, as long as a remote PIC directly supervises and can take immediate, direct control. The AC lists example methods: a "buddy box" setup with two control stations (CS) where the remote PIC's station overrides the other; the remote PIC standing close enough to physically take the CS away; or an automation system the remote PIC can engage immediately to put the aircraft into a pre-programmed safe mode such as a hover, a holding pattern, or return home.

For automated operations, the remote pilot loads a flight plan into the CS, which sends it to the autopilot on board, and flight control inputs then come from components on the aircraft rather than the CS. If the control link is lost, the aircraft continues the programmed mission or returns home to land. Even so, the remote PIC must retain the ability to change routing or altitude or command an immediate landing — by hand on the sticks or by automation command — and may command climb, descent, land now, a new waypoint, an orbit, or return to home to avoid a hazard or give right-of-way. Automation does not let one person run two aircraft at once (§ 107.35).

Illustration: You are a certificated remote pilot hired to film a high school soccer match. Your client's marketing intern wants to fly some of the footage herself, and she has no Remote Pilot Certificate. That is allowed under this guidance: she can manipulate the controls while you act as remote PIC, directly supervising with your own control station linked as the master in a buddy-box pairing, so you can override her at any moment. You keep your certificate and driver's license in your jacket pocket, readily accessible. Midway through, your co-worker — also a certificated remote pilot — wants to take over as remote PIC so you can reposition. You both confirm you can see the aircraft without losing control, and you say out loud, "You are remote PIC," and he answers, "I am remote PIC." From that point the responsibility for safe operation is his. What you cannot do is run a second aircraft over the opposite goal at the same time, even on a fully automated orbit — § 107.35 forbids one person operating more than one small unmanned aircraft simultaneously, and automation does not change that.

Common misreading: Two traps show up here. The first is reading "a non-certificated person may fly" as "supervision is loose." It is not — the remote PIC must be able to take immediate, direct control, which is why the AC describes buddy boxes, standing within arm's reach of the control station, or a one-button safe mode. Watching from across the field with no way to intervene does not meet it. The second is treating an automated flight as hands-off. Loading a flight plan does not hand off your authority; you must still be able to change routing or altitude or land the aircraft now, and you remain responsible for avoiding hazards and giving right-of-way.
