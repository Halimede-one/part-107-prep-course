This chunk is background on where Part 107 came from. Congress passed the FAA Modernization and Reform Act of 2012, Public Law (PL) 112-95. Section 333 of that law told the Secretary of Transportation to decide whether unmanned aircraft system (UAS) operations that pose the least public risk and no threat to national security could be flown safely in the National Airspace System (NAS), and if so, to set requirements for operating them safely there. Working from that direction, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) issued a final rule in June 2016 that added Part 107 and brought civil small UAS into the NAS. The practical takeaway in the last sentence: Part 107 lets you fly a small UAS for many different purposes without needing airworthiness certification, an exemption, or a Certificate of Waiver or Authorization (COA).

Illustration: A roofing contractor wants to start shooting aerial photos of damaged roofs for insurance claims. He assumes he has to get his drone certified as airworthy, or apply for an exemption the way operators did before 2016. Reading this section, he sees that Part 107 was created precisely so operations like his can happen without airworthiness certification, an exemption, or a COA. He can plan to operate under Part 107 itself rather than seeking a separate approval first, because that is the pathway the 2016 final rule established.

Common misreading: People read "without requiring airworthiness certification, exemption, or a COA" as meaning Part 107 flying needs no FAA paperwork at all. That sentence is only about those three approval mechanisms. It does not say anything about what Part 107 itself requires of you — those requirements are in the rule's own sections, so see those sections.
