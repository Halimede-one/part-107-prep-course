This section covers how an FAA-recognized identification area (FRIA) comes to an end. A FRIA is a site where unmanned aircraft without remote identification may be flown. There are two ways it ends. First, expiration: unless it is renewed, the FRIA expires on its own, and it has no further force or effect as of the day immediately after the expiration date. Second, termination before that date. Termination can happen by request — the individual listed as the point of contact for an approved FRIA can ask the Administrator to terminate it. Or the FAA can terminate it on its own if it finds any one of three things: that the FRIA may pose a risk to aviation safety, public safety, homeland security, or national security; that the FRIA is no longer associated with a person who is eligible to have one; or that the person who requested the FRIA gave false or misleading information when submitting, amending, or renewing it. When the FAA terminates, it notifies the primary point of contact of the decision and the reasons. If the termination was based on a risk finding (safety, public safety, homeland security, or national security), that location stays ineligible to be a FRIA for as long as those conditions remain in effect — subject to the reconsideration process in paragraph (c). That process gives a person no later than 30 calendar days after termination to petition the Administrator for reconsideration. The petition has to state the reasons justifying the request and include any supporting documentation. The Administrator reviews what was submitted and notifies the petitioner of the decision. Finally, the rule says part 13, subpart D, of this chapter does not apply to the termination and reconsideration procedures in paragraphs (b) and (c) — see that subpart for what it otherwise covers.

Illustration: A model aircraft club holds an approved FRIA at its field, and the club secretary is listed as the primary point of contact. The club votes to relocate and no longer wants the old field designated. The secretary submits a request to the Administrator to terminate the FRIA, which paragraph (b)(1) allows the point of contact to do. A year later at a different club, the FAA sends the primary point of contact a notice terminating that club's FRIA, stating the reason is a finding that the area may pose a risk to aviation safety. The club disagrees. Because the decision came from a risk finding, the field stays ineligible as a FRIA while those conditions hold — so the club's only route is paragraph (c). It drafts a petition for reconsideration, states its reasons, attaches its site survey and traffic data, and files it 18 calendar days after the termination, inside the 30-calendar-day window. The Administrator reviews the submission and notifies the club of the decision.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read the 30 calendar days as a grace period during which the FRIA keeps operating. It is not — it is only the window for filing a petition for reconsideration; the chunk says nothing about the termination being paused while the petition is pending. Second, people assume a FRIA runs indefinitely once approved. Paragraph (a) is explicit that it expires automatically unless renewed, and it has no force or effect starting the day right after the expiration date — no notice from the FAA is required to make that happen.
