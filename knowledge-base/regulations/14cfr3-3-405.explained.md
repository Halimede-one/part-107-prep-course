This section lets the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) Administrator take back or hold up something it granted you when the paperwork behind it contained an incorrect statement or left out a fact. Paragraph (a) lists the actions available: suspending, modifying, revoking, rescinding, removing, or withdrawing an acceptance, approval, authorization, certificate, rating, declaration, designation, qualification, or similar, and also denying an application or a request for reconsideration or similar.

Two situations can trigger that. Under (a)(1), the incorrect statement or omission appears in, or is missing from, a document submitted under any provision referenced in § 3.401 — see that section for which provisions those are. Under (a)(2), the problem is in a document that is kept, made, or used to show compliance with a requirement under those same referenced provisions. So it is not only the application you file; it is also the records you keep to prove you followed the rules.

In both cases the chunk sets one qualifier: the incorrect statement or omission must have been material to the issuance, validity, or granting of the thing at issue. The section applies to "any person," and to documents "in any format." Paragraph (b) is reserved, meaning no text has been placed there.

Illustration: A remote pilot applies for an authorization and, on the form, states a piece of information that is not correct — and that information is one of the things the FAA relied on to grant the authorization. Months later the FAA notices the error. Under (a)(1), the agency may withdraw or revoke that authorization, because the incorrect statement was material to granting it. The same exposure exists for the pilot's own compliance records: if a record the pilot keeps to show he met a requirement under the provisions referenced in § 3.401 omits a material fact, (a)(2) reaches that record too, even though it was never mailed to the FAA.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people assume this only covers the application packet, so a sloppy in-house logbook or compliance file is harmless — but (a)(2) covers documents merely kept, made, or used to show compliance. Second, people read the word "incorrect" as requiring a lie. This section as written is about incorrect statements and omissions of fact that were material; it does not state an intent requirement, and it does not say an honest mistake is excused. The limiting word here is "material," not "deliberate."
