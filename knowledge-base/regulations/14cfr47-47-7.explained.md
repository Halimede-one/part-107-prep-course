This section spells out who counts as an eligible owner when registering an aircraft under Part 47, and what proof each kind of owner has to supply.

A U.S. citizen applicant simply certifies that fact on the Aircraft Registration Application, AC Form 8050-1. A resident alien applicant must instead furnish a representation of permanent residence plus the alien registration number issued by the Department of Homeland Security.

Trusts get more detailed treatment. If the applicant holds legal title in trust, every trustee must be either a U.S. citizen or a resident alien. The application must include a copy of each document that legally affects a relationship under the trust. If all beneficiaries, including anyone whose security interest in the aircraft is folded into the trust, are U.S. citizens or resident aliens, the applicant files an affidavit saying so. If any beneficiary is not, then each trustee must file an affidavit stating the trustee is not aware of any reason, situation, or relationship by which those non-citizen, non-resident-alien persons together would have more than 25 percent of the aggregate power to influence or limit the trustee's authority. Separately, if such persons can direct or remove a trustee, whether directly or indirectly through control of another person, the trust instrument itself must cap their combined power to direct or remove at 25 percent. The section says plainly that this cap does not stop those persons from holding more than 25 percent of the beneficial interest.

Partnerships are the strictest case. A partnership may apply for a Certificate of Aircraft Registration only if every partner, general or limited, is an individual who is a U.S. citizen. The section then preserves one path: an aircraft that is not a partnership asset but is simply co-owned is not made ineligible when the co-owners are resident aliens, or a mix of one or more resident aliens and one or more U.S. citizens.

Illustration: Two friends want to register a larger drone under Part 47 rather than Part 48. One is a U.S. citizen, the other a lawful permanent resident. They first consider forming a partnership to own the aircraft. Reading paragraph (d), they realize a partnership qualifies only if every partner is an individual U.S. citizen, so the resident-alien partner would sink the application. Instead they take title as individual co-owners, not as a partnership asset. Paragraph (d)(2) covers exactly that mix of one or more resident aliens with one or more U.S. citizens, so the aircraft stays eligible. On the application, the citizen certifies citizenship; the resident alien furnishes a representation of permanent residence and the alien registration number issued by the Department of Homeland Security.

Common misreading: People read the 25 percent figures as a single ownership cap. They are two different things, and neither is about how much of the trust someone owns. One is a limit on the aggregate power of non-citizens and non-resident aliens to influence or limit the trustee's authority, addressed by trustee affidavit. The other is a limit written into the trust instrument on their aggregate power to direct or remove a trustee. The chunk states outright that nothing in that paragraph prevents those persons from holding more than 25 percent of the beneficial interest. Also note that paragraph (a) is about certifying on AC Form 8050-1, not about producing citizenship documents.
