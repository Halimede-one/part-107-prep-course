This part of Chapter 11 covers chart and navigation basics. Automatic Terminal Information Service (ATIS) recordings are labeled with a phonetic-alphabet code that changes every time the recording is updated: ATIS Alpha is replaced by ATIS Bravo, then Charlie, then Delta, and so on down the alphabet. An aeronautical chart is described as the pilot's road map, giving remote pilots information about the areas where they intend to operate. The two charts used by Visual Flight Rules (VFR) pilots are the Sectional and the VFR Terminal Area chart, and a free catalog of charts and publications with prices and ordering instructions is at the Aeronautical Navigation Products website, www.aeronav.faa.gov. Sectional charts are the most commonly used, at a scale of 1:500,000 (1 inch = 6.86 nautical miles (NM), or approximately 8 statute miles (SM)). They show airport data, navigational aids, airspace, and topography, and the legend is the key to reading most of it; the legend area also carries air traffic control (ATC) frequencies and airspace information. Sectionals are revised semiannually, except for some areas outside the conterminous United States, which are revised annually. On position: parallels of latitude run east and west, parallel to the equator, and measure degrees north (N) or south (S) of the equator; the angular distance from equator to pole is one-fourth of a circle, or 90 degrees. The 48 conterminous states lie between 25 degrees and 49 degrees N latitude. Meridians of longitude run from the North Pole to the South Pole at right angles to the equator, measured east (E) and west (W) from the Prime Meridian at Greenwich, England, out to 180 degrees; the 48 conterminous states lie between 67 degrees and 125 degrees W longitude. Any point can be named by its latitude and longitude: Washington, D.C. is roughly 39 degrees N, 77 degrees W, and Chicago roughly 42 degrees N, 88 degrees W. Variation is the angle between true north (TN) and magnetic north (MN), called east or west variation depending on which side of true north the magnetic north lies. The north magnetic pole sits close to 71 degrees N latitude, 96 degrees W longitude, about 1,300 miles from the true north pole. Because the Earth is not uniformly magnetized, the compass needle in the United States points generally toward the magnetic pole but can be off by many degrees in certain localities, so variation has been measured at thousands of selected locations. Charts show it as broken magenta isogonic lines connecting points of equal variation; the line where there is no variation between TN and MN is the agonic line. Bends in these lines come from unusual geological conditions affecting magnetic forces. The last paragraph begins a note on antenna towers: extreme caution should be used when flying less than 2,000 feet above ground level (AGL) because of numerous skeletal structures such as radio and television antenna towers that exceed 1,000 feet AGL.

Illustration: You are planning a mapping flight over farmland and you pull up the sectional for the area. Using the legend, you confirm the airspace class over the field, note the nearby airport data, and spot a magenta obstacle symbol for a television antenna tower. Because the chunk warns that such towers can exceed 1,000 feet AGL and that caution is needed below 2,000 feet AGL, you shift your flight area away from the tower and its supporting guy wires rather than planning to fly near it. While still on the chart, you read off the field's approximate latitude and longitude so you can describe the location precisely to your client. Decision: fly the offset area, because the chart told you where the hazard and the airspace boundaries are before you launched.

Common misreading: Two traps here. First, the sectional revision cycle: semiannually means the paper chart you bought months ago may be out of date, and the annual cycle applies only to some areas outside the conterminous United States, not to the charts you use over the lower 48. Second, the antenna tower caution is aviation-wide guidance about flight below 2,000 feet AGL; it is not a statement that small unmanned aircraft may fly up to 2,000 feet. Nothing in this chunk sets an altitude limit for remote pilots.
