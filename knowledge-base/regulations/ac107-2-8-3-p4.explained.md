This chunk covers the last two ways a Category 3 operation can legally happen, then introduces Category 4.

For Category 3, there are two paths. First, a closed- or restricted-access site: everyone inside the site must be on notice that a small unmanned aircraft may fly over them, and the area must not be an open-air assembly. People at the site who are not directly participating in the operation but who are performing functions there still have to be on notice, and should be advised of precautions or recommended actions if needed. The remote pilot is responsible for making sure nobody wanders in or gets in without authorization. Acceptable ways to do that include physical barriers such as barricading and fencing, or monitoring personnel. Natural geography — rivers, canals, cliffs, heavily wooded areas — can also work as a barrier.

Second, outside a closed- or restricted-access site, Category 3 operations are allowed as long as the aircraft does not sustain flight over people who are not participating in the operation. The chunk says the intent is momentary exposure, not sustained exposure over one or more persons. Sustained flight includes hovering above someone's head, flying back and forth over a person, or circling above an uninvolved person so that the aircraft stays above some part of them. The remote pilot should adjust the flightpath to minimize exposure, and may need to stop the operation entirely if the needed flightpath would require sustained flight over people.

Category 4 works differently. The FAA describes certification as how it manages risk through safety assurance. An eligible Category 4 small unmanned aircraft must have an airworthiness certificate issued by the FAA under part 21, and must be operated according to the operating limitations in the FAA-approved Flight Manual or as otherwise specified by the Administrator. That airworthiness certificate allows small unmanned aircraft operations for compensation and hire. To fly over people under § 107.140 and over moving vehicles under § 107.145(c), the remote pilot must follow every operating limitation that applies to the aircraft as specified by the Administrator, and those limitations must not prohibit operations over people. Category 4 remote pilots are prohibited from sustained flight over open-air assemblies unless the operation meets the requirements of § 89.110 or § 89.115(a) — see those sections. That prohibition can be waived.

Category 4 also carries maintenance obligations under § 107.140(c). Maintenance, preventive maintenance, or alterations must use the methods, techniques, and practices prescribed in the manufacturer's current maintenance manual or the instructions for continued airworthiness (ICA) prepared by the manufacturer, or other methods acceptable to the Administrator. The aircraft must be inspected per the manufacturer's instructions or other instructions acceptable to the Administrator, and any parts used must be of such quality that the aircraft ends up at least equal to its original or properly altered condition.

Illustration: You are hired to photograph progress on a highway bridge repair with a Category 3 aircraft. The work zone is fenced on three sides and bounded by a river on the fourth. At the morning safety briefing you tell every worker on site — including the flaggers and the concrete crew, who are not helping you fly — that a drone will be overhead that day and that they should stay clear of the pier area when they hear it. The fence and the river keep the public out, and a crew member watches the one gate. This is a valid closed- or restricted-access site, so you may fly over the workers. Later you want a shot of the bridge deck from the public sidewalk side, outside the fence, where pedestrians are walking. You can still fly there under Category 3, but only by transiting across — one pass, keep moving. When a group stops on the sidewalk to watch and you realize the shot you want would mean hovering above them for thirty seconds, you reposition to shoot from an angle that keeps the aircraft off their heads. If no such angle exists, you discontinue that shot, because hovering above a person's head is sustained flight.

Common misreading: Two traps. The first is treating "brief period" as a number you can look up and then budget against — the standard is momentary exposure versus sustained exposure, and hovering, flying back and forth over someone, or circling so the aircraft stays above part of them all count as sustained no matter how few seconds are involved. The second is assuming a Category 4 airworthiness certificate is a blanket pass over people. It is not: you must also comply with every operating limitation specified by the Administrator, those limitations must not themselves prohibit flight over people, and sustained flight over open-air assemblies is still prohibited unless you meet § 89.110 or § 89.115(a) or hold a waiver. Pilots also tend to overlook that Category 4 brings real maintenance and inspection duties under § 107.140(c) that Categories 1 through 3 do not.
