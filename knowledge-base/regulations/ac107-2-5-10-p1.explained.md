This part of the advisory circular (AC) covers where you may fly in relation to airspace and airports. Small unmanned aircraft may operate in either controlled or uncontrolled airspace. But you may not operate in Class B, Class C, or Class D airspace, or within the lateral boundaries of the surface area of Class E airspace designated for an airport, unless you have prior authorization from air traffic control (ATC) under section 107.41. The current authorization process is described at https://www.faa.gov/uas/. The remote pilot in command (PIC) is expected to understand airspace classifications and requirements; not understanding them can put you contrary to part 107 and can hurt safety. The AC notes that small unmanned aircraft systems (UAS) flying under part 107 may not be subject to part 91 requirements, since part 91's equipage and communications rules were written to provide safety and efficiency in the National Airspace System (NAS); still, ATC authorizations may depend on operational parameters similar to those in part 91. The FAA may approve or deny operations based on traffic density, controller workload, communication issues, or any other operation that could affect the safe and expeditious flow of air traffic there.

On airports (5.10.1): unless the flight is in controlled airspace, no notification or authorization is needed to fly at or near an airport. Near an airport you must still be aware of and avoid all traffic patterns and approach corridors to runways and landing areas, and must avoid any area where your small UAS may interfere with airport operations — approach corridors, taxiways, runways, or helipads (section 107.43). You must yield right of way to all other aircraft, including aircraft on the airport surface (section 107.43).

Section 5.10.1.1 explains that interference is a separate problem from a near miss. You are prohibited from operating in a way that interferes with operations and traffic patterns at airports, heliports, and seaplane bases. Small unmanned aircraft must always yield right of way to a manned aircraft, and a manned aircraft may alter its flightpath, delay its landing, or take off to avoid a small unmanned aircraft that presents a potential conflict. The AC's own example: a small unmanned aircraft hovering 200 feet above a runway may cause a manned aircraft holding short to delay takeoff, or a manned aircraft on the downwind leg to delay landing. Even though that drone would not be an immediate traffic conflict and would not violate the right-of-way provision of section 107.37(a), it would have interfered with traffic pattern operations at the airport.

Section 5.10.1.2 gives the practical standard: avoid flying in the traffic pattern or published approach corridors used by manned aircraft. If operational necessity puts you at an airport in uncontrolled airspace, fly so that the manned pilot does not need to alter his or her flightpath in the traffic pattern or on a published instrument approach to avoid a potential collision.

Section 5.10.2 addresses the Air Traffic Organization (ATO). When it receives requests to operate in controlled airspace, ATO does not approve or deny based on equipage beyond what part 107 requires. Extra equipment and technologies such as geo-fencing have not been certificated by the FAA and would need case-by-case examination for reliability and functionality, and reviewing equipage would burden ATO staff and pull them from other duties. A remote pilot who wants to operate in controlled airspace on the strength of mitigations through equipage should instead apply for a Certificate of Waiver (CoW) — see paragraph 5.20 of this AC.

Section 5.10.3 covers Temporary Flight Restrictions (TFRs). Some TFRs are imposed by way of a Notice to Airmen (NOTAM), and the AC points to https://www.1800wxbrief.com. The remote PIC must check for NOTAMs before each flight to see whether any airspace restrictions apply.

Illustration: A remote pilot is hired to photograph a hangar at a small non-towered airport that sits in uncontrolled airspace. Because the airport is not in controlled airspace, she needs no notification or authorization to fly there. Before launching she checks NOTAMs at 1800wxbrief.com and finds none affecting the field. On site she sets up on the ramp side away from the runway, keeps the aircraft low and clear of the runway, taxiways, and the approach corridor, and watches the pattern. A light plane enters the downwind leg; she lands the drone and waits, then yields again as the plane taxis in on the surface. Her reasoning: even hovering well clear of the plane's path, parking the drone over the runway or in the pattern could make that pilot delay landing, and that counts as interfering with airport operations under 107.43 — a violation even when no collision was ever likely.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read "no notification or authorization is necessary at or near an airport" as a blanket pass for all airports. It applies only when the flight is outside controlled airspace; a towered airport in Class B, C, or D, or inside the surface-area Class E of a designated airport, still requires prior ATC authorization under 107.41. Second, pilots assume that if they never came close to a manned aircraft they are fine. The AC's 200-foot-over-the-runway example makes the opposite point: that flight breaks no right-of-way provision of 107.37(a) and poses no immediate conflict, yet it still interferes with the traffic pattern and is prohibited by 107.43. Also note that having fancy equipment such as geo-fencing does not improve your odds with ATC — ATO does not weigh equipage beyond part 107 requirements; that argument belongs in a Certificate of Waiver application instead.
