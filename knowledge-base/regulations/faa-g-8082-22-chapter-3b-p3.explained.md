This chunk explains how moisture and temperature combine to set the stability of the air, and what that means for a remote pilot. Cool, dry air is very stable, resists vertical movement, and generally brings clear weather. The most unstable air is moist and warm — the chunk gives tropical regions in summer as the example, where thunderstorms appear daily.

A temperature inversion is the exception to the usual pattern of air cooling as it rises. In an inversion the temperature of the air rises with altitude up to the top of the inversion. These layers are commonly shallow, smooth, and stable, and sit close to the ground. The air at the top acts like a lid, trapping weather and pollutants underneath. If relative humidity is high, you can get clouds, fog, haze, or smoke inside the layer, which cuts visibility. Surface-based inversions form on clear, cool nights when the ground cools the air touching it, so the air within a few hundred feet of the surface becomes cooler than the air above. Frontal inversions form when warm air spreads over cooler air, or cooler air is forced under warmer air.

The dew point is the temperature, given in degrees, at which the air can hold no more moisture. When air is cooled to its dew point it is completely saturated, and moisture condenses out as fog, dew, frost, clouds, rain, or snow. The relationship between dew point and temperature is what defines relative humidity. When temperature and dew point are close together, fog, low clouds, and precipitation are highly likely. The chunk lists four ways air reaches saturation: warm air moving over a cold surface; cold air mixing with warm air; air cooling at night by contact with the cooler ground; and air being lifted or forced upward.

Dew and frost both come from the same process — on cool, clear, calm nights the ground and objects on it chill the surrounding air below the dew point, and moisture deposits on the ground, buildings, cars, and aircraft. Above freezing you get dew; below freezing you get frost. The chunk is explicit about the safety difference: dew poses no threat to a small unmanned aircraft (small UA), but frost is a definite flight safety hazard. Frost disrupts the airflow over the wing, can drastically reduce lift, and increases drag. Together those two effects can hurt the ability to take off. The chunk states the requirement plainly: a small UA must be thoroughly cleaned and free of frost before beginning a flight.

On clouds, the cumulonimbus is called perhaps the most dangerous cloud type for pilots. It shows up alone or in groups, as either an air mass thunderstorm (created by heating of the air near the Earth's surface) or an orographic thunderstorm (created by upslope motion of air in mountainous regions). Cumulonimbus clouds forming in a continuous line are nonfrontal bands of thunderstorms, or squall lines. Because rising air currents build them, they are extremely turbulent. The chunk's example: a small UA entering a thunderstorm could experience updrafts and downdrafts exceeding 3,000 fpm (feet per minute). Thunderstorms can also produce large hailstones, damaging lightning, tornadoes, and large quantities of water.

Standing lenticular altocumulus clouds form on the crests of waves created by barriers in the wind flow. They show little movement — hence "standing" — and have smooth, polished edges. Wind blowing through them can be quite strong. Their presence is a good indication of very strong turbulence and they should be avoided.

The closing table pairs the typical signatures of unstable and stable air. Unstable air: cumuliform clouds, showery precipitation, rough air (turbulence), and good visibility except in blowing obstructions. Stable air: stratiform clouds and fog, continuous precipitation, smooth air, and fair to poor visibility in haze and smoke.

Illustration: A remote pilot arrives at a field survey site at 7:30 a.m. after a clear, calm night. The temperature is a few degrees below freezing and there is a white coating on the car windshield and on the grass. The aircraft spent the night in an open truck bed and its arms and propellers have the same coating. That is frost, not dew, because the temperature is below freezing. The pilot does not launch. Frost disrupts the airflow over the lifting surfaces, drastically reducing lift while increasing drag, and the combination can keep the aircraft from taking off. The pilot moves the aircraft into a warm vehicle, waits for the frost to melt, wipes the surfaces thoroughly dry, and only then launches. While waiting, the pilot also notes shallow fog sitting in the low ground — a sign of a surface-based inversion holding moisture near the surface — and reassesses whether visibility will be adequate.

Common misreading: Two misreadings are common here. First, people treat dew and frost as the same pre-flight nuisance. The chunk draws a sharp line: dew poses no threat to a small UA, while frost is a definite flight safety hazard that must be cleaned off before flight. Second, people assume that because small unmanned aircraft are light and fly low, a thunderstorm overhead is only a rain-and-lightning problem. The chunk's numbers say otherwise — the updrafts and downdrafts inside can exceed 3,000 fpm, which is far beyond what a small UA can fly against, and that is on top of hail, lightning, tornadoes, and heavy water. Related: a standing lenticular cloud that is not moving can look like calm air. It is the opposite — the cloud stands still while strong wind blows through it, and it signals very strong turbulence to avoid.
