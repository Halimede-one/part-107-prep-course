This part of AC 107-2A (Advisory Circular 107-2A) explains when the remote pilot in command (remote PIC) must report an accident to the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), and how. A report is required within 10 days if the accident meets either threshold. First threshold: at least serious injury to any person, or any loss of consciousness. The circular defines serious injury as an injury at Level 3 or higher on the Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) of the Association for the Advancement of Automotive Medicine (AAAM) — an anatomical scoring system used by emergency medical personnel to rank injury severity, and already used as an injury threshold in other FAA regulations. The AC's AIS 3 example is a person who requires hospitalization from an injury that can fully heal, such as head trauma, a broken bone, or a laceration needing suturing. Second threshold: damage to any property other than the small unmanned aircraft itself, if the cost is greater than $500 to repair or replace the property — whichever of those two figures is lower. The AC gives two examples under § 107.9: $600 repair cost on property worth $200 fair market value is not reportable, and $200 of damage to property worth $600 is also not reportable, because in each case the lower figure falls below $500.

On submitting: the report must be made within 10 calendar-days of the operation that caused the injury or damage. It may go to the appropriate FAA Regional Operations Center (ROC) electronically at https://www.faa.gov/uas/ or by telephone using the ROC list in Figure 4-1, or to the responsible Flight Standards office. The AC says the report should include nine items: the remote PIC's name and contact information; the remote PIC's FAA airman certificate number; the aircraft's FAA registration number; the location, date, and local time of the accident; whether any serious injury or fatality occurred; the property damaged and extent of damage if any or known; and a description of what happened. Figure 4-1 lists four ROC phone numbers by the location where the accident occurred: 404-305-5150 for DC, DE, MD, NJ, NY, PA, WV, and VA; 404-305-5156 for AL, CT, FL, GA, KY, MA, ME, MS, NC, NH, PR, RI, SC, TN, VI, and VT; 425-227-1999 for AK, AS, AZ, CA, CO, GU, HI, ID, MP, MT, NV, OR, UT, WA, and WY; and 817-222-5006 for AR, IA, IL, IN, KS, LA, MI, MN, MO, ND, NE, NM, OH, OK, SD, TX, and WI.

Separately, some accidents must also be reported to the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB). NTSB rules at 49 CFR part 830 require immediate notification of an aircraft accident. NTSB defines an "unmanned aircraft accident" as an occurrence associated with the operation of any public or civil unmanned aircraft system (UAS), between the time the system is activated with the purpose of flight and the time it is deactivated at the end of its mission, in which any person suffers death or serious injury, or the aircraft has a maximum gross takeoff weight of 300 pounds or greater and sustains substantial damage. The NTSB has its own definitions of "serious injury" and "substantial damage" in 49 CFR § 830.2 — see that section. More information is at https://www.ntsb.gov.

Illustration: You are flying an inspection mission over a commercial roof in Ohio when a motor fails and the aircraft drops onto a parked delivery van in the lot below, cracking a side window and denting the door. No one is hurt. The body shop quotes $1,150 to repair; the van's fair market value is about $12,000. You compare the two figures and take the lower one — $1,150 — which is greater than $500, so the accident is reportable. You file within 10 calendar-days of the flight, either electronically at https://www.faa.gov/uas/ or by calling the ROC at 817-222-5006 (Ohio is on that line), and you include your name and contact information, your airman certificate number, the aircraft's FAA registration number, the location, date, and local time, the fact that no serious injury or fatality occurred, the damage to the van, and a description of the motor failure. Because no person died or was seriously injured and your aircraft is well under 300 pounds maximum gross takeoff weight, the NTSB notification criteria described here are not triggered.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read the $500 test as "repair cost over $500" and stop there. The AC says to use repair or replacement cost, whichever is lower — so a $600 repair bill on a $200 item is below the threshold, and $200 of damage to a $600 item is too. Second, people assume one report covers everything. The FAA report to the ROC and the NTSB notification are separate obligations with different triggers and different timing: the FAA report is within 10 days, while NTSB rules require immediate notification, and the NTSB uses its own definitions of serious injury and substantial damage plus a 300-pound maximum gross takeoff weight element. Also note the property damage threshold applies to property other than the small unmanned aircraft — destroying your own drone with no other damage and no injury is not what this reporting trigger is about.
