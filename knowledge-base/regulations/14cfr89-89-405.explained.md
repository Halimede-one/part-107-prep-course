This section covers who may hand the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) a "means of compliance" for remote identification, and what that submission must contain. A means of compliance is the method or standard that shows how a drone or add-on module actually meets the remote ID performance requirements. Paragraph (a) opens the door wide: any person may submit one. There is no requirement to be a manufacturer, a standards body, or a certificate holder. Paragraph (b) lists what has to go in, submitted in a form and manner acceptable to the Administrator: (1) the name of the person or entity submitting it, the name of the main point of contact for talking with the FAA, a physical address, an email address, and other contact information; (2) a detailed description of the means of compliance; (3) an explanation of how it addresses all of the minimum performance requirements in subpart D of this part, so that any standard remote identification unmanned aircraft or remote identification broadcast module built in accordance with it meets this part's remote ID requirements; and (4) any substantiating material the submitter wants the FAA to consider. Paragraph (c) adds one more mandatory ingredient: the means of compliance must include testing and validation procedures that the people producing the aircraft or modules can use to demonstrate, through analysis, ground test, or flight test as appropriate, how the product performs its intended functions and meets the subpart D requirements. That includes any applicable FAA performance requirements for radio station operation. See subpart D for what those minimum performance requirements actually are.

Illustration: A small engineering firm develops a broadcast module design and wants a documented standard that other builders could follow. The firm is not a large manufacturer and has never submitted anything to the FAA before. Under paragraph (a), that is not a barrier — any person may submit. The firm assembles a package: company name, the lead engineer as the main point of contact, physical and email addresses, a detailed description of the method, and a section mapping each subpart D performance requirement to how the method satisfies it. Before sending it, the firm checks paragraph (c) and realizes the package is incomplete: it describes the design but gives no test procedures. The firm adds bench test steps and a flight test profile that a producer could run to show the module performs its intended functions, including the radio station performance requirements. Only then does it submit. The reason is that paragraph (c) makes testing and validation procedures part of the means of compliance itself, not an optional attachment.

Common misreading: People read this as the approval path for an individual drone, as if submitting a means of compliance gets a particular aircraft blessed by the FAA. It does not. This section is about getting a method accepted — a recipe that any producer could then build to. It is also easy to skim "any substantiating material the person wishes the FAA to consider" in paragraph (b)(4) and assume the whole submission is optional supporting paperwork; only item (4) is discretionary. Items (1) through (3) and the testing and validation procedures in paragraph (c) are required.
