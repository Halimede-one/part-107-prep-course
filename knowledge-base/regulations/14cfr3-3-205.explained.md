This section covers what happens to your application for an FAA certificate when the Transportation Security Administration (TSA) raises a security concern about you. There are two stages. First, if the TSA tells the FAA that an applicant poses, or is suspected of posing, a risk of air piracy or terrorism, or a threat to airline or passenger safety, the FAA puts that person's certificate applications on hold — held in abeyance — and waits for further word from the TSA. Nothing is decided yet; the application simply stops moving. Second, if the TSA later tells the FAA it has made a final security threat determination about the person, the FAA denies all of that person's certificate applications. If instead the TSA tells the FAA it has withdrawn the security threat determination, the FAA resumes processing the applications. The trigger in both stages is a notification from the TSA, not an FAA judgment of its own. Note that this section speaks about individuals and about applications for a certificate or any part of a certificate. Illustration: A person passes the Part 107 aeronautical knowledge test and submits an application for a remote pilot certificate. Weeks pass with no temporary certificate and no denial letter — the application just sits. Behind the scenes, the TSA has notified the FAA that it suspects this applicant poses a security threat, so under paragraph (a) the FAA is holding the application in abeyance while it waits for the TSA's next word. The applicant may not fly commercially in the meantime, because no certificate has been issued. Months later the TSA completes its review and withdraws the determination, and notifies the FAA. Under paragraph (b), the FAA then continues processing the application. Had the TSA instead reported a final security threat determination, the FAA would have denied the application — and any other certificate application that person had pending.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read the hold in paragraph (a) as a denial. It is not. An application in abeyance is paused, and paragraph (b) shows it can still be processed to completion if the TSA withdraws its determination. But a paused application is also not an issued certificate, so there is no authority to fly on it. Second, people assume the FAA can weigh the evidence and overrule the TSA. As written, the FAA acts on the TSA's notification — abeyance on a suspicion, denial on a final security threat determination, resumed processing on a withdrawal.
