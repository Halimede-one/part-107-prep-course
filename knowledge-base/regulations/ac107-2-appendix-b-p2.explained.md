This part of Appendix B is advisory background on things that can bite a small unmanned aircraft system (small UAS) operation: wind, surface heating, battery fires, and radio frequencies.

Wind: wind speed and direction matter because they affect takeoff, landing, and cruise. Near the ground, terrain, trees, structures, and other anomalies break up the airflow and create gusts that change direction and speed quickly. The remote pilot in command (remote PIC) should be vigilant near large buildings, other man-made structures, and natural obstructions such as mountains, bluffs, or canyons. How rough the turbulence gets depends on the size of the obstacle and the primary velocity of the wind. In mountains, air flows smoothly up the windward side and the rising current helps carry an aircraft over the peak, but on the leeward side the air follows the terrain downward and gets increasingly turbulent, which tends to push an aircraft into the mountainside. Stronger wind means greater downward pressure and turbulence, and downdrafts in valleys or canyons can be severe.

Surface heat: surfaces radiate heat unevenly. Plowed ground, rocks, sand, and barren land give off more heat; water, trees, and vegetated areas absorb and retain it. The uneven heating makes small local circulations called convective currents, with rising and sinking air that produces bumpy, turbulent flight and can hurt controllability.

Battery fires: lithium-based batteries are highly flammable and can ignite. A battery fire can create an in-flight emergency by causing loss of control (LOC). Causes listed include a short circuit, improper charging, heating to extreme temperatures, crash damage, mishandling, or a simple defect. The remote PIC should consider following the manufacturer's recommendations, where they exist, for safe battery handling and use.

Frequencies: a small UAS typically uses radio frequencies (RF) for the link between the control station (CS) and the aircraft. Most use the unlicensed 2.4 GHz and 5.8 GHz bands. Those same bands carry computer wireless networks, so areas thick with wireless signals — dense housing, office buildings — can bring interference, and LOC and flyaways are among the reported problems. Many modern systems split the job: 5.8 GHz to control the aircraft and 2.4 GHz to send video and photos down. Check the operating manual and the manufacturer's recommended procedures first. Both bands are line of sight, so the command and control link will not work properly with barriers between the control station and the aircraft. The advisory circular notes that part 107's requirement for the remote PIC or person manipulating the controls to see the aircraft at all times should also help keep obstructions out of that line-of-sight radio path.

Spectrum authorization: the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) regulates the spectrum used for these operations and authorizes civil operations. Control transmissions and real-time video downlinks must use frequency bands approved for the operating agency's use. Some frequencies are unlicensed and free to use without FCC approval — 900 MHz, 2.4 GHz, and 5.8 GHz are the examples given. All other frequencies require a user-specific FCC license for all civil users, with Federal agencies excepted. The chunk cuts off mid-sentence where it points to a website for more information.

Illustration: You are hired to photograph a hillside housing development on a windy afternoon. The wind is blowing across the ridge, and the houses sit on the downwind side. You plan to launch from the valley below and climb along the slope. Reading this appendix, you recognize two problems stacked on each other: the leeward side of that ridge will have air following the terrain downward and growing more turbulent, tending to push the aircraft into the hillside, and the valley itself can hold severe downdrafts. On top of that, a dense block of houses means many wireless networks sharing your 2.4 GHz and 5.8 GHz bands, raising the odds of interference on a link that needs clear line of sight. You decide to reschedule for a calm morning and to position the control station on the open high ground with nothing between you and the aircraft. The reason is simple: the guidance says turbulence intensity scales with obstacle size and wind velocity, and that barriers between control station and aircraft keep the link from working properly.

Common misreading: The 900 MHz, 2.4 GHz, and 5.8 GHz bands being unlicensed does not mean they are protected or reliable. Free to use is not the same as free of interference — the chunk names those same bands as the source of interference from wireless networks, with loss of control and flyaways among the reported results. A second misreading: the chunk says part 107's see-the-aircraft requirement should also help prevent obstructions from interfering with the radio line of sight. That is a side benefit, not a guarantee; keeping the aircraft in sight does not by itself guarantee an unobstructed radio path.
