Part 107 lets you carry property with a small unmanned aircraft for compensation or hire, but with boundaries. The operation must stay in a confined area, follow all the operating restrictions of part 107, and the transport must happen wholly within the bounds of a single State. Two limits that are waivable for other kinds of flying become hard walls here. First, visual line of sight (VLOS) of the remote pilot is required; VLOS can be waived for some part 107 operations, but not for transportation of property. Second, part 107 does not allow operating a small unmanned aircraft system (small UAS) from a moving vehicle or aircraft when the aircraft is transporting property for compensation or hire, and that limitation cannot be waived either. The total weight of the small unmanned aircraft, counting the property on board, must stay under 55 pounds. Other part 107 provisions still apply: you must know the aircraft's location, determine its attitude, altitude, and direction, yield the right-of-way to other aircraft, and keep the ability to see and avoid other aircraft. Separately, Section 107.36 prohibits carrying hazardous materials by small unmanned aircraft. Hazardous material can be carried only if the operator holds an exemption permitting it, and petitions for exemption are governed by 14 CFR part 11 (Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations).

Illustration: A remote pilot is paid to fly medical samples between two clinic rooftops. The clinics are four miles apart and sit on opposite sides of a state line. The pilot plans to drive alongside the route in a van, launching and controlling from the passenger seat to keep the aircraft in sight. Both parts of that plan fail. The flight crosses a state border, and paid property transport must occur wholly within a single State. Controlling the aircraft from a moving vehicle while transporting property for compensation is also prohibited, and neither limit can be waived. The pilot declines the job as planned and instead proposes a shorter in-state route flown from a fixed position within visual line of sight, with aircraft plus payload weighing under 55 pounds.

Common misreading: People assume that because the FAA grants waivers for beyond visual line of sight flying, they can get one for a delivery operation. The chunk says the opposite: for transportation of property, VLOS cannot be waived, and neither can the ban on operating from a moving vehicle or aircraft. A second misreading is treating the under 55 pounds limit as applying to the empty aircraft; it is the maximum total weight including any property being transported.
