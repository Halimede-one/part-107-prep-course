This part of Chapter 9 covers hydration, medications, and alcohol.

Hydration: carry plenty of water and sip it often, whether or not you feel thirsty. The body absorbs water at about 1.2 to 1.5 quarts per hour. The guide recommends drinking one quart per hour in severe heat stress conditions and one pint per hour in moderate heat stress conditions. It points to the chapter's own "Dehydration" section for more.

Drugs: the Federal Aviation Regulations contain no specific list of medications. Instead, Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) prohibits acting as pilot in command (PIC), or in any other capacity as a required pilot flight crewmember, when the person (1) knows or has reason to know of any medical condition that would make them unable to meet the requirement for the medical certificate necessary for the pilot operation, or (2) is taking medication or receiving other treatment for a medical condition that results in their being unable to meet those requirements. On top of that, 14 CFR part 107 and 14 CFR part 91, sections 91.17 and 91.19, prohibit using any drug that affects a person's faculties in any way contrary to safety.

The guide warns that several thousand medications are approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), not counting over-the-counter (OTC) drugs, and that virtually all of them can cause adverse side effects in some people. Herbal and dietary supplements, sport and energy boosters, and other "natural" products can too, because they are often derived from the same substances found in medications. Reactions vary from person to person. The FAA regularly reviews FDA and other data to make sure medications it finds acceptable for aviation duties do not pose an adverse safety risk.

Antihistamines and decongestants are singled out as common OTC drugs that can cause drowsiness and cognitive deficits. Cold and upper respiratory symptoms already sap the desire to fly, and treating them with a sedating drug compounds the problem. Medications containing diphenhydramine (for example, Benadryl) are known to cause drowsiness and have a prolonged half-life, so they stay in the system longer and the side effects last longer.

Before every flight, do a physical self-assessment. The guide offers the IMSAFE mnemonic: Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, Emotion. For the Medication item, ask whether you are taking anything that might affect judgment or cause drowsiness. For any new medication, OTC or prescribed, the guide says to wait at least 48 hours after the first dose before flying, to confirm you have no adverse side effects that would make flying unsafe. It also advises: skip unnecessary or elective medications, eat regular balanced meals, bring a snack, stay well hydrated with plenty of water, sleep adequately the night before, and stay physically fit.

Alcohol: it impairs the body's efficiency, and studies tie drinking to deteriorating performance. A flight involves hundreds of decisions, some time-critical, and alcohol drastically reduces the chance of finishing a flight without incident. Even small amounts can impair judgment, decrease the sense of responsibility, affect coordination, constrict the visual field, diminish memory, reduce reasoning ability, and lower attention span. As little as one ounce of alcohol can slow and weaken muscular reflexes, make eye movements while reading less efficient, and increase error frequency. Vision and hearing impairment can show up after as little as one drink. A hangover still counts as being under the influence: motor and mental impairment persists even when the pilot feels normal. Considerable amounts of alcohol can remain in the body for over 16 hours, so be cautious about flying too soon after drinking.

Illustration: A remote pilot has a July roof-inspection job at 2 p.m. with air temperature well into severe heat stress territory, and she started a new prescription antihistamine that morning for allergies. Running IMSAFE, she stops at M: this is a first dose of a new medication, and the guide says wait at least 48 hours after the first dose before flying to see whether side effects appear. Antihistamines are also on the guide's short list of OTC and common drugs that cause drowsiness and cognitive deficits. She reschedules the flight for later in the week. For the rescheduled flight she loads a cooler with water, planning on about one quart per hour given the severe heat, plus a snack, because dehydration and a skipped meal would degrade her decision-making just as surely as the pill would.

Common misreading: Two things get misread here. First, the 48-hour wait after a first dose is advice in this study guide, not a numbered regulation — the chunk says plainly that the Federal Aviation Regulations include no specific references to medication usage. The actual prohibitions are the ones quoted: no acting as PIC or required pilot flight crewmember with a disqualifying condition or treatment, and no use of any drug that affects your faculties contrary to safety. Second, the medical-certificate wording does not mean the sedating-drug rule is somebody else's problem — the chunk states that part 107 itself, along with 14 CFR 91.17 and 91.19, bars flying under a drug that affects your faculties in any way contrary to safety. And "I slept it off" is not a defense: the chunk says a hangover means you are still under the influence, and considerable amounts of alcohol can remain in the body for over 16 hours.
