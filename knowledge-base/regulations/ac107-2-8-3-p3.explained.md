This part of the advisory circular covers what a remote pilot must check and where a flight may happen when operating a small unmanned aircraft under Category 3 of the operations-over-people rules.

Verification is the pilot's job. Before flying Category 3, the remote pilot must make sure the aircraft is listed on an FAA-accepted DOC (Declaration of Compliance) and is labeled as eligible for Category 3. The AC gives two ways to do that: check online at https://uasdoc.faa.gov to see whether the DOC is valid, and visually inspect the aircraft to confirm a Category 3 label is affixed. These checks are on top of the duties a remote pilot already has under part 107, such as the preflight responsibilities listed in section 107.49 (see that section).

The aircraft side has three things: it must display a label identifying its eligibility for Category 3 operations; it must have current remote pilot operating instructions that apply to its operation, described in paragraph 8.12 of the AC; and it must be subject to a product support and notification process. The applicant (typically the manufacturer) submits the DOC affirming the aircraft meets the safety requirements through an FAA-accepted MOC (means of compliance) — paragraph 8.9 of the AC describes the DOC and submission process.

Where you may fly Category 3 comes in two alternatives. First, the operation is conducted over a closed- or restricted-access site, and everyone inside that site must be on notice that a small unmanned aircraft may fly over them. Second, if the operation is not inside a closed- or restricted-access site, the aircraft must not sustain flight over any person unless that person is directly participating in the operation, or is under a covered structure, or is inside a stationary vehicle that can provide reasonable protection from a falling small unmanned aircraft.

Category 3 operations are never allowed over an open-air assembly of persons. The FAA does not define "open-air assembly" in regulation; it decides case by case, looking at the density of people who are not directly participating and the size of the operational area. The AC describes open-air assemblies as dense gatherings of people in the open, usually in public spaces, and offers possible examples: sporting events, concerts, parades, protests, political rallies, community festivals, or parks and beaches during certain events. Things less likely to count include individual persons or families exiting a shopping center, people playing casual sports in an open area with no spectators, individuals or small groups relaxing in a park or on a beach, and people walking or biking along a bike path. The remote pilot must make this assessment before flying.

Modifications matter too. The remote pilot operating instructions may spell out which modifications are allowed. A modification the instructions do not allow can make the aircraft ineligible for operations over people, would require submitting a new DOC, and may require relabeling the aircraft to reflect the category it is eligible for. If the aircraft is sold, transferred, or used by someone other than the applicant, the applicant must supply remote pilot operating instructions reflecting the aircraft's eligible category and acceptable modifications. The FAA encourages manufacturers to track modifications that would require updating those instructions.

Illustration: A remote pilot is hired to photograph a corporate campus during a weekday. The aircraft came with a Category 3 label on the shell, so she looks it up at https://uasdoc.faa.gov and confirms the DOC is valid, then inspects the aircraft and sees the Category 3 label still affixed. The campus is fenced with badge-controlled gates, and the facilities manager agrees to email all staff that a drone may fly overhead that morning — that makes it a closed-access site where everyone inside is on notice, so she can fly over people there. The next weekend the same client asks for aerial shots of the company picnic held in a public city park, with a few hundred guests spread across the lawn. She declines the overflight: a dense gathering in the open at a park during an event is the kind of thing the FAA describes as an open-air assembly, and Category 3 operations are not allowed over an open-air assembly of persons. She offers instead to fly outside the crowd area, where she can avoid sustained flight over any non-participant.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read "does not sustain flight over any person" as permission to hover briefly over a bystander. The exception list is the point: outside a closed- or restricted-access site, the person has to be directly participating, under a covered structure, or inside a stationary vehicle that gives reasonable protection — not merely overflown quickly. Second, pilots assume a factory Category 3 label settles the question forever. It does not: the DOC has to be valid, the label has to actually be on the aircraft, and an unapproved modification can knock the aircraft out of eligibility until a new DOC is submitted and the aircraft is relabeled.
