This section is the dictionary for Part 107. It tells you what certain words mean everywhere they appear in this part. It also sets a tie-breaker: if a definition here conflicts with a definition in § 1.1 of this chapter, the Part 107 definition wins for purposes of Part 107.

The terms defined here:

- **Control station** — the interface the remote pilot uses to control the flight path of the small unmanned aircraft. That is the thing in your hands (or on your desk) that steers the aircraft.
- **Corrective lenses** — spectacles or contact lenses.
- **Declaration of compliance** — a record submitted to the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) certifying that the small unmanned aircraft meets the Category 2 or Category 3 requirements under subpart D of this part. See subpart D for what those requirements are.
- **Small unmanned aircraft** — an unmanned aircraft weighing less than 55 pounds on takeoff, including everything on board or otherwise attached to the aircraft. The weight is measured at takeoff and it is a total: airframe, battery, camera, payload, mounts, anything strapped on.
- **Small unmanned aircraft system (small UAS)** — the small unmanned aircraft plus its associated elements, including communication links and the components that control the aircraft, that are required for safe and efficient operation in the national airspace system. So "small UAS" is broader than "small unmanned aircraft": it is the whole kit, not just the thing that flies.
- **Unmanned aircraft** — an aircraft operated without the possibility of direct human intervention from within or on the aircraft. The test is whether a human could intervene from inside or on the aircraft, not whether anyone happens to be aboard.
- **Visual observer** — a person the remote pilot in command designates to help the remote pilot in command and the person manipulating the flight controls see and avoid other air traffic or objects aloft or on the ground. A visual observer is not self-appointed; the remote pilot in command designates them.

Illustration: You are preparing a survey flight. The airframe weighs 41 pounds. You add a 9-pound sensor pod, a 2-pound battery pack, and a 1-pound mounting bracket, bolted to the airframe. Total at takeoff: 53 pounds. That is less than 55 pounds, so the aircraft still meets the definition of a small unmanned aircraft and your operation can proceed under Part 107. A colleague then asks you to strap on a 3-pound spare battery as cargo for delivery. That brings takeoff weight to 56 pounds. Because the definition counts everything on board or otherwise attached, the spare battery counts, the aircraft is no longer a small unmanned aircraft, and the flight cannot be conducted under Part 107. You leave the spare battery behind.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read the 55-pound limit as the manufacturer's listed airframe weight. The definition says weight on takeoff including everything on board or otherwise attached, so payload, batteries, mounts, and cargo all count. Second, people use "small unmanned aircraft" and "small unmanned aircraft system" as if they were the same term. The aircraft is the flying machine; the system includes the communication links and control components too. Also note the limit is 'less than 55 pounds,' not '55 pounds or less' — exactly 55 pounds does not qualify.
