This paragraph explains the emergency authority a remote pilot in command (remote PIC) has under part 107. An in-flight emergency is an unexpected, unforeseen serious occurrence or situation that needs urgent, prompt action. When one happens, the remote PIC may deviate from any rule of part 107, but only as far as necessary to respond to that emergency. Two conditions come with that power. First, if the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) asks, the remote PIC must send the FAA a written report explaining the deviation. Second, the emergency action should be taken in a way that minimizes injury or damage to property. The chunk cites § 107.21 as the rule behind this guidance.

Illustration: A remote PIC is flying a survey grid over a vacant lot when the aircraft reports a failing battery and begins to descend on its own. The only clear landing area is on the far side of a two-lane road, and the aircraft will briefly pass over a parked delivery van and out past the edge of the area the pilot had planned to stay within. The pilot steers for the clear area rather than letting the aircraft settle onto the road. That deviation is permitted because it was necessary to respond to the emergency and it was the choice that minimized the chance of injury or property damage. The pilot writes down what happened and why, so that a written report is ready if the FAA asks for one.

Common misreading: Two common misreadings. One is that a written report must always be filed after any emergency deviation — the chunk says the report is required upon the FAA's request. The other is that an emergency unlocks the rules generally; the deviation is allowed only to the extent necessary to respond to that emergency, and the action should still be taken so as to minimize injury or damage to property.
