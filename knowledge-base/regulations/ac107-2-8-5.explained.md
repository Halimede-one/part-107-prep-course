This part of the advisory circular (AC) describes the Means of Compliance, or MOC, which is the test-and-proof method a manufacturer uses to show its small unmanned aircraft is eligible to fly over people. An MOC is a method for showing three things: that the aircraft does not exceed the applicable injury severity limit when it hits a human being, that it has no exposed rotating parts that would lacerate human skin, and that it has no safety defects. The MOC must prove this through test, analysis, or inspection, and it may include consensus standards. Anyone may submit an MOC to the FAA for acceptance if it fulfills Category 2 or Category 3 safety requirements — an individual or a voluntary consensus standards body such as ASTM International or SAE could develop one. The key sequence is that the FAA must accept the MOC first; only then can an applicant rely on it to declare compliance. Once the FAA accepts an MOC, any person submitting a Declaration of Compliance (DOC) can use that accepted MOC to establish that their aircraft meets the rule. An applicant asking the FAA to accept an MOC must submit certain information in a manner the Administrator specifies. In reviewing it, the FAA uses a comprehensive set of criteria: whether the described testing, analysis, or inspection actually demonstrates the aircraft meets the regulatory requirements; whether the MOC addresses injury severity limits, the laceration prohibition, and verification of no safety defects; whether it aligns with accepted methods used by the medical industry, consumer safety groups, or other peer-reviewed test methods; and whether it leans on mitigations that would demand exceptional remote pilot skill or excessive workload. Acceptance is not permanent. An accepted MOC stays under ongoing FAA review, and if the FAA decides it no longer meets any or all of the requirements for demonstrating compliance with part 107 subpart D, the FAA may rescind acceptance and will publish a notice of rescission in the Federal Register. An applicant has two MOC options: the FAA-provided MOC discussed in this AC, or an FAA-accepted MOC developed by a voluntary consensus standards body, another entity, or developed independent of the FAA. An MOC written by a single applicant gets the same level of FAA review as one from a voluntary consensus standards body. Because the FAA generally works with those bodies while the standards are being written, an MOC based on those standards will already have gone through a comprehensive review process during development.

Illustration: A small company has built a 1.2-pound quadcopter and wants to sell it as a Category 2 aircraft for real estate crews shooting over neighborhood open houses. Their engineer drafts a test protocol using drop tests and a padded-guard design, then starts printing marketing material saying the aircraft is Category 2 eligible. The chief pilot stops this. The protocol is their proposed MOC, and under this section the FAA must accept it before anyone can rely on it to declare compliance. The company has two realistic paths: use the FAA-provided MOC described in this AC, or build on a voluntary consensus standards body's work, which will already have been through a comprehensive review during development. They also re-examine one part of their protocol that credits the pilot with manually feathering the props on a failure — the FAA considers whether an MOC relies on mitigations requiring exceptional remote pilot skill or excessive workload, so that credit is a weak spot. They pull the marketing claim until acceptance is in hand.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read "anyone may submit an MOC" as "anyone may use their own method." Submitting is open to all; relying on it is not, because the FAA must accept it first. Second, people treat the MOC and the DOC as the same filing. They are different: the MOC is the accepted method, and the DOC is what a person submits using that accepted method to establish their aircraft meets the rule. Also note acceptance can be rescinded, with notice published in the Federal Register, so an MOC that was good once is not guaranteed good forever.
