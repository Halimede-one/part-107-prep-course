This section is the visual line of sight rule. Paragraph (a) says that the remote pilot in command (the person responsible for the flight), the visual observer if one is used, and the person manipulating the flight controls must be able to see the unmanned aircraft throughout the entire flight, using vision unaided by any device other than corrective lenses. Glasses and contact lenses are fine; binoculars, telephoto views, and the camera feed on your screen do not count as seeing the aircraft. The rule also states why that view is required: to know where the aircraft is, to determine its attitude, altitude, and direction of flight, to watch the airspace for other air traffic or hazards, and to determine that the aircraft does not endanger the life or property of another. Paragraph (b) says who has to actually be exercising that ability at every moment of the flight. It can be the remote pilot in command and the person manipulating the flight controls, or it can be a visual observer. So if a visual observer is maintaining the view, the pilot and control manipulator are not both required to have eyes on the aircraft at that instant — but someone listed must, continuously, for the whole flight.

Illustration: You are flying a roof inspection with a helper acting as visual observer. To frame a shot you want to drop your eyes to the controller screen for several seconds. Before you do, you confirm your observer has the aircraft in sight with the naked eye and is calling out its position. That satisfies paragraph (b)(2), so you can look down briefly. Later the observer walks behind the building to take a phone call. At that moment nobody listed in the rule has the aircraft in unaided sight, so you stop, bring your own eyes back up to the aircraft before continuing, and do not resume screen work until the observer is back in position with the aircraft in view.

Common misreading: People read the visual observer option in paragraph (b) as permission to fly beyond where anyone can see the aircraft as long as an observer is 'assigned.' The observer substitutes for whose eyes are on the aircraft, not for the requirement that the aircraft be seen. People also treat the first-person video feed or binoculars as satisfying paragraph (a); the text limits the aid to corrective lenses only.
