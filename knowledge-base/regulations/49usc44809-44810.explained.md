This is a direction Congress gave to the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), not a rule that a remote pilot has to follow. Two things are required. First, under subsection (a), the FAA Administrator must work with the Secretary of Defense, the Secretary of Homeland Security, and the heads of other relevant Federal departments and agencies. The goal of that coordination is to make sure that technologies or systems these agencies develop, test, or deploy to detect and mitigate risks from errant or hostile unmanned aircraft system (UAS) operations do not adversely impact or interfere with safe airport operations, navigation, air traffic services, or the safe and efficient operation of the national airspace system. In other words, counter-drone equipment must not itself become a hazard to aviation. Second, under subsection (b), the Administrator must develop a plan for certifying, permitting, authorizing, or allowing deployment of detection and mitigation technologies, and that plan must provide for policies, procedures, or protocols letting appropriate FAA officials use those technologies to detect and mitigate potential airspace safety risks from UAS operations. The Administrator must also charter an aviation rulemaking committee to recommend the plan and any standards the Administrator decides may be needed for those technologies. Illustration: A remote pilot is hired to inspect a rooftop two miles from a large airport and hears that a Federal agency has installed drone-detection equipment nearby. She wonders whether this section gives her a new preflight step. It does not. Nothing in this chunk tells a remote pilot to do or avoid anything. The obligations run to the FAA Administrator: coordinate with the other agencies so their detection and mitigation systems do not interfere with airport operations or air traffic services, and build a plan plus an aviation rulemaking committee for approving such systems. Her own operating requirements come from the operating rules, not from this section.

Common misreading: People read "detect and mitigate" and assume this section authorizes someone to jam or shoot down their drone, or that it creates a drone-pilot duty. Read as written, the chunk only tells the FAA Administrator to coordinate with other agencies, develop a plan for certifying or authorizing such technologies, and charter a rulemaking committee. What any agency may actually do to an aircraft is not stated in this chunk.
