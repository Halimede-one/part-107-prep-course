This part of the study guide walks through the categories of special use airspace and then lists "other airspace areas." Prohibited areas are blocks of airspace with defined dimensions where flight is simply not allowed, set up for security or other reasons tied to the national welfare. They are published in the Federal Register, drawn on aeronautical charts, and labeled with a "P" and a number, such as P-40. The guide names Camp David and the National Mall in Washington, D.C. (site of the White House and Congressional buildings) as examples. Restricted areas are places where activity is hazardous to aircraft that are not part of it — artillery firing, aerial gunnery, guided missiles are the examples given. Flight there is not banned outright but is subject to restrictions, and entering without permission from the using or controlling agency may be extremely hazardous. The chunk explains how Air Traffic Control (ATC) handles these: if the restricted area is not active and has been released to the FAA, ATC lets aircraft operate in it without a specific clearance; if it is active and has not been released, ATC issues a clearance that keeps the aircraft out. Restricted areas are charted with an "R" and a number, such as R-4401, shown on the en route chart appropriate for the altitude or flight level being flown, with details on the back of the chart. Warning areas are like restricted areas except the United States does not have sole jurisdiction. A warning area starts 3 NM (nautical miles) out from the U.S. coast, may lie over domestic or international waters or both, and exists to warn nonparticipating pilots of potential danger. They are labeled with a "W" and a number, such as W-237. Military Operation Areas (MOAs) have defined vertical and lateral limits and separate certain military training from Instrument Flight Rules (IFR) traffic. When an MOA is in use, nonparticipating IFR traffic may be cleared through if ATC can provide IFR separation; otherwise ATC reroutes or restricts it. MOAs appear on sectional, VFR (Visual Flight Rules) terminal area, and en route low altitude charts, and carry names rather than numbers, such as "Camden Ridge MOA." The back of the sectional gives times of operation, altitudes affected, and the controlling agency. Alert areas are charted with an "A" and a number, such as A-211, and flag areas with a high volume of pilot training or an unusual type of aerial activity. Pilots should use caution there. All activity in an alert area must follow regulations without waiver, and participating pilots and pilots transiting the area share collision avoidance responsibility equally. Controlled Firing Areas (CFAs) hold activities that would be hazardous if not controlled, but the activity must stop when a spotter aircraft, radar, or ground lookout sees an aircraft that might be approaching. Because of that, CFAs never force another aircraft to change course, so they are not charted. Finally, the guide lists "other airspace areas" as a catch-all for most remaining airspace: local airport advisory (LAA), military training route (MTR), temporary flight restriction (TFR), parachute jump aircraft operations, published VFR routes, terminal radar service area (TRSA), national security area (NSA), Air Defense Identification Zones (ADIZ) both land and water based along with the need for a Defense VFR (DVFR) flight plan to operate VFR in that airspace, and Flight Restricted Zones (FRZ) near the Capitol and the White House. The figure captions in this chunk point to chart images that are not reproduced here, so you will want to look at a real sectional chart to see how each symbol appears.

Illustration: You are hired to photograph a hilltop estate in rural Maryland. Planning the flight, you pull up the sectional chart and see the area sits inside a blue hatched boundary labeled P-40. The chunk tells you what that label means: a prohibited area, where the flight of aircraft is prohibited, established for security reasons, and the guide names Camp David as the example. You do not launch. The reason is that the "P" designation is not a caution or a call-for-permission area the way a restricted area is — flight there is prohibited. A week later a different client wants footage near a boundary marked R-4401. That one is a restricted area, where flight is not wholly prohibited but is subject to restrictions, and the guide says penetration without authorization from the using or controlling agency may be extremely hazardous. So the next step is to find the using or controlling agency, which the chunk says is identified on the back of the chart, rather than treating the hatched line as either automatically closed or automatically open.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people assume every one of these areas carries the same consequence. The chunk draws real distinctions: prohibited means flight is prohibited, restricted means flight is subject to restrictions and unauthorized entry may be extremely hazardous, warning and alert areas exist to warn and tell you to use caution, and CFAs are not even charted. Second, the ATC clearance language under restricted areas and the MOA discussion are written about IFR traffic receiving clearances — that is background on how the airspace works, not a statement in this chunk about what a remote pilot must obtain. Also, 3 NM in the warning area paragraph is where the warning area begins outward from the coast; it is not a standoff distance for small unmanned aircraft.
