This section covers a narrow case: a corporation that is not a U.S. citizen but wants to register an aircraft in the United States under 49 U.S.C. 44102. Such a corporation files the Aircraft Registration Application, AC Form 8050-1, and must send four extra things with it: a certified copy of its certificate of incorporation, a certification that it is lawfully qualified to do business in one or more States, a certification that the aircraft will be based and primarily used in the United States, and the location where it will keep the records paragraph (e) requires.

The rest of the section defines and polices that "based and primarily used in the United States" promise. The test is 60 percent: at least 60 percent of the aircraft's total flight hours must be accumulated within the United States. The measuring window is the remainder of the registration month plus the next 6 calendar months, and then each 6 calendar month period after that. Paragraph (c) adds a definition that works in the registrant's favor: hours on a non-stop flight between two points in the United States count as hours within the United States even if the aircraft is physically outside the country for part of the flight, and stops in emergencies or for refueling do not destroy the "non-stop" character. Paragraph (d) says any stretch of time when the aircraft was not validly registered in the United States is simply left out of the math.

Two ongoing duties follow. Under (e), the corporation keeps records of the aircraft's total flight hours in the United States and makes them available to the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) on request, for three calendar years after the year the hours were flown. Under (f), at the end of each reporting period the corporation sends the Registry either a signed report giving the total time in service of the airframe as provided in § 91.417(a)(2)(i) for that period plus the total flight hours in the United States for that period, or a signed statement that all of the aircraft's flight hours while registered in the United States during that period were exclusively within the United States.

One gap to flag: paragraph (f) points to "paragraphs (b)(1) and (2) of this section," but paragraph (b) as printed here has no numbered subparagraphs. Read it as the periods described in (b) — the remainder of the registration month plus 6 calendar months, and each 6 calendar month period after.

Illustration: A corporation organized outside the United States registers an aircraft under 49 U.S.C. 44102 and files AC Form 8050-1 in March, attaching a certified copy of its certificate of incorporation, a certification that it is lawfully qualified to do business in a State, a certification that the aircraft will be based and primarily used in the United States, and the address of its Denver office where the flight-hour records will be kept. Its first measuring window runs from the rest of March through the end of September. During that window the aircraft logs 200 total flight hours. Of those, 130 hours are on domestic legs, and 20 more hours are on a non-stop Seattle-to-Boston flight that clipped Canadian airspace and made one refueling stop. Those 20 hours still count as hours within the United States under paragraph (c), because the flight was between two U.S. points and the only stop was for fuel. That puts the aircraft at 150 of 200 hours, or 75 percent, above the 60 percent floor. At the end of September the corporation sends the Registry a signed report with the airframe's total time in service for the period as provided in § 91.417(a)(2)(i) and the 150 U.S. hours, and it files the underlying records in Denver, where they must stay available to the FAA for three calendar years after the year the hours were flown.

Common misreading: People assume any time the aircraft leaves U.S. airspace is automatically lost from the 60 percent calculation, and that a stop kills the credit. Paragraph (c) says otherwise: a non-stop flight between two U.S. points counts in full even if part of it is outside the United States, and stops in emergencies or for refueling are excepted from the non-stop requirement. The flip side is also misread — paragraph (c) only protects flights between two U.S. points, so a leg from a U.S. airport to a foreign destination does not qualify no matter how brief. And paragraph (d) is a removal, not a credit: unregistered periods are disregarded, which means those hours drop out of both sides of the ratio rather than counting as U.S. hours.
