This part of the chunk finishes the walk-through of how to read a Terminal Aerodrome Forecast (TAF), then introduces the Convective SIGMET.

TAF elements covered here:

- Valid period: when the end of the period falls at midnight Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), it is written as 24 rather than 00. A 00Z TAF issued on the 9th and valid 24 hours reads 0900/0924.
- Forecast wind: a five-digit group plus "KT." In 15011KT the first three digits are the direction in reference to true north and the last two are the speed in knots. As with a METAR (aviation routine weather report), winds above 99 knots take three digits for speed.
- Forecast visibility: statute miles, whole numbers or fractions. More than six miles is coded P6SM.
- Forecast significant weather: coded the same way as in a METAR.
- Forecast sky condition: same format as a METAR, with one difference — only cumulonimbus (CB) clouds are forecast in a TAF, while a METAR reports both CB and towering cumulus.
- Forecast change group: significant changes during the valid period are flagged as FM (from) or TEMPO (temporary). FM marks a rapid and significant change, usually within an hour. TEMPO marks temporary fluctuations expected to last less than 1 hour.
- PROB30: a stated percentage probability of thunderstorms and precipitation in the coming hours. It is not used for the first 6 hours of the 24-hour forecast.

The KPIR example shows all of this strung together, with the plain-English translation underneath, ending in the = sign that closes the report.

Convective Significant Meteorological Information (Convective SIGMET, coded WST) is a separate product. It is issued for severe thunderstorms with surface winds greater than 50 knots, surface hail greater than or equal to ¾ inch in diameter, or tornadoes. It is also issued to warn of embedded thunderstorms, lines of thunderstorms, or thunderstorms with heavy or greater precipitation covering 40 percent or more of a region of 3,000 square miles or greater. The chunk says a remote pilot will find these alerts helpful for flight planning.

(Note: the plain-English explanation under the KPIR example describes an initial wind of 150° at 12 knots and broken clouds at 9,000 feet, but those groups do not appear in the coded text as printed here. Read the coded example and the translation as illustrating the format, not as a line-by-line match.)

Illustration: You plan an aerial survey at Pierre, South Dakota, launching around 1300Z on the 11th. You pull the TAF above. The line TEMPO 1112/1114 5SM BR tells you that between 1200Z and 1400Z there may be temporary periods of 5 statute miles visibility in mist — and TEMPO means each such period is expected to last less than 1 hour. Later, PROB30 1200/1204 3SM TSRA BKN030CB tells you that between 0000Z and 0400Z there is a 30 percent chance of 3 statute miles visibility with thunderstorms and moderate rain and broken cumulonimbus at 3,000 feet. Your 1300Z window sits in the mist period, not the thunderstorm window, so you keep the flight but brief your visual observer that visibility may drop to 5 SM for short stretches and set a hard abort if you lose sight of the aircraft. You also check for a Convective SIGMET before launch, because a thunderstorm line strong enough to trigger one would end the flight regardless of what the TAF said.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read TEMPO and FM as interchangeable. They are not: FM marks a lasting change from that time onward, usually arriving within an hour, while TEMPO marks brief fluctuations expected to last less than 1 hour. Second, people assume a clean TAF means no convective threat. PROB30 is not used in the first 6 hours of the 24-hour forecast, so a thunderstorm developing in the next few hours may not show as a probability group at all — which is why the Convective SIGMET is a separate check.
