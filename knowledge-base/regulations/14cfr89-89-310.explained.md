This section lists the minimum performance requirements that a standard remote identification unmanned aircraft must meet. These are design and production requirements, so they fall mainly on the manufacturer, but they shape how the aircraft behaves for the person flying it.

The requirements are:

(a) Control station location — the aircraft must generate and encode the control station location into the message elements, and that location must correspond to where the person manipulating the flight controls is.

(b) Time mark — the time mark message element must be synchronized with all the other remote identification message elements.

(c) Self-testing and monitoring — before takeoff the aircraft must automatically test its remote identification function and tell the person manipulating the flight controls the result. It must not be able to take off if the remote identification equipment is not functional. From takeoff to shutdown it must continuously monitor that function and notify the person at the controls of any malfunction or failure.

(d) Tamper resistance — it must be designed and produced in a way that reduces a person's ability to tamper with the remote identification function.

(e) Error correction — the equipment must build error correction into the broadcast of the message elements in § 89.305 (see that section for what those elements are).

(f) Interference — the remote identification equipment must not interfere with other systems or equipment on the aircraft, and those other systems must not interfere with it. The requirement runs both ways.

(g) Message broadcast — the aircraft must be able to broadcast the § 89.305 message elements using a non-proprietary broadcast specification, on radio frequency spectrum compatible with personal wireless devices under 47 CFR part 15, where operations may occur without an individual license from the Federal Communications Commission (FCC). Any broadcasting device used to meet this section must be integrated into the aircraft without modifying its authorized radio frequency parameters, and must be designed to maximize the range at which the broadcast can be received, while complying with 47 CFR part 15 and any other applicable laws in effect as of the date the declaration of compliance is submitted to the FAA for acceptance.

(h) Message element accuracy —
• Reported geometric position of both the aircraft and the control station: accurate to within 100 feet of true position, with 95 percent probability.
• Reported geometric altitude of the control station: within 15 feet of true geometric altitude, 95 percent probability.
• Reported geometric altitude of the aircraft: within 150 feet of true geometric altitude, 95 percent probability.
• Latitude, longitude, and geometric altitude of the aircraft and its control station must be broadcast no later than 1.0 seconds from the time of measurement to the time of broadcast.
• Message elements must be broadcast at a rate of at least 1 message per second.

(i) Take-off limitation — the aircraft must not be able to take off unless it is broadcasting the message elements in § 89.305.

Illustration: A remote pilot powers up a standard remote identification aircraft for a roof inspection. During the startup sequence the controller shows a remote ID self-test notice, and this time it reports a failure. The aircraft will not arm for takeoff. That is the design working as this section requires: paragraph (c)(2) says the aircraft must not be able to take off if the remote identification equipment is not functional, and paragraph (i) says it must not take off unless it is actually broadcasting the § 89.305 message elements. The pilot does not look for a workaround. The job waits until the aircraft passes its self-test. Later in a different flight, the controller flashes a remote ID malfunction warning mid-mission. That notification is the continuous monitoring required by (c)(3) doing its job, and it tells the pilot the broadcast has stopped being reliable.

Common misreading: Two common misreadings. First, people read the accuracy numbers in (h) as flight limits — they are not. The 100 feet, 15 feet, and 150 feet figures are tolerances on how accurately the equipment must report position and altitude; they say nothing about how high or how far you may fly. Second, people assume these requirements are something a remote pilot has to verify or configure. They are performance requirements the aircraft must be built to meet, declared through the manufacturer's declaration of compliance. The pilot's practical involvement is noticing the self-test result and the malfunction notifications the design is required to provide, and not flying when the aircraft tells them remote ID is not working.
