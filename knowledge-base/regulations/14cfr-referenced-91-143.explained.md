This section creates a no-fly area around space flight operations. When the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) issues a Notice to Airmen (NOTAM) under this section, nobody may operate an aircraft of U.S. registry inside the area that NOTAM designates, and nobody may pilot any aircraft using an airman certificate issued by the FAA inside that area. The only way in is authorization from Air Traffic Control (ATC). Note the two separate hooks: the aircraft being U.S. registered, or the pilot flying on an FAA certificate. Part 107 incorporates this section, so it reaches remote pilots flying small unmanned aircraft. The practical duty is a preflight one: check for NOTAMs covering your area before you fly, and if a space flight operations NOTAM covers it, stay out unless ATC authorizes you.

Illustration: A remote pilot in Florida has a paid roof inspection scheduled near the coast. During preflight planning he checks NOTAMs and finds one designating an area for space flight operations that includes the inspection site, active during his planned window. He does not launch. He either reschedules outside the NOTAM's effective period or contacts ATC to request authorization to operate inside the designated area. Flying the inspection without that authorization would violate this section, because the NOTAM area is closed to his aircraft and to him as an FAA-certificated pilot.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people assume this is only about big rockets and crewed aircraft at altitude, so a small drone at 100 feet is somehow outside it. The rule says 'any aircraft' within the designated areas, and part 107 incorporates it, so a small unmanned aircraft is covered. Second, people confuse this with airspace authorization through the usual drone channels. The exception written here is authorization by ATC, so the fix is an ATC authorization for the designated area, not a general assumption that Class G airspace is always open.
