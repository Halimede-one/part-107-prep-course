This chunk introduces aeronautical decision-making (ADM) and risk management, two subjects the FAA says it is required to test an applicant about for issuance of a small unmanned aircraft systems (sUAS) certificate. ADM is defined as a systematic approach to the mental process pilots use to consistently pick the best course of action for a given set of circumstances, based on the latest information the pilot has. The chunk's case for why it matters: accidents still happen despite better training, equipment, and services, because the human factor still produces errors. It estimates roughly 80 percent of all aviation accidents are related to human factors, with the vast majority of those occurring during landing (24.1 percent) and takeoff (23.4 percent). Historically, good pilot judgment has been recognized as critical for over 25 years. The airline industry built the first ADM-based training programs, and crew resource management (CRM) training teaches flight crews to use all available resources — human resources, hardware, and information — to support ADM. FAA research led to regulations requiring decision-making be taught in pilot training, and culminated in 1987 with six manuals aimed at different pilot ratings. Independent studies found pilots who got ADM training made about 10 to 50 percent fewer judgment errors, a statistically significant difference, and one operator flying about 400,000 hours annually showed a 54 percent reduction in accident rate after using the materials for recurrency training. The chunk's central claim is that, contrary to popular opinion, good judgment can be taught — it is not just a by-product of logging hours. It lists six steps for good decision-making: (1) identifying personal attitudes hazardous to safe flight, (2) learning behavior modification techniques, (3) learning how to recognize and cope with stress, (4) developing risk assessment skills, (5) using all resources, and (6) evaluating the effectiveness of one's own ADM skills. Risk management is described as an important component of ADM, with the goal of proactively identifying safety-related hazards and mitigating the associated risks; good decision-making practices reduce or even eliminate the inherent risk in a flight. The chunk says the formal risk management decision-making process involves six steps shown in Figure 10-1, but the figure itself is not reproduced in this text, so those six steps are not listed here. Finally, the chunk uses seat belts as an analogy for how the ability to make good decisions is built on direct or indirect experience plus education: a driver may learn the value of a seat belt directly, by being injured in a crash, or indirectly, by watching a loved one be injured for lack of one.

Illustration: A remote pilot is set to film a construction site at 7 a.m. He slept badly, the client is waiting, and a layer of fog is sitting over the site. His instinct is that he has flown this site a dozen times and can handle it. Applying the ADM steps from this chunk, he stops at step 1 and names the personal attitude at work — the urge to press on because the client is watching — and at step 3 he recognizes fatigue as a stressor. He then applies risk management: the hazard is loss of visual line of sight in fog combined with a tired pilot, so he delays the launch two hours until the fog lifts and he has had coffee and a walk-through. His reason is that the flight's risk came from the human factor, not the aircraft, and the chunk's own statistics point at human factors as the dominant accident cause, with takeoff and landing as the highest-risk phases.

Common misreading: The common misreading is that experience alone produces good judgment — that logging accident-free hours automatically makes a pilot a better decision-maker. The chunk says the opposite: that was the traditional assumption, and it is contrary to what research found. Good judgment can be taught, and ADM is a structured process to be learned and practiced, not a reward for time in the air. A second misreading is treating the 80 percent human-factors figure and the landing and takeoff percentages as rules to memorize for their own sake; in the chunk they are evidence for why ADM training exists, not operating limits.
