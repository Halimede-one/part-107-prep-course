This paragraph explains what § 107.37 asks of the remote pilot in command (remote PIC) — the person directly responsible for and the final authority over the flight. The small unmanned aircraft must stay clear of all other aircraft and yield to them. The FAA calls this the traditional "see and avoid" duty. To meet it, the chunk says the remote PIC has to know where the small unmanned aircraft is and where it is heading at all times, stay aware of other aircraft, persons, and property near the operating area, and maneuver the aircraft to avoid a collision. The last sentence sets the bar higher than simply not hitting anything: the remote PIC must act so that the other aircraft never has to maneuver to avoid the drone. The burden of getting out of the way sits entirely with the drone.

Illustration: You are flying a mapping grid at 300 feet over a rural parcel and hear, then see, a crop duster working the next field over. It is well clear right now, but its turns bring it toward your airspace. Rather than hold position and watch, you descend and land immediately, before the pilot of the crop duster would have any reason to alter course. The reason: the chunk requires you to take action so other aircraft will not need to maneuver to avoid you — so the moment a conflict looks possible, you move, not them.

Common misreading: People read "remains clear of and yields to" as meaning they only have to break off once a collision looks likely. The chunk is stricter: if the manned aircraft had to change anything to stay clear of your drone, you have already failed the standard, even if nothing came close to touching. It is also not enough to merely see the other aircraft — the paragraph requires continuous knowledge of your own aircraft's location and flightpath as part of the same duty.
