This paragraph explains the Federal Aviation Administration's (FAA) view of careless or reckless operation under 14 CFR § 107.23. The same prohibition that applies to manned aircraft pilots applies to the remote pilot in command (remote PIC) of a small unmanned aircraft system (small UAS). The FAA's added point is that small UAS have operating considerations manned aircraft do not, so conduct that would not even come up in a manned flight can still be careless or reckless when done with a small UAS. The example the FAA gives is failing to account for weather conditions near structures, trees, or rolling terrain while operating in a densely populated area. Note that this is advisory material describing how the FAA reads the rule; the rule itself is in that section.

Illustration: A remote pilot is hired to photograph a rooftop HVAC unit on an eight-story office building downtown. Winds are reported at 12 knots, well within the aircraft's capability on paper. But the pilot knows that wind funneling between tall buildings and rolling off the roof edge creates turbulence and sudden downdrafts that the reported figure does not capture, and the sidewalks below are full of pedestrians. The decision is to reschedule for a calmer morning rather than launch. The reason: under the FAA's reading, pressing on without accounting for how weather behaves around those structures in a densely populated area is exactly the kind of added consideration that can make an operation careless or reckless, even though the raw wind number looked acceptable.

Common misreading: Pilots sometimes assume that if no accident happened and no specific numeric limit in Part 107 was exceeded, the operation cannot be careless or reckless. This paragraph says the opposite: careless or reckless operation is judged on the conduct itself, and the FAA treats small UAS as having extra considerations beyond the ones that apply to manned flight, so an operation can be careless even where no other rule was broken.
