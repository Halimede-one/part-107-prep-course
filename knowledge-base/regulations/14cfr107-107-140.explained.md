This section sets out Category 4, the fourth way a small unmanned aircraft may be flown over human beings. Category 4 is the only category in this group built around a traditional airworthiness certificate rather than a manufacturer's declared compliance with an impact or injury standard.

For the remote pilot in command (the person directly responsible for and in final authority over the flight), two things are required: the aircraft must be eligible for Category 4 under paragraph (b), and the aircraft must be flown within all operating limitations that apply to it as specified by the Administrator. Separately, no remote pilot in command may fly in sustained flight over open-air assemblies of human beings unless the operation meets the requirements of either § 89.110 or § 89.115(a) — see those sections for what they require.

To be eligible, the aircraft must have an airworthiness certificate issued under part 21. It must be operated within the operating limitations in the approved Flight Manual or as otherwise specified by the Administrator, and those limitations must not prohibit operations over human beings. And its maintenance, preventive maintenance, alterations, and inspections must be done per paragraph (c)(1).

Paragraph (c) puts the maintenance burden on the owner, unless the owner has an agreement with an operator to carry it out, in which case the operator must. Whoever holds it must ensure the person doing the work uses the methods, techniques, and practices in the manufacturer's current maintenance manual or Instructions for Continued Airworthiness acceptable to the Administrator (or other methods acceptable to the Administrator); has the knowledge, skill, and appropriate equipment; inspects per the manufacturer's instructions or other instructions acceptable to the Administrator; and uses parts of such quality that the aircraft ends up at least equal to its original or properly altered condition.

Records: all maintenance, preventive maintenance, and alteration records must be kept and documented in a manner acceptable to the Administrator, each entry showing the description of the work, the date it was completed, and the name of the person who did it. Records must also track the status of life-limited parts, the inspection status of the aircraft, and the status of applicable airworthiness directives — including method of compliance, the airworthiness directive number, and the revision date, plus the time and date of the next required action if the directive recurs.

Retention splits two ways. Work records are kept for 1 year from when the work is completed, or until that maintenance is repeated or superseded by other work. Life-limited parts status, airworthiness directive compliance, and inspection status records are kept and transferred with the aircraft when ownership changes. All of these records must be available for inspection on request from the Administrator or any authorized representative of the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB).

Finally, paragraph (d) offers a shortcut: complying with part 43 and part 91, subpart E, satisfies the requirements of (b)(3) and (c).

Illustration: A survey company buys a larger unmanned aircraft that holds an airworthiness certificate issued under part 21, and its approved Flight Manual does not prohibit flight over people. The company signs an agreement with the aircraft's owner-lessor making the company, as operator, responsible for the maintenance requirements in paragraph (c). The remote pilot in command is asked to fly a traffic study over a city street with pedestrians below — not an open-air assembly — and keeps the flight inside every operating limitation in the Flight Manual. That is a legal Category 4 operation. Two weeks later the same pilot is asked to overfly a packed outdoor concert for sustained periods. The pilot declines until the company confirms the operation meets § 89.110 or § 89.115(a), because sustained flight over an open-air assembly is barred without that. Meanwhile the company logs a rotor-arm replacement with the description of work, the completion date, and the technician's name, and keeps it on file for 1 year; it also keeps the airworthiness directive and life-limited-part records in a file marked to transfer with the aircraft if it is ever sold.

Common misreading: People read "airworthiness certificate" as the only hurdle and assume a certificated aircraft may be flown over anyone, anywhere. It cannot. The Flight Manual limitations must not prohibit operations over human beings, the flight must stay within those limitations, and sustained flight over an open-air assembly still needs § 89.110 or § 89.115(a). A second misreading is treating paragraph (c) as the pilot's job — it falls on the owner, or on the operator if the owner has an agreement shifting it. A third is thinking the 1-year retention covers everything; life-limited parts status, airworthiness directive compliance, and inspection status records are not discarded after a year, they follow the aircraft to its next owner.
