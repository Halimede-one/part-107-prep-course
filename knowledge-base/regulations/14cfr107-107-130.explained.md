This section sets out what makes a small unmanned aircraft eligible for Category 3 operations, and what the person applying for the declaration of compliance has to do. Most of it is aimed at the manufacturer or modifier — the applicant — not at the remote pilot, though the pilot is the one who ends up relying on the label and the instructions.

Paragraph (a) is the design test. The aircraft must be designed, produced, or modified so that three things are true. First, it will not cause injury to a human being equivalent to or greater than the severity of the injury caused by a transfer of 25 foot-pounds of kinetic energy on impact from a rigid object. Second, it has no exposed rotating parts that would lacerate human skin on impact with a person. Third, it contains no safety defects.

Paragraph (b) adds three obligations on the applicant for the declaration of compliance. (1) A label on the aircraft showing it is eligible for Category 3 operations. The label must be in English, legible, prominent, and permanently affixed. (2) Remote pilot operating instructions for the system, which the applicant must make available when the aircraft is sold or transferred, or when someone other than the applicant who filed the declaration of compliance under § 107.160 uses it. At a minimum those instructions must cover a system description (required components, any system limitations, and the declared category or categories of operation), which modifications will not change the system's ability to meet the requirements for the category or categories it is eligible to conduct, and how to verify and change the mode or configuration if those are variable. (3) A product support and notification process, so the applicant can notify both the public and the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) of any defect or condition that causes the aircraft to no longer meet the requirements of this subpart, and of any identified safety defect that causes the aircraft to exceed a low probability of fatality.

The declaration of compliance process itself is in § 107.160 — see that section.

Illustration: You buy a used small unmanned aircraft from another operator who advertised it as "approved for flying over people." When it arrives, you look for the label described in paragraph (b)(1): an English-language, legible, prominent, permanently affixed label indicating eligibility for Category 3 operations. There is a peel-off sticker on the battery door, and nothing on the airframe. You also ask the seller for the remote pilot operating instructions, since paragraph (b)(2) says the applicant must make those available on sale or transfer; the seller says he threw away the paperwork. Reading the instructions matters here because paragraph (b)(2)(iii) contemplates aircraft whose mode or configuration is variable — you would have no way to verify which mode the aircraft is in. Decision: you do not treat this aircraft as Category 3 eligible until you can confirm a properly affixed label and obtain the operating instructions from the applicant who filed the declaration of compliance, because the eligibility this section describes rests on those items being in place, not on the seller's description.

Common misreading: People read § 107.130 as the rule that authorizes them to fly over people and assume that once the drone has a Category 3 label, they are done. This section only covers eligibility of the aircraft and the applicant's duties — the label, the instructions, and the product support and notification process. It says nothing about how or where a Category 3 operation may be conducted; those operating conditions are elsewhere in the part. A second misreading is treating the 25 foot-pounds figure as a measured impact energy limit for your flight. The chunk states it as an injury-severity benchmark the aircraft's design must stay below — it is a design standard the applicant must meet, not something a remote pilot calculates before takeoff.
