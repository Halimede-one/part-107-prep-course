This section covers a narrow corporate ownership arrangement. A "voting trust" is a legal device where shareholders hand their voting rights to an independent trustee. A domestic corporation can use one to qualify as a U.S. citizen for aircraft registration purposes. If a corporation goes that route, paragraph (a) says it must send the Registry two things: a true copy of the fully executed voting trust agreement, which must identify each voting interest of the applicant and must be binding on each voting trustee, the applicant corporation, all foreign stockholders, and every other party to the transaction; and an affidavit from each person named as a voting trustee. In that affidavit each trustee must represent four things: that the trustee is a citizen of the United States within the meaning of 49 U.S.C. 40102(a)(15); that the trustee is not a past, present, or prospective director, officer, employee, attorney, or agent of any other party to the trust agreement; that the trustee is not a present or prospective beneficiary, creditor, debtor, supplier, or contractor of any other party to the agreement; and that the trustee is not aware of any reason, situation, or relationship under which another party might influence the trustee's totally independent judgment under the agreement. The common thread is independence — the trustee must have no ties that could bend their vote. Paragraph (b) requires the agreement to spell out how a trustee is replaced if one dies, becomes disabled, resigns, loses citizenship, or any other event leads to replacement; the replacement trustee must immediately submit the same affidavit to the Registry. Paragraph (c) covers what happens if the trust ends or is changed and the result is that citizens of the United States control less than 75 percent of the voting interest in the corporation: that counts as a loss of citizenship by the holder of the Certificate of Aircraft Registration, and § 47.41(a)(3) applies — see that section for the consequence. Paragraph (d) is a one-line prohibition: the agreement may not let a trustee act through a proxy. The trustee must vote personally.

Illustration: A drone services company is incorporated in Delaware but a majority of its shares are held by investors abroad. To register its aircraft, the company sets up a voting trust and names a trustee. The first candidate is the law firm partner who drafted the investors' shareholder agreement. Counsel rejects the candidate: paragraph (a)(2)(ii) requires the trustee to swear they are not a past, present, or prospective attorney of any other party to the trust agreement, and this person cannot truthfully make that representation. The company selects an unaffiliated trustee instead, files the fully executed agreement and the trustee's affidavit with the Registry, and includes a succession clause as paragraph (b) requires. Two years later the foreign investors buy out enough domestic shareholders that U.S. citizen control of the voting interest drops below 75 percent after the trust is modified. Under paragraph (c) that is a loss of citizenship for the registration certificate holder, and the company must look to § 47.41(a)(3).

Common misreading: People assume the trustee only has to be a U.S. citizen, and that any willing citizen will do. Citizenship is just the first of four representations. The other three rule out anyone with a role, a business relationship, or even an awareness of a relationship that could sway their judgment — so the company's own lawyer, accountant, supplier, or an officer of an investor entity is disqualified even though they are citizens. A second slip is treating the affidavit as a one-time filing: a replacement trustee under paragraph (b) must immediately submit the same affidavit.
