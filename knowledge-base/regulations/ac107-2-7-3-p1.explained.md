This is the advisory circular's guidance on the preflight inspection that section 107.49 requires. Beyond sizing up the area of operation and planning the flight (described in paragraph 5.10, which you would need to read separately), the remote pilot in command (remote PIC) must inspect the small unmanned aircraft system (small UAS) before each flight to confirm it is in a condition for safe operation. That means looking for equipment damage or malfunctions. The chunk says the inspection should follow the manufacturer's inspection procedures when those exist — usually in the owner's or maintenance manual — and/or a procedure the owner or operator develops. Owners may optionally build their own inspection program, drawing on details found in various industry programs. The preflight check inside such a program should be scalable to the particular aircraft, program, and operation, and should cover the entire system. As another option and best practice, an operator may follow portions of Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations (14 CFR) part 43 appendix D. The chunk is candid that appendix D is technically a maintenance inspection checklist, not a preflight checklist, but it gives a logical, systematic order — airframe, then flight controls, then batteries, then engine, and so on. Most small UAS items are visible without disassembly, unlike manned aircraft. Appendix D is not comprehensive: it does not address UAS-specific features such as datalinks or support equipment, so an operator using it as a template must identify the uncovered items and add them. Finally, even where the manufacturer supplies a written procedure, the FAA recommends the remote PIC fold in the 23 listed items as a visual or functional check: visual condition of components; airframe structure including undercarriage, flight control surfaces, and linkages; registration markings for proper display and legibility (part 48, § 48.205); moveable control surfaces and their airframe attachment points; servo motors and their attachment points; the propulsion system including powerplants, propellers, rotors, and ducted fans; fuel for correct type and quantity; secure attachment of equipment such as a camera; control link connectivity between aircraft and control station (CS); communication with the aircraft and acquisition of Global Positioning System (GPS) location from the minimum number of satellites the manufacturer specifies; adequate power supply and proper function of all systems; correct avionics indications including control link transceiver, communication and navigation equipment, and antennas; the display panel if used; ground support equipment including takeoff and landing systems; adequate CS-to-aircraft communication and GPS satellite acquisition; correct control surface movement commanded from the CS; the flight termination system if applicable; a functioning anti-collision light if operating during civil twilight and night; compass calibration prior to any flight; controller operation for heading and altitude; starting the propellers to check for imbalance or irregular operation; a controlled low-altitude flight within range of any interference to recheck controls and stability; and battery levels for both the aircraft and the CS.

Illustration: You bought a used quadcopter and the seller never passed along the manual, and the manufacturer's website no longer hosts it. You still have to meet § 107.49's requirement to inspect before each flight, so you build your own written checklist. You use part 43 appendix D's structure as a skeleton — airframe first, then flight controls, then batteries, then the propulsion system — and then, because the AC warns appendix D misses UAS-specific items, you add lines for control link connectivity to the control station, GPS satellite acquisition, camera mount security, CS battery level, and anti-collision light function for your twilight jobs. On the day of a twilight roof survey you run the list, and item by item everything checks out until the anti-collision light fails to illuminate. You cancel the twilight portion and reschedule for daylight hours, because the light is on your list for twilight and night operations and a failed item means the aircraft is not in a condition for safe operation for that flight.

Common misreading: Two things get confused here. First, the 23-item list is an FAA recommendation in an advisory circular, not a 23-item regulatory checklist; the binding requirement is § 107.49's duty to inspect for a condition for safe operation. Skipping an item is not automatically a violation, but ignoring a defect it would have caught can be. Second, people read "create an inspection program" and "comply with part 43 appendix D" as mandatory steps. The chunk twice calls them options — appendix D is explicitly a maintenance checklist being borrowed as a structure, and the chunk warns it is not comprehensive on its own.
