Local Airport Advisory (LAA) is a service from Flight Service facilities located on the landing airport, given on a discrete ground-to-air frequency or on the tower frequency when the tower is closed. It includes local airport advisories, automated weather reporting with voice broadcasting, and a continuous display of Automated Surface Observing System (ASOS) or Automated Weather Observing Station (AWOS) data, other continuous direct reading instruments, or manual observations.

Military Training Routes (MTRs) are routes military aircraft use to stay proficient at tactical flying. They are usually established below 10,000 feet mean sea level (MSL) for operations at speeds over 250 knots, though some segments may be set higher for route continuity. Names start with IR for instrument flight rules (IFR) routes or VR for visual flight rules (VFR) routes, followed by a number. The digit count tells you the altitude: four number characters means no segment is above 1,500 feet above ground level (AGL), as in IR1206 or VR1207; three number characters means at least one segment is above 1,500 feet AGL, as in IR206 or VR207. IFR low altitude en route charts depict all IR routes plus the VR routes that handle operations above 1,500 feet AGL. VFR sectional charts depict military training activity, including IR, VR, Military Operations Area (MOA), restricted area, warning area, and alert area information.

A Temporary Flight Restriction (TFR) is created by a flight data center (FDC) Notice to Airmen (NOTAM). The NOTAM starts with the phrase "FLIGHT RESTRICTIONS," then gives the location, effective time period, the area defined in statute miles, and the altitudes affected. It also lists the FAA coordination facility and phone number, the reason for the restriction, and anything else considered appropriate. Reasons for a TFR include protecting persons and property from an existing or imminent hazard; providing a safe environment for disaster relief aircraft; preventing unsafe congestion of sightseeing aircraft over an incident or event of high public interest; protecting declared national disasters for humanitarian reasons in the State of Hawaii; protecting the President, Vice President, or other public figures; and providing a safe environment for space agency operations. TFR use has become much more common since the events of September 11, 2001, and incursions have led to security investigations and certificate suspensions. The guide says it is the pilot's responsibility to know about TFRs in the proposed area of flight, and that one way to check is the FAA website www.tfr.faa.gov.

Parachute jump aircraft operations are published in the Chart Supplement U.S. (formerly the Airport/Facility Directory), and frequently used sites are shown on sectional charts. Published VFR routes exist to transition around, under, or through complex airspace, and go by names such as VFR flyway, VFR corridor, Class B airspace VFR transition route, and terminal area VFR route; they are generally found on VFR terminal area planning charts. Terminal Radar Service Areas (TRSAs) are areas where participating pilots can get extra radar service, intended to separate all IFR operations from participating VFR aircraft. The primary airport or airports inside a TRSA become Class D airspace, and the rest of the TRSA overlies other controlled airspace, normally Class E starting at 700 or 1,200 feet, set up to transition aircraft to and from the en route and terminal environment. TRSAs appear on VFR sectional and terminal area charts as a solid black line with altitudes for each segment, and the Class D portion is charted with a blue segmented line. Participation is voluntary, but VFR pilots are encouraged to contact radar approach control and use the service. National Security Areas (NSAs) are airspace of defined vertical and lateral dimensions at places needing increased security and safety for ground facilities. Flight in an NSA may be temporarily prohibited by regulation under Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations (14 CFR) part 99, with prohibitions sent out by NOTAM. Otherwise, pilots are requested to voluntarily avoid flying through these depicted areas.

Illustration: You plan a Saturday morning roof inspection in a small town. On the sectional you notice a line labeled VR1207 passing near the property. Because the name has four number characters, you know from this chunk that no segment of that route is above 1,500 feet AGL, so military aircraft on it may be low and fast, up to speeds in excess of 250 knots. You also check www.tfr.faa.gov and find an FDC NOTAM beginning "FLIGHT RESTRICTIONS" for a public figure's visit that afternoon, with the area given in statute miles and the altitudes affected. Your morning window falls outside the effective time period, so you fly in the morning, keep a sharp lookout for fast low-level military traffic, and pack up well before the restriction starts. The decision rests on two facts from the chunk: the route numbering told you the traffic could be low, and the NOTAM told you exactly when and where the restriction applied.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, the MTR digit count runs opposite to intuition: more digits means lower, not higher. Four numbers (IR1206) means no segment above 1,500 feet AGL; three numbers (IR206) means at least one segment above 1,500 feet AGL. Second, "voluntary" has different weight in different places here. TRSA participation is voluntary and NSA avoidance is requested voluntarily, but a TFR is not voluntary at all, and the guide notes that incursions have led to security investigations and certificate suspensions. Also note that nothing in this chunk grants a small unmanned aircraft permission to operate above 2,000 AGL; those opening bullets are the tail of a list from the previous page about requests, and the 2,000 AGL figures belong to the operations described there, not to you.
