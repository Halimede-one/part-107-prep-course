This part of the advisory circular explains the medical-fitness expectation behind § 107.17. Safe operation depends on the physical and mental capabilities of everyone directly involved: the remote pilot in command (remote PIC), the person manipulating the controls, the visual observer (VO), and any other direct participant. The person manipulating the controls and the VO do not have to hold an airman medical certificate. But none of them may take part in the operation if they know, or have reason to know, that they have a physical or mental condition that could interfere with the safe operation of the small unmanned aircraft system (small UAS). The guidance then lists obvious examples of incapacitations, and says the list is not exhaustive: temporary or permanent loss of the dexterity needed to operate the control station (CS) safely; inability to keep up the required “see and avoid” vigilance because of blurred vision; inability to maintain proper situational awareness because of illness or medication, such as after taking medications that caution against driving or operating heavy machinery; a debilitating physical condition such as a migraine headache or moderate or severe body aches or pains that would leave the person unable to perform their duties; and a hearing or speaking impairment that would keep crew members from communicating effectively with each other. For that last one, the chunk says the remote PIC must make sure an alternative means of effective communication is in place — the example given is a hearing-impaired person using sign language to communicate effectively.

Illustration: You are the remote PIC for a roof inspection and your VO calls that morning to say she took a cold medicine that warns against driving or operating heavy machinery. Even though she holds no medical certificate and none is required of her, she has reason to know her situational awareness may be impaired, so she cannot serve as VO on this flight. You either find another VO or reschedule. The reason: § 107.17 bars any direct participant from taking part when they know or have reason to know of a condition that could interfere with safe operation.

Common misreading: People read “no medical certificate required” as “no medical standard applies.” The certificate requirement and the fitness requirement are separate. The VO and the person manipulating the controls skip the certificate but are still bound by the same self-assessment rule as the remote PIC. A second misreading is treating a hearing or speaking impairment as an automatic disqualification — the chunk's own example shows it is the breakdown in effective communication that matters, and the remote PIC can meet the requirement by putting an alternative means of communication, such as sign language, in place.
