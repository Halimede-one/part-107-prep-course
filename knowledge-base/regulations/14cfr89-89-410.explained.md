This section covers how the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) accepts a "means of compliance" — the method a manufacturer uses to show its remote identification equipment performs as required. It is aimed at the person submitting the means of compliance, not at the pilot flying the aircraft.

Paragraph (a) puts the burden on the submitter. They must show the Administrator two things: that the means of compliance addresses all of the requirements of subparts D and E of this part, and that a standard remote identification unmanned aircraft or a remote identification broadcast module designed and produced under that means of compliance would meet the performance requirements of subpart D. (Those subparts are not reproduced here — see those subparts for what they require.)

Paragraph (b) describes the FAA side. The Administrator evaluates what was submitted and may ask for additional information or documentation to supplement the submission. So acceptance is not automatic on filing; it is a review that can involve back-and-forth.

Paragraph (c) is the outcome. If the Administrator determines the submitter has demonstrated the means of compliance meets the requirements of subparts D and E, the FAA notifies the person that the means of compliance has been accepted. The notification is the formal act of acceptance.

Illustration: A company that builds broadcast modules writes a test procedure describing how it will verify its module's broadcast message elements and transmission behavior, and submits it to the FAA as a proposed means of compliance. Two months later the FAA writes back asking for the bench-test data behind one of the claimed performance figures — permitted under paragraph (b), which lets the Administrator request supplemental documentation. The company supplies the data. The FAA then determines the procedure addresses the requirements of subparts D and E and that a module built to it would meet subpart D performance, and sends notice of acceptance under paragraph (c). Only at that point does the company have an accepted means of compliance it can build against.

Common misreading: A common misreading is that this section is something a remote pilot does. It is not — a pilot never submits a means of compliance. A related slip is treating the submission itself as acceptance. Under paragraph (c), acceptance exists only once the FAA notifies the person that the Administrator has accepted it; a pending submission is not an accepted means of compliance.
