This closing part of Chapter 2 covers four loosely related background topics.

Air Traffic Control (ATC) and the National Airspace System (NAS): ATC exists first to prevent collisions between aircraft operating in the system, and to organize and speed up the flow of traffic. Beyond that primary job, ATC can also offer additional services, but only within limits. The chunk lists what limits them: volume of traffic, frequency congestion, quality of radar, controller workload, higher priority duties, and the plain physical inability to scan for and detect every such situation. When those factors get in the way, the services cannot be provided. Still, the chunk is clear that providing additional services is not optional for the controller — it is required whenever the work situation permits. Controllers work to the procedures and minima in their order unless a letter of agreement, an FAA directive, or a military document prescribes different ones.

Operating rules and pilot/equipment requirements: safety of flight is the top priority, and the chunk credits the United States' position as the safest aviation system in the world to strict FAA regulatory oversight. Aircraft in the NAS have complied with the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) governing their certification and maintenance, and pilots have completed certification training and testing. Equally important, the chunk says, are preflight planning, aeronautical decision-making (ADM), and risk management. ADM is described as a systematic approach to risk assessment and stress management that shows how personal attitudes shape decisions and how those attitudes can be changed to improve safety. More on ADM is in Chapter 10 of the study guide — see that chapter. Pilots also follow the FAA's general operating and flight rules in the CFR, including the "see and avoid" mandate.

Visual Flight Rules (VFR) terms and symbols: this is a reading assignment, not a rule. Remote pilots are told to be familiar with material on the FAA Aeronautical Chart User's Guide website — all information on the VFR Terms tab, and these sections under "VFR Aeronautical Chart Symbols" on the VFR Symbols tab: Airports, Airspace Information, Navigational and Procedural Information, Chart Limits, Culture, Hydrography, and Relief.

Notices to Airmen (NOTAMs): these are time-critical aeronautical items that are either temporary or came up too late to be printed on charts or in other operational publications. They go out immediately through the National NOTAM System. They carry notices considered essential to the safety of flight plus supplemental data affecting other operational publications. The chunk gives some of the reasons NOTAMs are issued: hazards such as air shows, parachute jumps, kite flying, and rocket launches; flights by important people such as heads of state; inoperable lights on tall obstructions; temporary erection of obstacles near airfields; and passage of flocks of birds through airspace, which is called a BIRDTAM. NOTAMs can be had in printed form by subscription from the Superintendent of Documents, or online at PilotWeb. Local airport NOTAMs are available from various websites — the chunk names www.fltplan.com and www.aopa.org/whatsnew/notams.html as examples. Most such sites want a free registration and acceptance of terms, and in return give updated NOTAMs and Temporary Flight Restrictions (TFRs).

Illustration: A remote pilot plans a rooftop photography flight in a suburb two miles from a general aviation airport. Before heading out, she checks NOTAMs for that airport and finds one announcing a parachute jump demonstration that afternoon, plus a second noting that the obstruction lights on a nearby broadcast tower are inoperative. Both fall squarely in the categories this chunk lists. She moves her flight to the morning window, well before the jump activity, and keeps clear of the tower. The reason: NOTAMs carry exactly this kind of time-critical information that was not known early enough to print on a chart, and the chunk calls that information essential to the safety of flight. She also spends time on the Aeronautical Chart User's Guide VFR Symbols tab beforehand so she can read the airspace boundaries and airport symbols on the sectional chart covering her site.

Common misreading: Two traps here. First, people read the list of NOTAM reasons as a complete catalog. It is not — the chunk says "there are many different reasons that NOTAMs are issued" and then offers "some of those reasons." Finding nothing in your flight area that matches one of the six listed items does not mean there is no NOTAM. Second, the ATC passage gets read in opposite wrong directions. Some take "additional services" as something a pilot can count on receiving; the chunk lists six factors that can prevent it, so it is not guaranteed. Others conclude that controllers may therefore hand out additional services at their discretion; the chunk says flatly that provision is not optional on the controller's part but required when the work situation permits. Note also that none of this ATC and pilot-certification discussion is a rule imposed on remote pilots by this chunk — it is background on the system a small unmanned aircraft shares airspace with.
