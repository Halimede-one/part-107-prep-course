This section tells the people on the manufacturing side of operations over people how long they must keep their paperwork. It does not apply to remote pilots flying the aircraft.

Paragraph (a) covers anyone who submits a declaration of compliance under this subpart. That person must keep all the supporting information used to show the small unmanned aircraft meets the requirements of § 107.120(a) for Category 2 operations and § 107.130(a) for Category 3 operations (see those sections for what those requirements are). The information must be made available to the Administrator on request. How long they keep it depends on what they do:

- If they produce the aircraft: two years after production of that small unmanned aircraft system stops.
- If they design or modify the aircraft: two years after they submitted the declaration of compliance.

Paragraph (b) covers a different person: anyone who submits a means of compliance under this subpart. That person must keep the detailed description of the means of compliance, plus the justification showing how it meets the requirements of §§ 107.120(a) and 107.130(a), and make it available to the Administrator on request. The retention period here is not a fixed number of years. It lasts as long as the means of compliance remains accepted.

The line "Subpart E—Waivers" at the end is just the heading for the next subpart and carries no rule.

Illustration: A small company designs a lightweight quadcopter, submits a declaration of compliance for Category 2, and never manufactures it themselves. They file the declaration in March. Under paragraph (a)(2)(ii), because they designed rather than produced the aircraft, their two-year clock starts at the date they submitted the declaration, so they must hold the supporting test data and analysis until March two years later and hand it over if the Administrator asks. Compare a factory that builds the same airframe under its own declaration: its clock does not start until it stops producing that system, so if the line runs for six years, the records must be kept for eight years total.

Common misreading: Two traps here. First, people assume the production clock starts when the declaration is filed. It does not. For a producer, the two years begin only after production of that system ceases, which can be many years later. Second, people apply the two-year period to means of compliance records. Paragraph (b) sets no two-year limit. Those records must be kept for as long as the means of compliance remains accepted. Also note this section binds the submitters of declarations and means of compliance, not remote pilots, so a pilot flying a Category 2 aircraft has no record-keeping duty under this section.
