This section covers registration for any small unmanned aircraft that is NOT flown exclusively as a limited recreational operation under 49 U.S.C. 44809 — which includes every aircraft flown under Part 107. The certificate issued under § 48.110 (see that section for what the application requires) is what counts as registration, and it covers the specific aircraft named on the application.

Registration starts when you actually receive the Certificate of Aircraft Registration, not when you file. The effective date is the date of issue printed on the certificate itself.

The certificate expires 3 years after the date of issue unless renewed. To renew, the certificate holder verifies — in the form and manner the Administrator prescribes — that the information given under § 48.110 is still accurate, and provides updated information if it is not. That verification can be done any time within the six months before the month the certificate expires. A renewed certificate runs three years from the expiration date of the previous certificate, so renewing early does not cost you time.

Paragraph (d) lists events that end a certificate's effectiveness even before expiration. A certificate stays effective — unless it was revoked, canceled, expired, or ownership transferred — until one of these happens: the aircraft is registered under a foreign country's laws (subject to the Convention on the International Recognition of Rights in Aircraft where it applies); the aircraft is totally destroyed or scrapped; the certificate holder loses U.S. citizenship; thirty days pass after the certificate holder's death; a non-citizen individual owner loses resident alien status without becoming a U.S. citizen at the same moment; or, for a corporation that is not a U.S.-citizen corporation, either the corporation stops being lawfully organized and doing business under U.S. or State law, or the aircraft was not operated exclusively within the United States during the registration period.

Illustration: You registered your mapping drone and the certificate shows a date of issue of May 12, 2024, so it expires May 12, 2027. In November 2026 you decide to get the renewal out of the way. November is within the six months preceding May 2027, so the verification is allowed. You confirm your address and serial number are unchanged and submit. The new certificate runs three years from May 12, 2027 — not three years from November 2026. Separately, in January 2027 that drone goes into a lake and is unrecoverable. Under (d)(2) the aircraft is totally destroyed, so the certificate stops being effective; the replacement airframe you buy needs its own registration, because a certificate covers the specific aircraft on the application.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people assume renewing early extends the three years from the day they renewed — paragraph (c)(2) says the new certificate runs three years from the previous certificate's expiration date, so early renewal gains you nothing in duration. Second, people treat registration as covering the pilot or the fleet. It covers the one aircraft identified on the application, which is why destruction, scrapping, or transfer of that airframe ends the certificate's effect rather than carrying over to a new drone.
