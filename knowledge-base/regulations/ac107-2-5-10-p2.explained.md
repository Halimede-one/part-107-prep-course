This paragraph puts the burden of knowing the airspace on the person flying. The remote PIC (remote pilot in command) has to know what type of airspace the flight will take place in before flying the small unmanned aircraft. The chunk names two ways to find that out: the B4UFly app, or a current aeronautical chart from the FAA's digital products page (the link is given in the chunk). The chunk describes both as aids to the remote PIC's decision making about operating in the NAS (National Airspace System) — they give you the information you need to decide, but the decision and the responsibility stay with you.

Illustration: You are asked to photograph a warehouse roof in a part of town you have never flown in. Before you leave, you open B4UFly and also pull up a current sectional chart from the FAA digital products site. The chart shows the site sits under the shelf of a controlled airspace ring around a nearby airport. Because you now know the airspace type, you can make an informed decision about whether and how to proceed, rather than discovering the issue after you launch.

Common misreading: People treat a green screen in B4UFly as permission to fly. This paragraph does not say either tool authorizes anything — it says both aid the remote PIC's decision making. The app and the chart tell you what the airspace is; they are not the airspace authorization itself.
