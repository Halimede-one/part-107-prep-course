This chunk introduces where aviation weather information comes from and what form it arrives in. Weather service for aviation is a joint effort: the National Weather Service (NWS), the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), the Department of Defense (DOD), other aviation groups, individuals, and — because the need for weather service is worldwide — foreign weather organizations. Forecasts are not 100 percent accurate, but scientific study and computer modeling let meteorologists predict patterns, trends, and characteristics with increasing accuracy. The point of this whole system is to put up-to-date reports and forecasts in a pilot's hands so decisions about weather and flight safety can be made before and during a flight.

Surface aviation weather observations are the raw material. They are a compilation of current weather elements taken at individual ground stations across the United States, from a network of government and privately contracted facilities. Automated sources are a major part of that gathering: Automated Weather Observing Systems (AWOS), Automated Surface Observing Systems (ASOS), and other automated facilities. A surface observation describes conditions at one specific airport and includes the type of report, station identifier, date and time, modifier (as required), wind, visibility, runway visual range (RVR), weather phenomena, sky condition, temperature/dew point, altimeter reading, and applicable remarks. The observation may come from a person, from an automated station, or from an automated station that a weather observer updates or enhances. Because these reports cover a small area, the study guide says they will be beneficial to the remote pilot.

Aviation weather reports are built to depict current conditions accurately, and each one is updated on its own schedule. The guide names METARs and PIREPs as typical examples and points to http://www.aviationweather.gov/ to view reports. A METAR — Aviation Routine Weather Report — is an observation of current surface weather in a standard international format, issued on a regularly scheduled basis unless significant weather changes have occurred. A special METAR, called a SPECI, can be issued at any time between the routine reports. The chunk gives one sample METAR (METAR KGGG 161753Z AUTO 14021G26KT 3/4SM +TSRA BR BKN008 OVC012CB 18/17 A2970 RMK PRESFR) but does not decode it here; that breakdown belongs to the sections that follow.

Illustration: You are planning a morning roof inspection by small unmanned aircraft near a regional airport. The night before, you pull the airport's METAR from aviationweather.gov and see a routine report issued on schedule. The next morning, 20 minutes before launch, you check again and find a SPECI has been issued since the last routine report. That tells you something changed enough between scheduled reports to warrant a special observation, so you read it rather than relying on last night's picture. You hold the launch and keep refreshing, because the report you act on should be the current one — the system exists precisely so you can make the go/no-go call before and during the flight.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, treating a METAR as a forecast: it is an observation of weather that is happening at one station, not a prediction of what is coming. Second, assuming a scheduled METAR is the only report to check. A SPECI can appear at any time between routine METARs, so the absence of a new routine report does not mean nothing has changed. Also note the surface observation covers a small area around one specific airport — it is not a description of conditions across your whole operating region.
