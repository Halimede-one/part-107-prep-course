This part of the advisory circular explains the maintenance and recordkeeping expectations for Category 4 small unmanned aircraft — the category that relies on an FAA-issued airworthiness certificate. Three ideas are covered.

First, who may do the work and how. Anyone performing maintenance, preventive maintenance, or alterations must follow the methods, techniques, and practices in the manufacturer's current maintenance manual or Instructions for Continued Airworthiness (ICA) that are acceptable to the Administrator, or other methods acceptable to the Administrator. Inspections must follow the manufacturer's instructions or other instructions acceptable to the Administrator. The person must have the knowledge, skill, and appropriate equipment for the job, and must use parts of a quality that leaves the aircraft at least equal to its original or properly altered condition.

Second, records. The owner or operator must keep records of maintenance performed, records showing the status of life-limited parts, records of compliance with Airworthiness Directives (AD), and records of inspection compliance.

Third, how this interacts with other rules. When a remote pilot operates a small unmanned aircraft under part 107 with an FAA-issued airworthiness certificate under part 21, the requirements of parts 43 and 91 do not apply. Such an aircraft may still be eligible to operate under part 91 in certain circumstances. Because Category 4 prescribes fewer maintenance and record retention requirements than parts 43 and 91, the FAA notes it may be hard to move between the two operating rules, and an aircraft maintained only to part 107 standards may struggle to show part 43 and 91 compliance. An owner or operator may voluntarily elect to comply with the relevant parts 43 and 91 requirements while operating under part 107; because those requirements are more stringent than § 107.140 on maintenance and airworthiness, doing so can make switching back and forth easier.

On responsibility: the owner is normally responsible for maintaining the aircraft per § 107.140(c). If the owner enters an agreement with another entity to operate the aircraft, the operator becomes responsible for the maintenance and record retention requirements. The FAA expects most Category 4 operators under part 107 will also be the owner, or will operate under the owner's direction, in which case responsibility stays with the owner. Section 107.140(c) lets an agreement spell out who holds the responsibility, and if the agreement so specifies the FAA will hold the operator accountable. The agreement may be a written lease or contract, a verbal agreement, or another form, and should at a minimum address the requirements of § 107.140(c). If an agreement is found invalid or unenforceable, responsibility falls back to the owner.

Illustration: A survey company owns a Category 4 aircraft with an airworthiness certificate and leases it to a contractor who flies it over crowds at public events under part 107. The written lease states the contractor is responsible for maintenance and records. Under § 107.140(c) as described here, the FAA would hold the contractor — the operator — accountable for maintaining the aircraft to the manufacturer's maintenance manual or ICA, for using a person with the knowledge, skill, and appropriate equipment, and for keeping maintenance records, life-limited part status, AD compliance, and inspection compliance records. The owner also decides to have the contractor comply with the relevant parts 43 and 91 requirements anyway, because the company sometimes wants to fly the same aircraft under part 91, and the AC notes that the more stringent parts 43 and 91 requirements make moving between operating rules easier. If a court later found the lease unenforceable, the maintenance and records responsibility would revert to the owner.

Common misreading: Two traps. One: people read "parts 43 and 91 do not apply" as meaning there is no maintenance standard for a Category 4 aircraft under part 107. The chunk says the opposite — § 107.140 still imposes maintenance and record retention duties; they are simply fewer than parts 43 and 91. Two: people assume a handshake deal cannot shift responsibility, or conversely that any agreement permanently moves it off the owner. The chunk says an agreement may be written, verbal, or other, but if it is found invalid or unenforceable the responsibility returns to the owner.
