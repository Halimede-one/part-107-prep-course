This part of Appendix A is a worked example of a risk assessment worksheet, plus the notes that define its scales. The worksheet has eight columns: Hazard (what might happen), Cause (why it might happen), Effect (what the result looks like), Likelihood (the chance the hazard occurs), Severity (how bad the consequences are if it does), Risk (the two combined), Mitigation (how you reduce the risk), and Emergency or Contingency Procedures (your plan of action if the event still happens anyway). The example entry is loss of control link over people, caused by loss of power supply from the control station, the small unmanned aircraft system going out of range, or signal interference from another device such as Wi-Fi or Bluetooth. Its effect is ground impact, injury to people, damage to the small unmanned aircraft. Likelihood is rated Occasional, severity is injury to people (possibly including death), and the overall risk is High. The mitigation shown, in part, is that the remote pilot in command must conduct a control link check before operations over people, and the preflight briefing must include control link loss procedures. The contingency is to try to reestablish the control link connection, and if it cannot be reestablished, start emergency or contingency procedures for loss of control link. The notes give the rating words you choose from. Likelihood is Improbable, Remote, Occasional, Probable, or Frequent. Severity is No safety effect, Minor, Major, Hazardous, or Catastrophic. Risk is Low, Medium, High, or Avoid, where Avoid means changes to the operation are required for mitigation or the operation should not be conducted. These risk levels are assigned before you consider the effect of your mitigations. The appendix then says that to identify all potential hazards effectively you should start with a thorough description of the operational environment, including but not limited to: current and forecasted weather; the condition of the equipment to be used and its operational limitations; fatigue and awareness levels of the remote pilot, observer, and other participants; terrain and obstacles such as proximity to power lines and buildings in both the planned and the emergency or contingency flightpath; the hazards of flying over people; the hazards of flying at night if the operation will occur at night, including missions that span day, twilight, and night, such as night vision adaptation where unlit towers and buildings are present, and weather and terrain features that may affect the ability of other aircraft in the area to see the anti-collision light for at least 3 statute miles; and other hazards present during all small unmanned aircraft flights, such as schedule pressure, health issues, or lack of familiarity with the equipment. Once hazards are identified, you complete the worksheet steps for each one, beginning with listing its causes.

Illustration: You are hired to shoot video of a charity 5K and the route crosses a plaza where runners will pass under your flight path. Before the job you fill out the worksheet. Hazard: loss of control link over people. Causes: control station battery failure, aircraft drifting out of range behind the building, and interference from the event's Wi-Fi hotspots and the crowd's Bluetooth devices. Effect: ground impact, injury to people, damage to the aircraft. Likelihood: Occasional. Severity: injury to people, possibly including death. Risk: High. Because the risk is High rather than Avoid, you can proceed with mitigations, so you write them down: a control link check immediately before any flight over the plaza, and a preflight briefing that covers what everyone does if the link drops. Your contingency column says you will first try to reestablish the link, and if that fails you will run your loss-of-link emergency procedure. Having written it out, you also notice from the operational environment list that you had not considered the power lines along the emergency flight path, so you add that as a separate hazard line.

Common misreading: Two things get misread here. First, people assume the ratings in the example are the correct answers for their own flight. They are not; Occasional and High are what this one example assessment concluded, and your own likelihood, severity, and risk depend on your aircraft, site, and crew. Second, people treat the Risk rating as something to assign after mitigation so the number looks better. Note (3) says these definitions are used to assign the level of risk before considering the effect of risk mitigation, so a High risk stays logged as High and you record the mitigations separately.
