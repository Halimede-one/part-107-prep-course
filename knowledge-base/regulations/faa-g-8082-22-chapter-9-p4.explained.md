This chunk covers two separate things: alcohol and vision.

On alcohol, the study guide explains that intoxication is measured as a percentage of alcohol by weight in the blood. It states that 14 CFR part 91 (Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations, the general operating rules) requires blood alcohol level to be less than .04 percent, and that 8 hours must pass between drinking alcohol and piloting an aircraft. Both conditions have to be satisfied. If your blood alcohol is .04 percent or higher even after 8 hours have gone by, you still cannot fly until it drops below that figure. And even if your blood alcohol is far under .04 percent, you still cannot fly until the 8 hours have elapsed. The guide adds its own advice: be more conservative than the regulation requires.

On vision, the point is that understanding how the eyes work helps a pilot use them well. The scanning technique described is a series of short stops rather than a sweeping glance. Scan right to left or left to right. Start at the greatest distance an object can be perceived (the top of your view) and work inward toward the aircraft's position (the bottom). Each stop should cover an area about 30° wide. No single stop should last longer than 2 to 3 seconds, though how long you pause depends on how much detail you need. When you move to the next stop, overlap the previous field of view by 10°. The chunk references Figure 9-2 for an illustration of this; the figure itself is not reproduced in this text.

Illustration: A remote pilot has two beers with dinner at 7 p.m. and is scheduled to fly a roof inspection at 1 a.m. that night. He feels completely clear-headed and figures any alcohol is long gone. Only 6 hours have passed, so he cannot act as pilot. He reschedules the flight for after 3 a.m., which puts him past the 8-hour mark, and he also confirms he feels no residual effects before launching. Separately, during the flight he keeps visual line of sight by stopping his gaze in roughly 30° blocks across the sky, holding each stop no more than 2 to 3 seconds and overlapping each new block 10° into the last one, instead of letting his eyes drift continuously across the horizon.

Common misreading: The two alcohol conditions are often read as alternatives, as if being under .04 percent excuses the 8-hour wait, or as if waiting 8 hours clears you no matter your blood alcohol. The chunk says both must be met: under .04 percent and at least 8 hours since drinking. A second misreading is treating scanning as one smooth sweep. A continuous sweep is not what is described; the eyes only resolve detail when stopped, which is why the technique is a series of brief fixed stops with overlap.
