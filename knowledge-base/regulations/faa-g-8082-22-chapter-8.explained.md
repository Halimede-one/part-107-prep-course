This chapter opening covers two things: where performance data for a small unmanned aircraft (UA) comes from, and how air density affects that performance.

On data: the manufacturer may publish operational and performance information — takeoff, climb, range, endurance, descent, and landing figures. You need to understand what those numbers mean to use the aircraft's capabilities and limitations safely and efficiently. The chunk stresses that manufacturers do not standardize this data, so one maker's numbers may not be presented the way another's are. If your manufacturer publishes nothing, the guide advises looking for performance data that other users of the same manufacturer model have already worked out and published, and using it as a starting point.

On temperature: raising a substance's temperature lowers its density; lowering the temperature raises it. So air density varies inversely with temperature — but this holds only at constant pressure. In the real atmosphere, both temperature and pressure drop as you climb, and these pull density in opposite directions. Pressure falls fairly rapidly with altitude and that effect usually dominates, so pilots can expect density to decrease with altitude.

On humidity: air is never perfectly dry. Water vapor is lighter than air, so moist air is lighter than dry air. As the water content of the air goes up, the air gets less dense, density altitude goes up, and performance goes down. Relative humidity is the amount of water vapor in the air expressed as a percentage of the maximum the air can hold at that temperature. Warm air holds more water vapor; cold air holds less. Perfectly dry air is zero percent; saturated air that can hold no more is 100 percent. Humidity by itself is usually not considered an important factor in calculating density altitude and aircraft performance, but it is a contributing factor.

Illustration: You buy a small UA whose maker publishes nothing about endurance or climb rate. Rather than guess, you search out logged performance figures published by other operators flying the same manufacturer model and treat those as your starting point for planning. On the flight day it is hot and muggy. You reason through it: the high temperature makes the air less dense, and the high water vapor content makes it less dense still, raising density altitude and cutting performance. So you treat the borrowed endurance and climb numbers as optimistic rather than as a floor, and plan a shorter mission with more margin.

Common misreading: People read "water vapor is lighter than air, so moist air is lighter" and expect humid air to help performance because the aircraft is flying through lighter air. It works the other way: lighter air is less dense air, which raises density altitude and decreases performance. The other trap is the temperature rule — density varies inversely with temperature only at constant pressure, so you cannot apply it straight to a climb, where pressure is dropping too and the pressure effect usually dominates.
