This section covers who has to sign an aircraft registration paperwork and what proof of authority they must attach when someone signs for someone else. It applies to the Aircraft Registration Application (AC Form 8050-1), any document submitted as supporting evidence under this part, and requests to cancel a Certificate of Aircraft Registration.

The basics: signatures must be in ink or by another means the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) accepts. If signed in ink, the application must also carry each signer's typed or legibly printed name in the signature block.

Then the rule sorts owners by how they hold title:

- People doing business under a trade name: signed by, or on behalf of, each person who shares title.
- An agent signing for the owner: must state the owner's name, sign as agent or attorney-in-fact, and submit a signed power of attorney (or a true copy certified under § 49.21 of this chapter — see that section).
- A corporation: an authorized person signs by means acceptable to the FAA, the title of that signer's office is shown, and a copy of the board of directors' authorization to sign is submitted, certified as true under § 49.21 by a corporate officer or other person in a managerial position. That authorization copy is not needed if the signer is already a corporate officer or other person in a managerial position and states the title of the office with the signature, or if a valid authorization to sign is already on file at the Registry. The authorization-copy requirement also does not apply to an irrevocable deregistration and export request authorization under the Cape Town Treaty that is signed by a corporate officer and filed with the Registry.
- A partnership: state the full partnership name, name each general partner, and have a general partner sign.
- Co-owners who are not business partners: every person who shares title must sign.

Finally, how long authority lasts. A power of attorney or other evidence of authority to sign for another is good, unless revoked sooner, until the expiration date stated in it. If no expiration date is stated, it is good for not more than 3 years after the date it is signed, or after the date the grantor certifies in writing that the authority is still in effect (where the grantor is a corporation, that certification comes from a corporate officer or other person in a managerial position).

Illustration: A survey company registers an aircraft. The office manager fills out the Aircraft Registration Application and signs it, but leaves the signature block showing only a scrawled signature with no printed name and no office title, and attaches nothing else. Under this section that filing is incomplete: because the applicant is a corporation, the signer's office title must be shown, and a certified copy of the board's authorization to sign must come with the application unless the signer is a corporate officer or person in a managerial position whose title is stated with the signature, or a valid authorization is already on file at the Registry. The company's vice president signs instead, prints the name in the signature block, and writes "Vice President" next to the signature — which fits the exception in paragraph (d)(3)(i), so no separate board authorization copy is needed.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people treat the 3-year limit as the life of every power of attorney. It is not — if the document states its own expiration date, that stated date controls; the 3-year clock only applies when no expiration date is stated, and any power of attorney can be revoked sooner. Second, co-owners often assume one signature covers the group. For co-owners not in business as partners, and for people doing business under a trade name, each person who shares title must sign or have it signed on their behalf. Only a partnership gets by with a single general partner's signature, and only after naming the partnership and each general partner.
