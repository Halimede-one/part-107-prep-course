This part of the advisory circular describes what the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) expects from the company or person who holds an FAA-accepted Declaration of Compliance (DOC) under part 107 subpart D — the paperwork that makes a particular make and model of small unmanned aircraft eligible for Category 2 or Category 3 operations over people. The duties fall on the DOC holder (usually the manufacturer), not on the remote pilot, but they shape what a pilot can rely on.

A DOC holder must be able to track its products and tell the public if one of its products is judged unsafe for a given category of operations over people. It must also let the FAA see technical data and facilities if the FAA decides an operational safety issue warrants that. Submitting the DOC is itself the holder's declaration that it is willing to do these things, and the FAA may review a applicant's procedures, processes, and facilities to check compliance with the subpart.

The FAA expects the holder to keep three things true: that the Means of Compliance (MOC) remains valid, so injury to a human being on impact with the aircraft does not exceed the established safety requirements; that the aircraft's construction, safety analysis, and service history reveal no hazardous conditions or safety defects that would put it out of compliance; and that the holder monitors its manufacturing processes, operational usage, and accident and incident data — which can include information from owners and operators — to confirm continued compliance with the performance-based safety requirements.

Safety defects found after a DOC is accepted must be resolved. Defects can surface through owner complaints, industry safety bulletins, or the manufacturer's own notice. If the FAA finds the defect, it notifies the DOC holder, who may either correct it or show the aircraft still meets the safety requirements. If an unresolved defect leads the FAA to rescind the DOC, a new DOC must be submitted before operations over people may resume. If the holder will not fix it, the owner or operator may correct the issue and submit a new DOC — but only if they can and will meet every eligibility requirement for a DOC. Holders must also keep a product support and notification process in place and supply remote pilot operating instructions for the aircraft.

Rescissions are published on the FAA website, which is how remote pilots learn an aircraft is no longer eligible to fly over people. Separately, the holder's declared notification process must alert owners, the public, and the FAA when an aircraft no longer meets the subpart's requirements or has a safety defect. The public and owner notice should say the aircraft is not eligible for operations over people until the defect is corrected; the FAA notice describes the noncompliance and how it will be addressed. If the holder fixes the defect and the FAA accepts a new DOC, the holder should tell the public and owners of that make and model that the aircraft is eligible again for the applicable category or categories.

Illustration: You fly a Category 2 aircraft and have a job next week photographing a street fair, which means flying over people. Two days before the shoot, an email from the manufacturer says a cracking issue in the propeller guard means the model is not eligible for operations over people until corrected, and you confirm on the FAA website that the DOC has been rescinded. You cancel the over-people portion of the job and reschedule it for after the fix, because without an accepted DOC the aircraft is no longer eligible for Category 2 operations — the manufacturer's notification and the published rescission exist precisely to tell you that.

Common misreading: Two traps here. First, a rescinded DOC does not ground the aircraft — the advisory circular says rescission makes the aircraft unsafe and ineligible for operations over people, not inoperable. You can still fly it in operations that do not go over people. Second, the line about an owner or operator submitting a new DOC is not a do-it-yourself loophole: it applies only if that person is capable and willing to satisfy all the eligibility requirements of the DOC, which is a manufacturer-level undertaking, not a repair in your garage.
