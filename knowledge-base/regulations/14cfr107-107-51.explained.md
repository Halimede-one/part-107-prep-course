This section sets four hard operating limits that both the remote pilot in command (the certificated person responsible for the flight) and the person actually manipulating the flight controls must follow. First, groundspeed — speed over the ground, not airspeed — may not exceed 87 knots (100 miles per hour). Second, altitude may not exceed 400 feet above ground level (AGL), with one exception: if the aircraft is flown within a 400-foot radius of a structure, it may go higher, but it still may not fly higher than 400 feet above that structure's immediate uppermost limit. Both parts of the exception must be true at once. Third, flight visibility observed from the location of the control station must be at least 3 statute miles. The section defines flight visibility for its own purposes as the average slant distance from the control station at which prominent unlighted objects can be seen and identified by day, and prominent lighted objects can be seen and identified by night. Fourth, the aircraft must stay at least 500 feet below a cloud and at least 2,000 feet horizontally from a cloud. The heading 'Subpart C—Remote Pilot Certification' at the end is just the start of the next subpart and carries no rule.

Illustration: You are hired to inspect a 600-foot broadcast tower. Flying straight up from a launch point in the parking lot a quarter mile away, you would be capped at 400 feet AGL. Instead you reposition so the aircraft operates within a 400-foot radius of the tower. Now you may climb above 400 feet AGL to inspect the upper sections, but you must stop no higher than 400 feet above the tower's immediate uppermost limit — the top of the tower — which puts your ceiling at 1,000 feet AGL in that spot. Before launch you also check that visibility from where you are standing with the controller is at least 3 statute miles and that the overcast layer is high enough that you can stay 500 feet below it at your intended altitude.

Common misreading: Two traps. One: people read the structure exception as '400 feet above the structure' and forget it also requires staying within a 400-foot radius of that structure — drift away from the tower at 900 feet AGL and you are no longer legal. Two: the cloud and visibility numbers are measured from the control station and the aircraft, not from a weather report for the nearest airport, and the 3 statute miles is flight visibility as this section defines it — the slant distance at which you can see and identify prominent objects — not simply whatever a METAR says.
