This part of the advisory circular puts the labeling burden on the remote pilot, not just the manufacturer. Before flying over people, you must make sure the small unmanned aircraft carries a proper label. The point of the label is that a remote pilot, an FAA inspector, or a bystander can look at the aircraft and tell what kinds of operations over people it is eligible for. If there is no clearly legible label, the aircraft is not eligible to operate over people. If a label wears off, becomes unreadable, or falls off, you are responsible for putting on a new one before flying over people. It does not matter how you got the aircraft — directly from the applicant or secondhand through a later transfer — the labeling requirement still applies. No pilot may fly the aircraft without first verifying the label meets the requirements of §§ 107.120(b)(1) and 107.130(b)(1), as applicable (see those sections for what the label must say). If the aircraft was built before the effective date of this rule, or simply was never labeled, you are responsible for checking whether it appears on an FAA-accepted Declaration of Compliance (DOC). If it is listed and is eligible to operate over people, you are the one who must label it in accordance with § 107.135. Separately, the label has to change when the aircraft is modified into a different or additional category. If a modification makes the aircraft no longer eligible for the category on its old label, the label must show the category it actually qualifies for now. Whoever performed the modification has to remove or cover the old label so only the correct, current category label is visible.

Illustration: You buy a used small unmanned aircraft from another operator to use for a job filming a parade crowd. The aircraft has a faded sticker on the underside of the shell that you can barely read. Because the label is not clearly legible, the aircraft is not eligible to operate over people as it sits. You check the FAA-accepted Declaration of Compliance listings, confirm the model is listed and eligible, then make and apply a new legible label yourself before the flight — the advisory circular puts that responsibility on you as the remote pilot, including for an aircraft you acquired by subsequent transfer. Before launching you verify the label meets the applicable requirements in § 107.120(b)(1) or § 107.130(b)(1).

Common misreading: Pilots often assume labeling is purely the manufacturer's job and that an unlabeled or worn-off label is somebody else's problem. This chunk says the opposite for operations over people: the remote pilot must verify the label, must replace a degraded one, and must label an unlabeled or pre-rule aircraft after confirming it is on an FAA-accepted DOC. A second misreading is thinking you can add a new category label alongside the old one after a modification — the old label has to be removed or covered so only the currently eligible category is visible.
