This part of the advisory circular talks about keeping records, and it frames the whole thing as optional. The FAA says owners and operators "may find recordkeeping to be beneficial" — it is advice, not a requirement in this passage. The suggested practice is to write down any repair, modification, overhaul, or replacement of a system component that comes out of normal flight operations, and to note the time-in-service for that component when the work was done. With that history in hand, an operator can build a reliable maintenance schedule for the small unmanned aircraft system (small UAS) and its parts. Records can be on paper, electronic, or both, and can cover periodic inspections, maintenance, preventative maintenance, repairs, and alterations. The AC says records should cover every component of the small UAS, not just the aircraft: the small unmanned aircraft itself, the control station (CS), launch and recovery equipment, the Command and Control (C2) link equipment, the payload, and anything else needed to operate safely. The reasons given are practical. Records reinforce the owner/operator's responsibility by giving a systematic way to judge whether the aircraft is in a condition for safe flight. They give retrievable evidence about the condition of safety-critical systems and components, which supports the decision to launch. They are called an essential safety support for operators who rapidly accumulate flight hours or cycles. And methodical data collection helps track component service life and spot patterns of component, equipment, and structural failures.

Illustration: A remote pilot flies roof inspections five days a week and is piling up hours fast. After a hard landing she replaces a landing leg and logs it: date, part, and the airframe's time-in-service that day. Four months later a second leg cracks. Because she logged both events with hours, she can see the legs are failing at roughly the same accumulated flight time rather than from one-off bad luck, so she starts replacing them on a schedule before they crack. She also logs a firmware update to the control station and a repair to a payload gimbal cable, since the AC's list of components to track includes the CS and the payload, not just the aircraft.

Common misreading: People read this as a rule and assume Part 107 makes them keep a maintenance logbook. The chunk does not say that — it uses "may find recordkeeping to be beneficial" and "would then be able to," which is recommendation language in an advisory circular. Conversely, some pilots read it as purely optional and skip it entirely, missing that the preflight inspection duty it supports comes from the regulation cited at the top of this section; see that section for what is actually required.
