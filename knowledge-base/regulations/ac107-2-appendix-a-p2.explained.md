This part of Appendix A to Advisory Circular (AC) 107-2A covers tools for thinking through risk before a flight. It is guidance, not a rule. Three ideas run through it.

First, gather outside information. The chunk lists a weather briefing, air traffic control (ATC), the FAA, local pilots, and landowners as sources, and says technology can aid decision making and improve situational awareness. The point is not just collecting information but managing it, which the chunk says is key to situational awareness and can have a positive effect on your decisions.

Second, judge your own aeronautical decision making (ADM) by results. The chunk defines successful decision making as a pilot's consistent ability to keep himself or herself, any persons involved in the operation, and the aircraft in good condition regardless of the conditions of any given flight. It warns that complacency and overconfidence are risks in unmanned operations just as in manned ones. It names two tools: the IMSAFE checklist, to confirm you are mentally and physically prepared for the flight, and the DECIDE model, to keep evaluating each operation for hazards and analyzing risk. For more on these, see paragraph A.4.8 and AC 60-22, Aeronautical Decision Making.

Third, identify hazards systematically. Hazards tied to the small unmanned aircraft and its operating environment must be identified and controlled. The analysis has to consider all components of the system, based on the equipment used and the environment it is flown in, and the key question to ask is "what if?" Remote pilots in command (remote PICs) are expected to exercise due diligence in finding significant and reasonably foreseeable hazards in their operations, and the chunk recommends documenting those hazards using the process in Figure A-1. Paragraph A.4 then says Safety Risk Assessment and Mitigation steps should be taken before flight, and offers Figure A-2 as a sample table. The chunk is explicit that this sample is not a required format; it is one way to document a risk assessment and mitigation plan.

Gap: the content of Figure A-1 (Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment Process Chart) and Figure A-2 (Sample Safety Risk Assessment and Mitigation Steps Before Flight) did not come through in this chunk, only their titles. See the advisory circular itself for the charts.

Illustration: You are the remote PIC for a roof inspection on a commercial building you have flown twice before. Both prior flights went smoothly, so you are tempted to launch straight away. Following the appendix, you stop and work the sources it lists: a weather briefing shows gusts building through the afternoon, and a call to the property owner (the landowner) reveals that a contractor crew will be on the roof today, which was not the case on earlier flights. You run IMSAFE on yourself and note you slept poorly. Then you ask "what if?" about the new element: what if a gust pushes the aircraft toward the crew? You write down the hazards and your mitigations in your own risk assessment table, and you decide to move the flight to early morning when winds are calmer and the crew is not yet on site. The reason: your two uneventful prior flights were breeding complacency, and the equipment-plus-environment analysis turned up a hazard that did not exist before.

Common misreading: People read "hazards... must be identified and controlled" and Figure A-2 and conclude Part 107 requires a written risk assessment on a particular form. This appendix is advisory. The chunk itself says documenting hazards per Figure A-1 is recommended, that the Safety Risk Assessment and Mitigation steps should be taken before flight, and that the Figure A-2 format should not be considered a required format. It is one example of how to document a plan, not a mandated worksheet.
