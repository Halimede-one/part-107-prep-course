This section defines three words and then bans certain kinds of false or misleading claims made in advertising or sales.

The definitions: "Airworthy" means the aircraft conforms to its type design and is in a condition for safe operation. "Product" means an aircraft, aircraft engine, or aircraft propeller. "Record" is very broad — any writing, drawing, map, recording, tape, film, photograph, or other documentary material that preserves or conveys information, in any format, including paper, microfilm, identification plates, stamped marks, bar codes, or electronic format. A record can be separate from the item, attached to it, or inscribed on it. So a listing on a website, a photo of a data plate, or a stamped mark on a part all count as records.

The prohibitions all apply only when you are conveying information related to an advertisement or a sales transaction, and only about the airworthiness of a type-certificated product or the acceptability of a product, part, appliance, or material for installation on a type-certificated product. Within that scope, paragraph (b) bars any fraudulent or intentionally false statement in a record, and any fraudulent or intentionally false reproduction or alteration of a record. Paragraph (c) goes further: even without intent to deceive, you may not make a material representation that is likely to mislead a consumer acting reasonably under the circumstances — and that includes misleading a consumer by leaving out material information.

Paragraph (d) is the escape hatch. Paragraphs (b) and (c) do not apply if the person can show the product actually is airworthy, or that the product, part, appliance, or material actually is acceptable for installation on a type-certificated product. The burden sits with the person making the claim.

Note that this section speaks about type-certificated products. The chunk does not say which aircraft are type-certificated, so that question belongs to other sections. The last two lines of the chunk are the heading and source note for Subpart B, Security Threat Disqualification, with no rule text attached.

Illustration: A remote pilot who also flies a certificated airplane decides to sell a spare propeller through an online aviation marketplace. The listing photo shows the data plate, and the description says "airworthy, ready to install." The pilot knows the propeller was removed after a prop strike but does not mention it, figuring the buyer will have it inspected anyway. That omission is the problem. Under paragraph (c)(2), leaving out material information that makes the acceptability claim misleading to a reasonable consumer is prohibited, even though every word written was literally true. The right move is to state the prop strike history in the listing, or to drop the "airworthy, ready to install" language. If the pilot can actually show the propeller is acceptable for installation — for example because of what the records demonstrate — paragraph (d) would take the claim out of reach of (b) and (c), but that showing is the seller's to make.

Common misreading: Two common slips. First, people read this as a rule about talking — it is not. It applies only to information conveyed in connection with an advertisement or a sales transaction, and only to records about airworthiness or installation acceptability. Casual conversation that has nothing to do with selling something is outside what this section describes. Second, people assume that if they did not intend to deceive, they are clear. Paragraph (b) is the intent-based prohibition, but paragraph (c) stands separately and asks only whether the representation is likely to mislead a reasonable consumer, including by omission. Honest sloppiness in a listing can still violate (c).
