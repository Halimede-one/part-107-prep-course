This is the middle portion of 49 U.S.C. 44809, the statutory exception that lets people fly unmanned aircraft recreationally without operating under Part 107. The chunk opens mid-sentence: items (i) and (ii) finish a list that began in an earlier paragraph about flying at a fixed site, which is not in this chunk — see that paragraph for what it requires. As shown here, operations at such a site may go up to 400 feet above ground level without prior authorization from the Administrator, and above 400 feet above ground level only with prior authorization from the Administrator.

Paragraph (3) covers unmanned aircraft weighing 55 pounds or greater, counting the weight of anything attached to or carried by the aircraft. Such an aircraft may be flown only if two things are true: it complies with standards and limitations developed by a community-based organization and approved by the Administrator, and it is operated from a fixed site as described in paragraph (1) (that paragraph is not in this chunk).

Paragraph (4) directs the Administrator, when implementing subpart C of part 89 of title 14, Code of Federal Regulations, to prioritize reviewing and deciding requests to establish FAA-Recognized Identification Areas at fixed sites established under this section.

The next three subsections limit how the exception is read. Subsection (d), the savings clause, says nothing in that subsection expands the Administrator's authority to make a person flying under this section seek permission beyond what this section already requires. Subsection (e) says nothing in the section limits the Administrator's authority to bring an enforcement action against a person flying any unmanned aircraft who endangers the safety of the national airspace system. Subsection (f) says nothing in the section stops the Administrator from issuing rules that apply to unmanned aircraft generally — including aircraft eligible for this exception — on the operational parameters in subsection (a), the registration and marking of unmanned aircraft, standards for remotely identifying owners and operators, and other standards consistent with maintaining the safety and security of the national airspace system.

Subsection (g) requires the Administrator, consulting manufacturers, community-based organizations, and other industry stakeholders, to develop, maintain, and update an aeronautical knowledge and safety test, administered electronically by the Administrator or someone the Administrator designates. The test must adequately demonstrate the operator's understanding of aeronautical safety knowledge and knowledge of Federal Aviation Administration regulations and requirements for operating an unmanned aircraft system in the national airspace system.

Subsection (h) defines "community-based organization" as a membership-based association entity that meets seven conditions: it is recognized by the Administrator of the Federal Aviation Administration; it is described in section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986; it is exempt from tax under section 501(a) of that Code; its mission is demonstrably the furtherance of model aviation; it provides a comprehensive set of safety guidelines for all aspects of model aviation covering assembly and operation of model aircraft and emphasizing safe aeromodelling and the protection of people and property on the ground, and it may also provide safety rules and programming for unmanned aircraft with advanced flight capabilities enabling active, sustained, controlled navigation beyond visual line of sight of the operator; it provides programming and support for local charter organizations, affiliates, or clubs; and it provides assistance and support in developing and operating locally designated model aircraft flying sites.

Subsection (i) requires the Administrator, working with aeromodelling stakeholders, to publish an advisory circular within 180 days of the date this section was enacted identifying the criteria and process for recognizing community-based organizations. The editorial notes at the end record that the section was added by Pub. L. 115–254 on Oct. 5, 2018, and amended by Pub. L. 118–63 on May 16, 2024, and that subsection (i)'s "date of enactment" means Oct. 5, 2018.

Illustration: A certificated remote pilot belongs to a model aircraft club and wants to fly a 60-pound scratch-built aircraft at the club's field on a Saturday. She is not being paid and intends to fly purely for fun, so she looks at the recreational exception. Under paragraph (3), because the aircraft weighs 55 pounds or greater once she adds the camera mount, she may fly it under this exception only if it complies with standards and limitations her community-based organization developed and the Administrator approved, and only from a fixed site as described in paragraph (1). She confirms the club field qualifies as such a site and that her organization's large-model program covers the aircraft. She also notes that at that site she can go up to 400 feet above ground level without prior authorization, but a planned high pass above 400 feet above ground level would need prior authorization from the Administrator first, so she keeps the flight below 400 feet. If her club's approved standards did not cover an aircraft that size, the recreational exception would not be available to her for this flight.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read subsections (d) and (e) as making recreational flying a protected zone the FAA cannot touch. The savings clause only stops the agency from adding permission requirements beyond what this section already requires; subsection (e) expressly preserves enforcement against anyone who endangers the safety of the national airspace system, and subsection (f) expressly preserves the agency's power to write general rules on registration, marking, and remote identification that reach even aircraft eligible for this exception. Second, the 400-foot figures and the community-based organization standards in this chunk belong to the recreational exception, not to Part 107 — a flight conducted under Part 107 follows Part 107's own altitude and airspace rules, not these.
