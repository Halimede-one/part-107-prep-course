This chunk is background on airports for remote pilots. "Airport" is defined broadly: any area of land or water used or intended for landing or takeoff of aircraft, including seaplane bases, heliports, and facilities for tilt rotor aircraft, plus the buildings, facilities, and rights of way that go with them. There are two types of airports: towered (has an operating control tower, where air traffic control, or ATC, provides a safe, orderly, and expeditious flow of traffic) and non-towered (no operating control tower). Airports also subdivide into civil (open to the general public), military/Federal Government (operated by the military, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA), or other Federal agencies), and private (private or restricted use, not open to the general public).

At a non-towered airport, two-way radio communications are not required, but monitoring other aircraft on the specified frequency is described as a good operating practice. The right frequency to monitor is the Common Traffic Advisory Frequency (CTAF), the frequency designated for airport advisory practices at airports without an operating control tower. A CTAF may be a Universal Integrated Community (UNICOM), MULTICOM, FSS, or tower frequency, and it is identified in appropriate aeronautical publications. UNICOM is a nongovernment air/ground radio station that may provide airport information at public use airports with no tower or FSS. Manned traffic patterns at non-towered airports are always entered at pattern altitude; the preferred entry from the downwind side is a course 45 degrees to the downwind leg, joining at midfield.

The chunk then says to review current data for an airport when operating in its vicinity, because that data covers things like communication frequencies, services available, closed runways, and airport construction. It introduces "three common sources" but then lists four items: aeronautical charts, the Chart Supplement U.S. (formerly Airport/Facility Directory), Notices to Airmen (NOTAMs), and the Automated Terminal Information Service (ATIS). The Chart Supplement U.S. is called the most comprehensive source for a given airport, covers airports, heliports, and seaplane bases open to the public, is published in seven books organized by region, is revised every 56 days, and is available digitally at www.faa.gov/air_traffic/flight_info/aeronav. NOTAMs carry time-critical, temporary information, or information not known far enough in advance to be printed on charts, and get immediate dissemination; examples given are taxiway and runway closures, construction, communications, and changes in status of navigational aids. The chunk says pilots should check NOTAMs before any flight and points back to Chapter 2 for more (see that chapter). ATIS is a looped recording of local weather and other non-control information on a local frequency, normally updated once per hour and more often when changing local conditions warrant; it includes weather, runways in use, specific ATC procedures, and airport construction activity. Illustration: You are hired to photograph a barn roof about a mile from a non-towered rural airstrip. Before the job you pull up the Chart Supplement U.S. entry for that airstrip and find the CTAF listed, along with a note that services include a UNICOM. You also check NOTAMs and see one for runway construction, which tells you crews and aircraft are likely working an unusual pattern that day. You bring a handheld receiver and monitor the CTAF while flying, not because the chunk requires it, but because it is described as good operating practice, and you know arriving aircraft will likely be entering the pattern at pattern altitude on a 45-degree course to the downwind leg, joining at midfield, so you can picture where traffic will come from. Finding no operational need to be close in, you keep your work on the far side of the barn from the airstrip.

Common misreading: Two things trip people up here. First, the chunk says three common sources and then lists four bullets, which is an inconsistency in the source, not a hidden rule; treat all four as sources to know. Second, "two-way radio communications are not required" at a non-towered airport is a statement about pilots at non-towered airports in this chapter, and the traffic-pattern entry description (pattern altitude, 45 degrees to downwind, join at midfield) describes how manned aircraft arrive, not a pattern a small unmanned aircraft should fly. Nothing in this chunk tells you whether or when you may operate near an airport or whether authorization is needed; that comes from the airspace rules elsewhere.
