This section is a narrow, Hawaii-only rule. If the Governor of the State of Hawaii (or the Governor's designee) asks the FAA Administrator for protection, and the Administrator decides an inhabited area inside a declared national disaster area in Hawaii needs protection for humanitarian reasons, the Administrator issues a NOTAM (Notice to Airmen) that sets up temporary flight restrictions. The NOTAM itself spells out how large the restricted area is and how long it lasts, chosen to protect people and property on the ground.

Once that NOTAM is out, nobody may fly an aircraft inside the designated area unless at least one of five conditions is met: (1) the person has authorization from the official in charge of the associated emergency or disaster relief response activities and flies under the terms of that authorization; (2) the aircraft is carrying law enforcement officials; (3) the aircraft is carrying persons involved in an emergency or a legitimate scientific purpose; (4) the aircraft is carrying properly accredited newspersons, and before entering, a flight plan is filed with the FAA or air traffic control (ATC) facility named in the NOTAM, and the flight follows the conditions and restrictions set by the official in charge of on-scene emergency response; or (5) the aircraft is operating under an ATC clearance or instruction.

The NOTAM runs for 90 days or until the national disaster area designation ends, whichever happens first. It can be ended earlier by notice, or extended by the Administrator if the Governor of Hawaii or the Governor's designee asks.

Illustration: A remote pilot on the Big Island is hired to photograph damage to a residential neighborhood after a disaster declaration. Checking NOTAMs before the flight, the pilot finds a temporary flight restriction issued under this section covering the neighborhood. The pilot does not launch. Instead, the pilot contacts the official in charge of the disaster relief response and requests authorization under paragraph (b)(1). If that authorization comes through, the pilot flies only within the conditions it sets. Without one of the five listed conditions, the flight inside that area is simply not permitted, no matter how worthwhile the photos would be.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people assume a press or media assignment is by itself a free pass. It is not. The newsperson path in (b)(4) requires properly accredited newspersons, a flight plan filed in advance with the facility the NOTAM names, and compliance with the on-scene official's conditions. Second, people read the 90 days as a guarantee the restriction stays up that long, or as a countdown they can wait out. The NOTAM can end sooner when the disaster designation is terminated or by notice, and it can be extended at the Governor's request, so the only reliable move is to check the current NOTAM each time you fly.
