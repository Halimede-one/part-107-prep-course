This rule applies to whoever submitted a declaration of compliance that the Administrator accepted — in practice, the producer of a standard remote identification unmanned aircraft or a remote identification broadcast module. It is a recordkeeping duty, not a flight rule, so it does not fall on a remote pilot who simply buys and flies the equipment.

The retention clock runs for as long as the product listed on that declaration of compliance is still being produced, plus another 24 calendar months after production ends. Three categories must be kept and made available for the Administrator to inspect: (a) the means of compliance itself, along with all documentation and substantiating data related to the means of compliance used; (b) records of all test results; and (c) any other information needed to show that the product meets the means of compliance, the remote identification requirements, and the design and production requirements of this part.

Illustration: A small company builds a remote identification broadcast module and files a declaration of compliance that the Administrator accepts. It produces the module from 2026 through 2029, then discontinues it. Its test logs, the means of compliance document, and the supporting engineering data cannot be discarded when the last unit ships — the company must hold them for 24 calendar months past the end of production, so into 2031, and hand them over if the Administrator asks to inspect. The reason is that the acceptance of the declaration rests on that evidence, and the agency may need to verify it after the fact.

Common misreading: People read the 24 months as the whole retention period. It is an add-on. The period is the entire production run of that listed product plus 24 calendar months after it, so a product made for ten years carries more than a decade of records.
