This chapter opener explains why radio knowledge matters to a remote pilot even though the rules do not require you to transmit. In the NAS (National Airspace System), manned pilots exchange information by radio before, during, and after a flight — traffic position, unexpected weather, in-flight emergencies. Small UA (unmanned aircraft) pilots are not expected to communicate over radio frequencies, but if you use a radio to build situational awareness, you need to understand "aviation language" and the kinds of conversations you will hear. The chunk points to the Pilot/Controller Glossary in the AIM (Aeronautical Information Manual) for standard terminology and examples, and notes that ICAO (International Civil Aviation Organization) has adopted a phonetic alphabet that pilots should use when identifying their aircraft to ATC (air traffic control). The alphabet itself is in Figure 7-1, which is an image not carried in this text.

The rest of the chunk covers airports with no operating control tower. Alertness is the main defense there, because some aircraft have no radio at all and some pilots simply do not announce their presence or intentions. The safest arrangement is for all radio-equipped aircraft to transmit and receive on one common frequency, with small UA pilots monitoring. An airport may have a full- or part-time tower, a full- or part-time FSS (flight service station) on the field, a full- or part-time UNICOM (universal communications) station, or no aeronautical station at all. A pilot at a non-towered airport has three ways to state intentions and get airport and traffic information: communicate with an FSS, communicate with a UNICOM operator, or make a self-announce broadcast. Many airports now have an automated UNICOM giving weather, radio check capability, and airport advisory information, with features typically selected by microphone clicks on the UNICOM frequency; availability is published in the Airport/Facility Directory and on approach charts.

The common frequency is called the CTAF — Common Traffic Advisory Frequency — designated for airport advisory practices at an airport without an operating control tower. A CTAF may be a UNICOM, MULTICOM, FSS, or tower frequency, and it is identified in the appropriate aeronautical publications. UNICOM is usually 122.8, but that can vary where many non-towered airports sit close together, so check the Chart Supplements U.S. or the sectional chart. Where a non-towered airport has no UNICOM or other listed frequency, the MULTICOM frequency of 122.9 is used. These frequencies appear on a sectional chart next to the airport or in the FAA's Chart Supplements.

Finally, the chunk states the guidance plainly: a remote pilot in command is not required to communicate with manned aircraft in the vicinity of a non-towered airport, but safety in the NAS requires that remote pilots be familiar with traffic patterns, radio procedures, and radio phraseology. One standard practice worth knowing: an inbound manned pilot "broadcasts in the blind" at 10 miles from the airport and includes position relative to north, south, east, or west — for example, "Town and Country traffic, Cessna 123 Bravo Foxtrot is 10 miles south inbound for landing, Town and Country traffic."

Illustration: You are hired to photograph a grain elevator about a mile north of a non-towered airstrip. Before the flight you look the airport up on the sectional and in the Chart Supplements. No UNICOM is listed, so you set your handheld receiver to the MULTICOM frequency of 122.9 and just listen. Partway through the flight you hear: "Smithfield traffic, Bonanza 4 Tango Romeo is 10 miles north inbound for landing, Smithfield traffic." You know from the phraseology that a manned aircraft is ten miles out and coming from your side of the field. You bring the aircraft down and hold it low until you hear the Bonanza report on final and then on the ground. You were not required to say anything, and you did not transmit — the radio was purely for awareness, and it gave you several minutes of warning that looking at the sky alone would not have.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read "not required to communicate" as "radio knowledge is not tested or useful." The chunk says the opposite: because you are not talking, listening and understanding the phraseology is how you stay aware, and familiarity with traffic patterns, radio procedures, and phraseology is called out as a safety requirement. Second, people memorize 122.8 as "the" non-towered frequency. The chunk says UNICOM is usually 122.8 but can vary where many non-towered airports cluster, that the CTAF may instead be a UNICOM, MULTICOM, FSS, or tower frequency, and that 122.9 is used only where no UNICOM or other frequency is listed — so you check the sectional or Chart Supplements for the specific airport rather than assuming.
