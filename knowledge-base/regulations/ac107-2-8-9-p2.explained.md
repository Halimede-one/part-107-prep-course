This part of the advisory circular (AC) describes two promises a manufacturer makes when it files a Declaration of Compliance (DOC) for a small unmanned aircraft, plus what the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) does with DOCs afterward.

First, the applicant declares it has set up and keeps running a support and notification process for the public, covering the aircraft listed on the DOC. The purpose of that process is to tell aircraft owners, the public, and the FAA about safety issues that result in noncompliance with regulatory requirements. Notice to owners could be a posting on a website or an electronic message. Owners may register their aircraft with the manufacturer under a warranty program or to update the aircraft's software, and that registration can be used to tell the remote pilot that the aircraft does not meet the safety requirements for eligibility in one or more categories of operations over people. The DOC holder should use due diligence so that word of a possible noncompliant condition reaches the responsible individual. Manufacturers are encouraged to build a system for this communication and should use it to pass along corrective actions for safety defects. Once the manufacturer can confirm the defects are fixed for specific serial numbers, it may submit a new DOC, and it must verify those serial-numbered aircraft have no safety defects before doing so.

Second, the DOC includes an agreement that the holder will let the Administrator inspect its facilities, technical data, and any manufactured small unmanned aircraft when a safety issue warrants that level of FAA involvement. The FAA will coordinate with the holder before visiting facilities and explain the safety concerns. The responsible person should be ready to discuss production and safety procedures, including engineering and quality systems, procedures manuals, and handbooks, when practical, and to discuss an evaluation of and a proposed solution to the safety concerns. That discussion may include critical processes and critical suppliers, recent design changes, significant changes in manufacturing personnel, procedures, or inspections, quality issues or escapes, witnessing any tests needed to determine compliance with part 107 subpart D, other relevant correspondence or data about issues found during new product deliveries or acceptance, and service history data and service difficulties. The FAA expects the responsible person to have this information and other relevant quality data, procedures, or records on hand.

On acceptance: if the FAA determines the applicant has shown compliance with part 107 subpart D, it notifies the applicant that the DOC is accepted, and all FAA-accepted DOCs are made available on the FAA's website.

On rescinding: in deciding whether to rescind a DOC, the FAA will consider any safety defect, material, component, or feature that increases the likelihood the aircraft could cause a serious injury to a person during an operation over people. If such a condition exists, the FAA will email the DOC holder to discuss resolving it. The FAA could rescind a DOC if the aircraft is no longer compliant with the applicable safety requirements or with the prohibition on exposed rotating parts that would cause lacerations. The FAA could also rescind a DOC if the aircraft contains a safety defect the applicant is unable or unwilling to correct, or if the FAA finds a DOC violates § 107.5 — see that section for what it covers.

Illustration: You fly a small unmanned aircraft that the manufacturer declared eligible for a category of operations over people, and you registered it with the manufacturer so you would get software updates. One morning you get an electronic notice from the manufacturer saying a defect has been found and your aircraft does not currently meet the safety requirements for that category. You check the FAA website list of accepted DOCs and see the status has changed for your model. You cancel the planned flight over a crowded farmers market and reschedule the job as a flight away from people instead. The reason: the notification process described here exists precisely to tell you your aircraft no longer satisfies the eligibility requirements for that category of operations over people, and the eligibility rested on the DOC. Weeks later the manufacturer confirms the fix for your serial number and submits a new DOC; once that is accepted and posted, the category eligibility is back.

Common misreading: People read the inspection agreement and the notification duty as something a remote pilot has to do. They do not — these are commitments by the applicant who holds the DOC, normally the manufacturer. The pilot's practical stake is different: the manufacturer's notices and the FAA's published list of accepted DOCs are how you learn whether your aircraft is still eligible for a category of operations over people. A second misreading is thinking a rescinded DOC is permanent. The chunk says the manufacturer may submit a new DOC after confirming the safety defects are corrected for specific serial numbers, provided it verifies those aircraft have no safety defects first.
