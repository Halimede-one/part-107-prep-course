This is the tail end of an Advisory Circular appendix that offers risk assessment tools. Advisory Circular (AC) material is guidance, not regulation, so these are suggested habits rather than requirements. Two things appear here. First, the last part of a self-questioning checklist. One block of questions is about people and vehicles: how will I keep from creating hazards to people not directly participating in the operation, could my operational area count as an open-air assembly of persons, will I be flying over moving vehicles and if so how do I avoid creating hazards to them, and will I need to move my landing spots because of people. The next block is External Pressures, which asks you to look at yourself: am I stressed or anxious, will this particular flight make me stressed or anxious, is someone pushing me to finish fast, am I working inside an unhealthy safety culture, and am I being honest with myself and others about my own abilities and limitations.

Second, the chunk walks through what to do after you have found your hazards. A.4.9 says once hazards and risks are understood, you must design and put in place risk controls. Examples given are new or changed procedures, new or modified equipment, adding visual observers (VOs), or any of many other changes. A.4.10 defines residual risk as the risk left over after mitigation, and notes this is often a multistep process that continues until risk is reduced to an acceptable level to begin or keep operating. It also says that after you design controls but before the operation begins or continues, you must assess whether the controls will actually work and whether they create new hazards. Creating a new hazard is called substitute risk, described as a situation where the fix is worse than the original problem. The loop in Figure A-1 that returns to the top of the diagram shows you running hazard identification, risk analysis, and risk assessment again on the modified operation to see whether it is now acceptable. A.4.11 closes the loop: once the remote pilot in command (remote PIC) develops and implements appropriate risk controls, the operation can begin.

Illustration: A remote PIC is hired to photograph a construction site next to a busy four-lane road. Working through the questions, she notes she will be operating near moving vehicles and that her planned landing spot is on a sidewalk where workers walk at shift change. She also admits the client has asked her to be done in twenty minutes, which is an external pressure. So she designs controls: move the launch and landing site into the fenced contractor lot away from foot traffic, add a visual observer to watch the road side, and tell the client the job will take forty minutes. Before launching she checks her own fix for substitute risk and spots one. The contractor lot puts a tall crane between her and part of the flight path, which would break her line of sight. That control introduced a new hazard, so she loops back through the process, picks a different spot in the lot with a clear view, and only then begins the flight.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read the people-and-vehicle questions as if the Advisory Circular were setting the rules for operating over people and moving vehicles. It is not. These are prompts to make you think; the actual requirements live in the regulations, so see those sections. Second, people treat risk control as a one-pass checklist: find hazard, apply fix, fly. The chunk explicitly says mitigation is often multistep and that you must assess your controls before you start, because a fix can be worse than the original problem. Adding a visual observer or changing equipment is not automatically an improvement until you have checked what it changed.
