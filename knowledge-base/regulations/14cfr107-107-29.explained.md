This section sets the conditions for flying a small unmanned aircraft system (sUAS, a drone and its control equipment) at night and during civil twilight.

For night operations, two things must both be true unless paragraph (d) applies. First, the remote pilot in command must have completed an initial knowledge test or training, as applicable, under § 107.65 after April 6, 2021 (see that section for what the test or training involves). Second, the small unmanned aircraft must have lighted anti-collision lighting visible for at least 3 statute miles, with a flash rate sufficient to avoid a collision. The remote pilot in command may dim that lighting if operating conditions make dimming safer, but may never turn it off.

For civil twilight, only the lighting requirement is stated: the same 3-statute-mile visible anti-collision lighting with a flash rate sufficient to avoid a collision, and the same permission to reduce intensity but not extinguish it.

Paragraph (c) defines civil twilight for purposes of paragraph (b). Outside Alaska it is the period beginning 30 minutes before official sunrise and ending at official sunrise, and the period beginning at official sunset and ending 30 minutes after official sunset. In Alaska, it is the period of civil twilight as defined in the Air Almanac.

Paragraph (d) closes out the old waiver path. After May 17, 2021, no person may operate a small unmanned aircraft system at night under a certificate of waiver issued before April 21, 2021 under § 107.200. Certificates of waiver issued before March 16, 2021 under § 107.200 that authorized deviation from § 107.29 terminate on May 17, 2021.

Illustration: You are hired to photograph a parking lot and want to start 20 minutes before official sunrise at a site in Ohio. That start time falls inside civil twilight, because civil twilight there begins 30 minutes before official sunrise and ends at official sunrise. So paragraph (b) applies: the aircraft needs lighted anti-collision lighting visible for at least 3 statute miles with a flash rate sufficient to avoid a collision. Your drone's stock lights are not rated that far, so you fit an approved strobe and verify it works before launch. Halfway through, ground fog begins reflecting the strobe back into your eyes and washing out your view of the aircraft. You dim the strobe, because the rule lets the remote pilot in command reduce intensity in the interest of safety given operating conditions — but you do not switch it off, because extinguishing it is not permitted.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people assume the knowledge test or training requirement in paragraph (a)(1) also covers civil twilight. Paragraph (b) as written states only the lighting requirement for civil twilight. Second, people read the dimming allowance as a general permission to run without lights in dark or sensitive areas. The chunk allows reducing intensity only, and only when the remote pilot in command determines operating conditions make it safer; the lighting may not be extinguished. Also note that civil twilight here is defined only for purposes of paragraph (b), and that Alaska uses the Air Almanac definition rather than the 30-minute windows.
