This part of Advisory Circular (AC) 107-2A describes what the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) means by maintenance of a small Unmanned Aircraft System (small UAS) and how it suggests you handle it. Maintenance covers scheduled and unscheduled overhaul, repair, inspection, modification, replacement, and system software upgrades of the small UAS and the components it needs to fly. The guidance says that whenever possible the operator should follow the manufacturer's instructions. The manufacturer may supply a maintenance program; if none is supplied, the applicant may build one. For the benefits of keeping records, the chunk points to paragraph 7.3.5; for Category 4 maintenance requirements and Category 4 record retention requirements and owner and operator responsibilities, it points to paragraphs 8.3.7.4 and 8.3.7.4.1 — see those paragraphs.

Scheduled maintenance (7.2.1): the manufacturer may give documentation for scheduled maintenance of the whole aircraft and the associated system equipment, and may name components that should get periodic maintenance or replacement based on time-in-service limits such as flight hours, cycles, and/or calendar-days. Operators should follow that schedule to get the longest and safest service life. If no scheduled maintenance instructions exist (7.2.1.1), the operator should set up its own protocol — for example, documenting each repair, modification, overhaul, or replacement arising from normal flight operations and recording that component's time-in-service when the work was done. Over time that record lets the operator build a reliable schedule.

Unscheduled maintenance (7.2.2): during a preflight inspection the remote pilot in command (remote PIC) may find a component needing servicing such as lubrication, or repair, modification, overhaul, or replacement outside the scheduled period — either from normal flight operations or from a mishap. The manufacturer of the aircraft or of a component may also require an unscheduled system software update to fix a problem. When such a condition is found, flight operations should not occur until the issue is corrected.

Performing maintenance (7.2.3): sometimes the manufacturer requires certain tasks be done by the manufacturer itself or by a person or facility it specifies, and maintenance should follow the manufacturer's instructions. If the operator declines to use the manufacturer, or the recommended personnel cannot do the work, the operator should weigh the expertise of maintenance personnel familiar with that specific small UAS and its components. If the operator or other maintenance personnel cannot restore the aircraft or component to its safe operational specification (7.2.3.1), the operator should replace it with one in a condition for safe operation. All required maintenance should be completed before each flight, preferably per the manufacturer's instructions, or failing that, within known industry best practices.

Illustration: You fly a mapping aircraft whose manufacturer calls for motor replacement at 200 flight hours. During preflight you notice one motor sounds rough, and your log shows 173 hours on it — short of the scheduled interval. This is unscheduled maintenance: a condition found during preflight inspection from normal flight operations. Following 7.2.2, you do not fly the mission until the motor is addressed. The manufacturer's manual says motor replacement must be done at an authorized service center. You decide not to ship it and instead use a local shop that works on this exact airframe daily — 7.2.3 lets you weigh that shop's expertise with this specific small UAS. The shop cannot get the vibration back within spec, so under 7.2.3.1 you replace the motor with one in a condition for safe operation rather than fly a marginal aircraft. You then log the repair and the 173 hours of time-in-service, which over time builds the reliable schedule described in 7.2.1.1.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, this is an Advisory Circular, and almost every sentence here says "should," not "must" — these are recommended practices, not independent legal requirements. Do not quote 7.2 as a regulation that mandates a maintenance program or maintenance records for every operation; the chunk itself routes mandatory Category 4 maintenance and record retention requirements to other paragraphs. Second, people read "the manufacturer may provide the maintenance program" as permission to skip maintenance when no program exists. The chunk says the opposite: if the manufacturer gives no scheduled maintenance instructions, the operator should establish its own protocol, and all required maintenance should still be completed before each flight.
