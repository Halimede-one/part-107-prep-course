This is the closing part of the study guide's chapter on aeronautical decision-making. It covers four linked ideas, all advisory rather than regulatory.

Stress management: the guide suggests setting aside time for relaxation each day or keeping up a physical fitness program to reduce stress levels, and managing time better so you aren't pressured by falling behind schedule or missing deadlines.

Use of resources: to make informed decisions in flight you have to know what resources are available to you. Useful tools and information sources aren't always obvious, so recognizing them is part of aeronautical decision-making (ADM) training. Beyond spotting a resource, you need the judgment to decide whether there is time to use it and what effect using it will have on safety of flight.

Situational awareness: the accurate perception and understanding of all factors and conditions within the five fundamental risk elements — flight, pilot, aircraft, environment, and type of operation — that affect safety before, during, and after the flight. It requires understanding how significant each flight-related factor is and how it will matter later in the flight. A pilot with real situational awareness has an overview of the whole operation and is not fixated on one factor that merely seems important. Knowing the aircraft's geographic location is not enough; you also have to understand what is happening. Fatigue, stress, and work overload all push a pilot toward that fixation and shrink overall awareness. The chunk notes that a distraction pulling the pilot's attention away from monitoring the aircraft is a contributing factor in many accidents.

Workload management: planning, prioritizing, and sequencing tasks so the essential ones get done and overload is avoided. With experience, a pilot learns to see high-workload periods coming and prepares for them during low-workload periods. The guide also says a pilot should listen to ATIS, the Automated Surface Observing System (ASOS), or the Automated Weather Observing System (AWOS) if available, then monitor the tower frequency or Common Traffic Advisory Frequency (CTAF) to get a good idea of expected traffic conditions.

Recognizing overload matters as much as preventing it. The first sign is working harder while accomplishing less. As workload climbs, attention cannot be spread across several tasks, and the pilot starts focusing on just one. A task-saturated pilot takes in no input from the various sources around them, so decisions get made on incomplete information and errors become more likely. The remedy the guide gives: stop, think, slow down, and prioritize. Figure 10-7, which is referenced but not reproduced here, illustrates environmental, physiological, and psychological stress as factors affecting decision-making, with the greatest impact during high workload.

Illustration: You are flying a roof inspection near a non-towered airport. You have a client on site asking questions, a visual observer calling out a distant aircraft, a low-battery warning starting to flash, and a gust that keeps pushing the aircraft off the roofline. You notice you've been fighting the gust for two minutes and have taken no usable images — working harder, accomplishing less, the first sign of overload. You apply the guide's advice: stop, think, slow down, prioritize. You bring the aircraft back to a stable hover away from the structure, tell the client you'll answer questions after landing, and land on the low battery rather than trying to finish the pass. Before the next sortie you check the AWOS at the nearby field for the wind trend and monitor the CTAF so you know what traffic to expect, and you sequence the shot list on the ground so the high-workload moments are planned instead of improvised. The reason: a task-saturated pilot stops receiving input from all those sources at once, and decisions made on incomplete information are where errors come from.

Common misreading: Two traps here. First, people read "situational awareness" as simply knowing where the aircraft is. The guide is explicit that geographic position is only part of it — you also have to understand what is happening, across all five risk elements, including after the flight. Second, the ATIS/ASOS/AWOS and tower/CTAF listening advice is presented as a workload management technique, not as a requirement imposed on small unmanned aircraft operations; the chunk says to use them "if available." Whether you must do anything with air traffic control comes from the operating rules, not from this chapter.
