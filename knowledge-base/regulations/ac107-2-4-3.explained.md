This passage explains why honesty in paperwork matters. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) depends on what owners and remote pilots of small unmanned aircraft systems (small UAS) tell it, both when it authorizes operations and when it has to decide whether someone complied with the rules. Because the agency acts on that information, the chunk says the United States government may take appropriate action against a small UAS owner, operator, remote pilot in command (remote PIC), an applicant for a Declaration of Compliance (DOC), or anyone else who fraudulently or knowingly provides false records or reports, or who reproduces or alters any records, reports, or other information for fraudulent purposes. The chunk lists two kinds of consequence: civil sanctions imposed by the FAA, and suspension or revocation of a certificate or waiver. It points to § 107.5 for the underlying rule — see that section. Note that the sentence beginning this chunk is cut off from the previous page; the opening words continue a sentence that started with the heading.

Illustration: A remote pilot applies for a waiver and, wanting to look more experienced, writes in flight hours and a training record that never happened. The waiver is granted and the operation goes fine. Later, during an FAA inquiry, the agency compares the application against the pilot's actual records. Because the pilot knowingly provided false information on a record the FAA relied on to authorize the operation, this passage says the government may take appropriate action — which could include civil sanctions and suspension or revocation of the pilot's certificate and the waiver itself. The clean flight history does not cure the false statement; the falsification is the violation.

Common misreading: People read this as covering only the remote pilot certificate application. The chunk is broader: it names the owner, the operator, the remote PIC, an applicant for a Declaration of Compliance, and "anyone else," and it covers any records, reports, or other information — not just certificate paperwork. A second misreading is that only making something up counts; the chunk also covers reproducing or altering existing records for fraudulent purposes.
