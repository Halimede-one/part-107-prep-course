This section lists what you send the FAA Registry to get a Certificate of Aircraft Registration, and when the FAA will throw the application back at you. Three things must go in: the Aircraft Registration Application, AC Form 8050-1, signed the way § 47.13 prescribes (see that section); the Aircraft Bill of Sale, AC Form 8050-2, or other evidence of ownership authorized by § 47.33, § 47.35, or § 47.37 — unless that evidence is already recorded at the Registry; and the fee required by § 47.17 (see those sections for the amounts and the acceptable forms of evidence). The FAA rejects the application for three paperwork faults: a form that is not completed, a name and signature that are not the same throughout, or a signature block with no legibly printed or typed name alongside the signature. Paragraph (c) gives a temporary way to fly while you wait. If the aircraft was last previously registered in the United States, and you have complied with paragraph (a), you carry the second copy of the Aircraft Registration Application in the aircraft as temporary authority to operate without registration. That authority is good for operation within the United States until you receive the Certificate of Aircraft Registration or until the FAA denies the application. It is not available on an application received once 12 months have passed since receipt of the first application following transfer of ownership by the last registered owner. And if no registration number has been assigned when you apply, the second copy cannot be used as temporary authority at all.

Illustration: An operator buys a used aircraft that already carries a United States registration number from its previous owner. She fills out AC Form 8050-1 completely, signs it with her name printed legibly in the signature block, encloses the AC Form 8050-2 Aircraft Bill of Sale, pays the § 47.17 fee, and mails the package to the Registry. She keeps the second copy of the application in the aircraft. Decision: she may operate within the United States without the registration certificate in hand, because the aircraft was last previously registered in the United States, a registration number is already assigned, and she has complied with paragraph (a). The moment the Certificate of Aircraft Registration arrives — or if the FAA denies the application — the second copy stops serving as her authority.

Common misreading: People read paragraph (c) as a blanket "the application copy lets me fly while I wait." It does not. It applies only to an aircraft last previously registered in the United States, only for operation within the United States, and only if a registration number has already been assigned. A brand-new aircraft with no number yet gets no temporary authority from the second copy. The other trap is the rejection list in paragraph (b): a mismatch between the name you typed and the name you signed, or a missing printed name next to your signature, is enough for the FAA to reject the whole application — these are not minor formatting notes.
