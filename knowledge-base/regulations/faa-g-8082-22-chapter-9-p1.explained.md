This chapter covers the human body as a limiting factor in small unmanned aircraft (UA) operations. Part 107 does not allow a small UA operation if the remote pilot in command (remote PIC), the person manipulating the controls, or the visual observer (VO) cannot safely carry out their responsibilities. The remote PIC is responsible for making sure no crewmember takes part while impaired. Four specific disqualifiers are listed: a person may not serve as remote PIC, person manipulating the controls, VO, or other crewmember if he or she has consumed any alcoholic beverage within the preceding 8 hours, is under the influence of alcohol, has a blood alcohol concentration of .04 percent or greater, or is using a drug that affects mental or physical capabilities. The guide stresses that impairment is not limited to alcohol and illegal drugs: certain over-the-counter (OTC) medications, such as some antihistamines and decongestants, may cause drowsiness, and certain medical conditions, such as epilepsy, may create risk. It is the remote PIC's responsibility to determine that their medical condition is under control and that they can safely conduct the operation.

The chapter then lists medical and physiological factors a pilot should know: hyperventilation, stress, fatigue, dehydration, heatstroke, and the effects of alcohol and drugs. Three are explained in this part of the chunk. Hyperventilation is breathing too fast and too deeply, which causes an abnormal loss of carbon dioxide from the blood. It happens to pilots more often than is generally recognized and seldom incapacitates completely, but its symptoms alarm an uninformed pilot, and rising anxiety and breathing rate make it worse. It can lead to unconsciousness as the respiratory system overrides breathing to regain control. Symptoms include visual impairment, unconsciousness, a lightheaded or dizzy sensation, tingling sensations, hot and cold sensations, and muscle spasms. Treatment is restoring the proper carbon dioxide level: breathing normally is both the best prevention and the best cure, and slowing the breathing rate, breathing into a paper bag, or talking aloud all help. Recovery is usually rapid once breathing returns to normal.

Stress is the body's response to physical and psychological demands. The body releases chemical hormones such as adrenaline and raises metabolism to feed the muscles; blood sugar, heart rate, respiration, blood pressure, and perspiration all increase. A "stressor" is the element that causes the stress, and examples include physical stress (noise or vibration), physiological stress (fatigue), and psychological stress (difficult work or personal situations). Stress is either acute (short term) or chronic (long term). Acute stress is an immediate perceived threat that triggers the "fight or flight" response, whether the threat is real or imagined, and a healthy person can normally cope with it. Ongoing acute stress can become chronic stress, which is a level of stress that is an intolerable burden, exceeds the person's ability to cope, and makes performance fall sharply. Causes can include loneliness, financial worries, and relationship or work problems. Pilots at that level are not safe and should not exercise their airman privileges; those who suspect chronic stress should consult a physician.

Fatigue is frequently associated with pilot error. Its effects include degraded attention and concentration, impaired coordination, and decreased ability to communicate, all of which seriously affect decision-making. Physical fatigue comes from sleep loss, exercise, or physical work; mental fatigue comes from factors such as stress and prolonged cognitive work. Like stress, fatigue is acute or chronic. Acute fatigue is short term and normal in everyday life, the tiredness that follows strenuous effort, excitement, or lack of sleep, and rest after exertion plus 8 hours of sound sleep ordinarily cures it. The chunk ends mid-discussion before chronic fatigue is described.

Illustration: A remote PIC is scheduled to fly a roof inspection at 9:00 a.m. The night before, he had two beers with dinner, finishing at about 10:00 p.m., and he woke up with a head cold and took an over-the-counter decongestant at 8:00 a.m. He thinks through both items. The alcohol is not a problem: by 9:00 a.m. more than 8 hours have passed since his last drink, he is not under the influence, and his blood alcohol concentration is not .04 percent or greater. The decongestant is the problem. The study guide names decongestants and antihistamines as medications that may cause drowsiness, and part 107 prohibits serving as remote PIC while using a drug that affects mental or physical capabilities. He also notices he feels foggy and slow, which fits the fatigue effects the chapter describes. He cancels the flight and reschedules, because he cannot safely carry out his remote PIC responsibilities while the medication is working on him. He also makes the same check with his visual observer, since the same prohibitions apply to the VO.

Common misreading: Two common misreadings. First, people treat the 8-hour alcohol rule as the whole test, assuming that if 8 hours have passed they are clear to fly. The chunk lists four separate disqualifiers, and any one of them grounds you: you can be past 8 hours and still be under the influence, or still at a blood alcohol concentration of .04 percent or greater. Second, people assume "drug" means illegal drug. The chunk explicitly includes over-the-counter medications such as certain antihistamines and decongestants, and the test is whether the drug affects your mental or physical capabilities, not whether it is legal or prescribed. Also note these restrictions are not just for the person holding the controller: they apply to the remote PIC, the person manipulating the controls, the visual observer, and other crewmembers alike.
