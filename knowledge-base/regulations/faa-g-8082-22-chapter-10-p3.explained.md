This chunk gives two self-check tools a remote pilot can use before flying, plus an introduction to human factors.

IMSAFE is a personal readiness check covering six items: Illness (am I sick?), Medication (am I taking anything that might affect judgment or cause drowsiness?), Stress (job, money, health, or family pressure, which hurts concentration and performance), Alcohol (have I been drinking within 8 hours? within 24 hours?), Fatigue (am I tired and not adequately rested?), and Emotion (am I emotionally upset?). The chunk notes that as little as one ounce of liquor, one bottle of beer, or four ounces of wine can impair flying skills, and that alcohol also makes a pilot more susceptible to disorientation and hypoxia. On stress, the chunk says the regulations list medical conditions that require grounding but stress is not among them, so the pilot has to judge its effect personally. On fatigue, it warns that fatigue may not be apparent to the pilot until serious errors are already made.

PAVE is a preflight hazard check that sorts the risks of a flight into four categories: Pilot-in-command (PIC), Aircraft, enVironment, and External pressures. Pilot asks whether you are ready in terms of experience, recency, currency, and physical and emotional condition, with IMSAFE supplying the answers. Aircraft asks whether this is the right aircraft, whether you are familiar and current in it, and whether it can carry the planned load. EnVironment covers weather (current ceiling and visibility, the chance that weather differs from the forecast, thunderstorms present or forecast, and icing or temperature/dew point spread if there are clouds), terrain, and airspace including temporary flight restrictions (TFRs). External pressures are influences from outside the flight that push you to complete it at the expense of safety: wanting to demonstrate your qualifications, wanting to impress someone, a general goal-completion orientation, and pride about skill or experience levels.

After identifying the risks, the pilot decides whether they can be managed safely. If not, cancel the flight. If continuing, develop strategies to reduce the risks, such as setting personal minimums tailored to your own experience and proficiency. The chunk singles out managing external pressure as the single most important key to risk management, because it is the one category that can make a pilot ignore all the others; personal standard operating procedures (SOPs) are offered as a way to give those pressures a release.

Finally, conditions like fatigue, complacency, and stress are called human factors. The chunk states human factors have been documented as a primary contributor to more than 70 percent of aircraft accidents, and that they matter in maintenance and air traffic management as well as flight operations.

Illustration: You are scheduled to fly a roof inspection at 7 a.m. for a client who has been waiting two weeks. You slept four hours because a sick child kept you up, and you are running IMSAFE in the truck. Illness: fine. Medication: none. Stress: moderate. Alcohol: none. Fatigue: you are clearly not adequately rested. Emotion: frayed. Then you run PAVE. Pilot: fatigued, so this is a red flag. Aircraft: fine. EnVironment: light wind, good visibility, no TFR over the site. External pressure: strong, because the client has waited and you want to look dependable. The dominant risks are fatigue plus the pressure to deliver. Since fatigue may not show itself until you have already made a serious error, and since external pressure is the factor that can make you wave off all the others, you call the client and move the flight to the afternoon after a nap. Your personal SOP of "no flying on under six hours of sleep" gives you a ready answer that takes the decision out of the moment.

Common misreading: Two traps here. First, people read the alcohol line as if it set a legal limit. It does not. This chunk is a self-check prompt, not a rule; it simply asks whether you have been drinking within 8 hours and within 24 hours and warns how little alcohol it takes to impair flying skills. The actual drug and alcohol prohibitions live in the regulations, not in IMSAFE. Second, the stress note is sometimes read as "stress does not matter because it is not grounding." The chunk says the opposite: because the regulations do not list stress among the medical conditions requiring grounding, the pilot is the one who must weigh its effect on performance.
