This is Appendix E of the advisory circular — a sample preflight assessment and inspection checklist. The opening line sets its legal weight: remote pilots "may choose to use this checklist or develop their own for the operation of their specific small UAS" (unmanned aircraft system). So the list is a model, not a mandate. What it models is the shape of a thorough preflight.

The first items cover the operating environment: local weather, local airspace along with any air traffic control (ATC) coordination or communication requirements and flight restrictions, where persons and property on the surface are, and other ground hazards. Then crew briefings, if you have a crew: operating conditions, purpose of flight, potential hazards, emergency procedures, contingency procedures, who does what, and human factors using I.M.S.A.F.E. Then paperwork on hand for inspection, confirmation that every manufacturer-required component of the system is present and working as designed, a look at the Remote Pilot Operating Instructions if there are any, and a Certificate of Waiver (CoW) review with its risk assessment and mitigations if you hold one. Item 7 asks you to confirm the control station (CS) location and your takeoff, landing, emergency, and alternate landing sites are suitable.

Items 8 and 9 split by whether the flight goes over human beings. If it will not, consider whether you need lateral and vertical offsets from people to keep the operation safe, and adjust the flightpath to account for people moving. If it will, verify the aircraft is eligible for the intended category of operations over human beings, verify it is clearly labeled for that category, verify the manufacturer allowed any modification you made as described in the remote pilot operating instructions, verify the aircraft is properly configured and the right mode selected, and consider whether your operational area counts as an open-air assembly of persons. For Category 3 specifically, the flightpath must not go over an open-air assembly; outside a closed- or restricted-access site you may fly above a human being only if the operation does not maintain sustained flight over any person, unless that person is directly participating or is under a covered structure or inside a stationary vehicle; within or over a closed- or restricted-access site you may fly above a human being only if everyone inside that site is on notice that a small unmanned aircraft may fly over them.

Items 10 and 11 are about you and the aircraft's capability: put mitigations in place so you do not get distracted or lose visual line of sight (VLOS), and review aircraft performance against density altitude and wind so you can keep positive control and a clear flightpath, and a safe distance over people if that applies.

Items 12 through 20 are the hands-on inspection: visual condition of the components; airframe structure including undercarriage, flight control surfaces and linkages; registration markings displayed properly and legible; moveable control surfaces and their airframe attachment points; servo motors and their attachment points; the propulsion system including powerplants, propellers, rotors, and ducted fans; fuel for correct type and quantity; any equipment such as a camera securely attached; and adequate, properly functioning power on both the aircraft and the control unit for the intended flight. The appendix continues past item 20.

Illustration: You are hired to shoot stills of a parking lot renovation at a shopping center, and the client wants a few passes over the lot while a handful of contractors work below. Your aircraft carries a Category 3 label from the manufacturer. Working down the sample checklist, you brief your visual observer on the flightpath, hazards, and contingency procedures, confirm your registration marking is still legible after a season of hard use, and verify the aircraft is in the configuration and mode the manufacturer specifies for Category 3. At item 9f you stop. The lot is not closed or restricted — anyone can walk in from the sidewalk. So Category 3 outside a closed site means no sustained flight over any person who is not participating, not under a covered structure, and not inside a stationary vehicle. You redraw the passes so the aircraft crosses above the working contractors only in transit, never hovering or orbiting over them, and you ask the client to move the crew's staging table under the covered walkway during the shoot. You also check item 11: it is a hot afternoon at elevation, so you reduce payload weight to keep margin for positive control.

Common misreading: Two traps here. The first is treating this checklist as the legal requirement — it is a sample the appendix explicitly says you may replace with your own, and the actual rules live in the regulation sections, not in this list. The second is the reverse mistake: assuming that because it is only a sample, the substance inside it is optional too. Items like verifying registration markings, aircraft eligibility and labeling for a category of operations over human beings, and the Category 3 restrictions on sustained flight and open-air assemblies are restating binding requirements from elsewhere in the rules. Skipping the checklist is your choice; skipping those conditions is not.
