This chunk covers four weather topics from the study guide: pressure measurement, wind and obstructions, low-level wind shear, and atmospheric stability.

Pressure. The International Standard Atmosphere (ISA) is a common reference used as the basis for most aircraft performance data. Standard sea level pressure is 29.92 inches of mercury ("Hg) with a standard temperature of 59 °F (15 °C). Pressure is also reported in millibars (mb), where 1 "Hg is roughly 34 mb. Standard sea level pressure in millibars is 1,013.2 mb, and typical readings run from 950.0 to 1,040.0 mb. Surface charts, high and low pressure centers, and hurricane data use millibars. Because weather stations sit at many different elevations, each one converts its local reading to a sea level value by adding about 1 "Hg for every 1,000 feet of elevation. The chunk's example: a station 5,000 feet above sea level reading 24.92 "Hg reports 29.92 "Hg. Pressure trends matter too — rising pressure at a station generally means fair weather is approaching, while decreasing or rapidly falling pressure usually means bad weather and possibly severe storms.

Obstructions and wind. Ground topography and large buildings break up wind flow and create gusts that change direction and speed rapidly. These obstructions range from man-made structures like hangars to natural ones like mountains, bluffs, and canyons. How bad the turbulence gets depends on the size of the obstacle and the velocity of the wind. In mountainous terrain the effect is more pronounced: air flows smoothly up the windward side with upward currents, but on the leeward side it follows the terrain downward and becomes increasingly turbulent, tending to push an aircraft into the side of the mountain. Low-level wind shear. Wind shear is a sudden, drastic change in wind speed and/or direction over a very small area, producing violent updrafts and downdrafts and abrupt horizontal changes. It can happen at any altitude, but low-level wind shear is especially hazardous because the aircraft is close to the ground. It is commonly associated with passing frontal systems, thunderstorms, temperature inversions, and strong upper level winds greater than 25 knots. A tailwind that quickly becomes a headwind increases airspeed and performance; a headwind becoming a tailwind decreases both. The most severe form is a microburst, associated with convective precipitation falling into dry air at cloud base. It may show up as an intense rain shaft at the surface, but often virga at cloud base and a ring of blowing dust are the only visible clues. A typical microburst is 1–2 miles across horizontally, about 1,000 feet deep, and lasts about 5–15 minutes, producing downdrafts of up to 6,000 feet per minute and headwind losses of 30–90 knots. A small unmanned aircraft flying into one typically feels a performance-increasing headwind first, then performance-decreasing downdrafts, then a rapidly increasing tailwind — a sequence that can end in terrain impact or flight dangerously close to the ground. Wind shear is often undetected even when it is reported somewhere.

Stability. Atmospheric stability is the atmosphere's ability to resist vertical motion. In a stable atmosphere vertical movement is difficult and small disturbances dampen out. In an unstable atmosphere small vertical movements grow, producing turbulent airflow and convective activity, and instability can lead to significant turbulence, extensive vertical clouds, and severe weather.

Illustration: You plan an afternoon mapping flight over a ridge-top property. Your weather check shows pressure falling steadily through the morning, and a distant thunderstorm cell with a visible rain shaft and ragged streaks that do not reach the ground. Your launch point is on the downwind, leeward side of the ridge with a 20-knot wind blowing over the crest.

Decision: do not fly. Three things in this chunk line up against it. Falling pressure usually signals approaching bad weather and possibly severe storms. The rain shaft with virga above it is the visible clue for microburst activity, and a microburst only lasts 5–15 minutes but can put out 6,000 feet per minute of downdraft and take 30–90 knots off your headwind — more than a small unmanned aircraft can fly out of. And flying on the leeward side of a ridge in a 20-knot wind puts you in air that is following the terrain downward and growing increasingly turbulent, which tends to push an aircraft into the slope. You wait for the cell to pass and the pressure to stabilize, then reposition to the windward side.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, the 1 "Hg per 1,000 feet conversion is something weather stations do to their own readings so reports can be compared; it is not a correction you apply to a reported sea level pressure to get your local pressure. The 5,000-foot station reading 24.92 "Hg truly has 24.92 "Hg of pressure at its location — it reports 29.92 "Hg. Second, the microburst dimensions sound small, and people conclude a 1–2 mile, 1,000-foot-deep, 5–15 minute event is easy to avoid or sit out. The hazard is not its size but its intensity: 6,000 fpm down and a 30–90 knot headwind loss inside that small box, usually with nothing visible but virga and a ring of blowing dust to warn you.
