This section lists what the remote pilot in command (the person responsible for the flight) must do before every flight. There are six duties.

First, assess the operating environment, thinking about risks to people and property nearby, both on the ground and in the air. That assessment has to cover four specific things: local weather conditions; local airspace and any flight restrictions; where persons and property are on the surface; and other ground hazards.

Second, brief everyone who is directly participating in the operation. They must be told about the operating conditions, emergency procedures, contingency procedures, roles and responsibilities, and potential hazards.

Third, confirm that all control links between the ground control station and the small unmanned aircraft are working properly.

Fourth, if the aircraft is powered, confirm there is enough available power for the system to operate for the intended operational time.

Fifth, if anything is attached to or carried by the aircraft, confirm it is secure and does not adversely affect the aircraft's flight characteristics or controllability.

Sixth, if the flight will be conducted over human beings under subpart D of this part, confirm the aircraft meets the requirements of § 107.110, § 107.120(a), § 107.130(a), or § 107.140, as applicable. This chunk does not state what those sections require, so see those sections.

Illustration: You are hired to photograph a construction site. Before launching, you check the weather, look up the airspace and any restrictions for that location, walk the area to note where workers and parked cars are, and spot an unmarked trench and overhead power lines as ground hazards. You then gather your visual observer and the site foreman's safety officer, who is helping keep bystanders back, and brief them: wind and light conditions, what you will do if you lose the link, who calls a stop, and the trench and power lines. You power up, confirm the controller shows a solid link, and check that the battery has enough charge for the 12-minute pass you planned. You are mounting a small spotlight, so you tug on it to make sure it is secure and will not shift in flight. Everything checks out, so you fly. Because you are not flying over any human beings, paragraph (f) does not come into play.

Common misreading: People read (b) as a briefing for bystanders. It applies to persons directly participating in the operation, such as a visual observer or a person manipulating the controls, not to the public standing nearby. The other common slip is treating (a) as a general 'look around' step. It is four named items, and the FAA expects all four to be considered: weather, airspace and restrictions, people and property on the surface, and other ground hazards.
