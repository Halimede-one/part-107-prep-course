This chunk is the opening of the weather-and-performance chapter. It explains why air conditions matter to how well a small unmanned aircraft flies.

The key idea is density altitude: the altitude in the standard atmosphere that corresponds to a particular air density. When air density goes up, density altitude is lower and aircraft performance improves. When air density goes down, density altitude is higher and performance drops. High density altitude means thin air; low density altitude means dense air.

Three things change air density:

Pressure. Air is a gas, so it compresses and expands. Density is directly proportional to pressure at a constant temperature — double the pressure and you double the density; lower the pressure and density falls too.

Temperature. Density varies inversely with temperature at a constant pressure. Hotter air is less dense; colder air is more dense.

Humidity (moisture). Water vapor is lighter than air, so moist air is lighter than dry air. As water content rises, air becomes less dense, density altitude increases, and performance decreases. Relative humidity is the amount of water vapor in the air expressed as a percentage of the maximum the air can hold at that temperature. Warm air holds more water vapor, colder air less. Perfectly dry air is zero percent; saturated air is 100 percent. The guide says humidity alone is usually not considered an essential factor in calculating density altitude and performance, but it does contribute.

So the combinations that produce HIGH density altitude (worse performance) are high elevations, low atmospheric pressures, high temperatures, high humidity, or some mix of these. LOW density altitude (better performance) comes from lower elevations, high atmospheric pressure, low temperatures, and low humidity.

In the atmosphere both temperature and pressure drop as you go up, and those two pull density in opposite directions. But the fairly rapid pressure drop usually dominates, so pilots can expect density to decrease with altitude.

The chunk then defines performance as the aircraft's ability to do the things that make it useful. The factors most affected are takeoff and landing distance, rate of climb, ceiling, payload, range, speed, maneuverability, stability, and fuel economy.

On climb specifically: climb performance depends directly on the ability to produce excess thrust or excess power. Weight, altitude, and configuration changes all affect that excess, so they all affect climb. Add weight and the aircraft must fly at a higher angle of attack (AOA) to hold a given altitude and speed. That raises induced drag on the wings and parasite drag on the airframe. More drag needs more thrust to overcome, which leaves less reserve thrust for climbing. The chunk notes a weight change produces a twofold effect on climb performance. Increasing altitude both increases the power required and decreases the power available, so climb performance diminishes with altitude.

Illustration: A remote pilot flies the same aircraft on two jobs. In March she surveys a parking lot near sea level at 40 degrees Fahrenheit on a high-pressure morning with dry air: low elevation, high pressure, low temperature, low humidity — all the low-density-altitude conditions. The aircraft climbs briskly and handles its full camera payload without complaint.

In August she takes the same aircraft and the same payload to a mountain job site at high elevation, 95 degrees Fahrenheit, with muggy air after a thunderstorm. Now she has high elevation, high temperature, and high humidity stacked together — a high density altitude. She expects the aircraft to climb more slowly and to feel sluggish, because thin air reduces performance and because altitude itself increases the power required while decreasing the power available. Her decision: fly with a lighter payload and plan a longer, shallower climb away from obstacles, since adding weight would force a higher angle of attack, increase induced and parasite drag, and eat into the reserve thrust she needs to climb.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people reverse the direction of the term: "high density altitude" sounds like dense air, but it means the opposite — thin air and worse performance. Read it as "the air is behaving like it would at a high altitude." Second, people assume humid air is heavier because it feels heavy. The chunk says the reverse: water vapor is lighter than air, so moist air is less dense, which raises density altitude and cuts performance. Also note the pressure and temperature rules each come with a condition — density is proportional to pressure only at constant temperature, and inversely related to temperature only at constant pressure.
