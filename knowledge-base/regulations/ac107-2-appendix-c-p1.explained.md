This appendix is advisory, not a rule. It exists because remote pilots come to small Unmanned Aircraft Systems (small UAS) with very different levels of experience, so the FAA offers a chart of conditions that, if you notice them during a preflight inspection or check, may support a decision that the small unmanned aircraft is not in a condition for safe operation. The chunk is careful about two things. First, the list is explicitly open-ended — it says 'include, but are not limited to' — so these are examples, not a complete checklist. Second, spotting one of these conditions is not automatically the end of the discussion: the chunk says further inspection to identify the scope of the damage and the extent of possible repair may be necessary before flight. The chunk also points you elsewhere for two related topics: for Category 4 maintenance requirements when operating under 14 CFR part 107, see Chapter 8, paragraph 8.3.7.4, and for Category 4 record retention requirements, see Chapter 8, paragraph 8.3.7.4.1. Those paragraphs are not reproduced here, so see those sections. The three conditions shown in this part of the table are: (1) structural or skin cracking, (2) delamination of bonded surfaces, and (3) liquid or gel leakage. For the first two, the recommended action is the same — inspect further to determine the scope of the damage and whether hidden damage exists that may compromise structural integrity, then assess the need and extent of repairs needed for continued safe flight. For leakage, the action is to inspect further to find the source, because the FAA notes this condition may pose a risk of fire, producing extreme heat that negatively affects aircraft structures, aircraft performance characteristics, and flight duration; then assess the need and extent of repairs.

Illustration: During a preflight check in a parking lot, a remote pilot in command runs a finger along an arm of the airframe and feels a hairline crack in the skin near where the arm meets the body. Following the chart, the pilot does not simply note it and launch. The pilot inspects further — removing the shell to look for a longer crack or damage underneath that would compromise structural integrity — and finds the crack continues inside. Because the damage reaches a load-bearing area and no repair has been made, the pilot judges the aircraft is not in a condition for safe operation and cancels the flight. Reason: the chart lists structural or skin cracking as a condition that may support exactly that determination, and the further inspection confirmed hidden damage rather than ruling it out.

Common misreading: Two misreadings are common. One is treating Table C-1 as a complete list, so that anything not named on it must be fine; the table says the conditions 'include, but are not limited to' those listed, and sound judgment still applies to problems the table never mentions. The other is reading each listed condition as an automatic grounding. The chart's action column says to inspect further and assess what repair, if any, is needed — the finding opens an inquiry rather than closing it. Also note this appendix is best-practice guidance; the actual maintenance and record retention requirements are in the chapter paragraphs this chunk cross-references.
