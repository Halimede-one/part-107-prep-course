This paragraph of the advisory circular spells out what the remote pilot in command (remote PIC) has to do before any flight under section 107.49 — a preflight familiarization, an inspection, and crewmember briefings. The FAA points to its own publications on weather, loading and performance, emergency procedures, risk mitigation, aeronautical decision-making (ADM), and airspace, and to Appendix E for a sample preflight assessment and inspection checklist.

Before flight the remote PIC must do seven things. First, assess the operating environment, covering at least: local weather; local airspace and any flight restrictions; where non-participating people, moving vehicles, and property on the surface are; whether the aircraft is eligible for the category or categories of operations if flying over people or moving vehicles (see Chapter 8); the chance that non-participating people or moving vehicles will enter the operational area during the flight; whether the flight will be over an open-air assembly of persons; and other ground hazards. A note adds that remote pilots may not operate as a Category 1, 2, or 4 operation in sustained flight over open-air assemblies unless the operation meets the requirements of section 89.110 or section 89.115(a) — see those sections.

Second, brief everyone directly participating on operating conditions, emergency procedures, contingency procedures (including what to do if non-participating people or moving vehicles enter the operational area), each person's roles and responsibilities, and potential hazards. Third, confirm the control links between the control station (CS) and the aircraft work, and before each flight move the flight control surfaces needed for safety of flight using the CS; if any one of them does not respond correctly, no flight may be conducted until correct movement of all flight control surfaces is established. Fourth, confirm there is enough power to fly and land normally — using the manufacturer's power consumption tables, or an onboard system that warns when remaining power is getting too low. Fifth, before any flight during civil twilight or at night, confirm the anti-collision light or lights work, and consider during the preflight whether running them cuts into available power; the planned flight may need to be shortened so there is power both to keep the lights lit and to land normally. Sixth, confirm anything attached to or carried by the aircraft is secure and does not hurt the aircraft's flight characteristics or controllability. Seventh, have the required documentation available for inspection — the remote PIC's Remote Pilot Certificate, identification, aircraft registration, and Certificate of Waiver (CoW) if applicable, under section 107.7.

The advisory circular then notes these preflight steps can be folded into an overall safety risk assessment. The FAA encourages that assessment as a way of complying with the restriction on operating over any person not directly involved in the operation, unless the aircraft is eligible for an operation over people under part 107 subpart D. The assessment also helps keep the aircraft clear of other aircraft. Appendix A has more guidance on conducting one.

Illustration: A remote pilot is hired to shoot footage of a high school parking lot at 7:15 p.m. in late autumn, during civil twilight. Her preflight assessment turns up the weather, the airspace and restrictions for that location, and the position of parked cars and a few pedestrians on the sidewalk — and she also notes the school's evening class lets out at 7:30, so non-participants and moving vehicles are likely to enter the area partway through the flight. She briefs her visual observer on the planned route, the emergency procedures, and specifically what they will do if cars start rolling through: land immediately on the grass strip. She exercises the control station and watches the aircraft respond, checks the anti-collision light works, and then does the math on power: with the light drawing current, her usual 22-minute block is optimistic, so she plans a 15-minute flight to be sure she can land normally with the light still lit. She confirms her Remote Pilot Certificate, driver's license, and registration are in her bag. The decision to shorten the flight is driven by two separate requirements in this paragraph — the anti-collision light must function for a civil twilight flight, and there must be sufficient power to continue controlled flight to a normal landing.

Common misreading: Two spots trip people up. One is item 3: people read "flight control surfaces" and assume it does not apply to a multirotor with no movable surfaces. The requirement is to confirm the control links work and that the control surfaces necessary for safety of flight respond correctly to control station inputs — so the point is to verify the aircraft actually answers your control inputs before you fly, and if anything does not respond correctly, you do not fly until all of it does. The other is the anti-collision light item: it is not just a yes/no bulb check. The paragraph also requires you to consider whether the light drains power, and it may mean cutting the planned flight time short.
