Part 21 of the regulations covers the certification procedures the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) uses for aircraft products and articles — the design and production approvals that manned aircraft go through. This section says those provisions do not apply to small unmanned aircraft systems (small UAS) operated under Part 107. There is one exception: if the small unmanned aircraft system will operate over human beings in accordance with § 107.140, then Part 21 does apply. For what § 107.140 requires, see that section. In practical terms, this is the rule that tells you your drone does not need a type certificate or production certificate just to be flown under Part 107 — unless you are relying on § 107.140 to fly over people.

Illustration: You buy an off-the-shelf quadcopter and want to fly it commercially under Part 107. You check whether the aircraft holds any FAA design or production approval and find none. Under this section, that is fine: Part 21 certification procedures do not apply to your small unmanned aircraft system operated under Part 107, so you register the aircraft, hold your remote pilot certificate, and fly within the Part 107 operating rules. Later a client asks you to fly over a crowd at an outdoor event. Because operating over human beings under § 107.140 is the stated exception, you can no longer assume Part 21 is irrelevant — you go read § 107.140 before agreeing to the job.

Common misreading: People read this as saying certification never applies to Part 107 drones. The exception is written right into the sentence: it applies when the small unmanned aircraft system will operate over human beings in accordance with § 107.140. Also, this section only switches off Part 21; it does not excuse you from aircraft registration or from any other requirement in Part 107.
