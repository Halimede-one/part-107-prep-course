This section covers what happens when the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) takes back its acceptance of a declaration of compliance. A declaration of compliance is the paperwork a producer submits for a standard remote identification unmanned aircraft or a remote identification broadcast module. The rule is aimed at the person who submitted that declaration, not at remote pilots, but the result affects what equipment is backed by an accepted declaration.

Part (a) says a declaration of compliance stays under ongoing review by the Administrator, who may rescind acceptance. The listed circumstances are not exhaustive — the chunk says "including but not limited to" — and they are: the listed aircraft or broadcast module does not meet the minimum performance requirements of § 89.310 or § 89.320 (see those sections); a previously accepted declaration does not meet a requirement of this subpart; or the FAA rescinds acceptance of the means of compliance that the declaration listed. The Administrator will notify the submitter of any noncompliance issue. If the Administrator decides it is in the public interest, the Administrator may give the submitter a reasonable period of time to fix the problem before rescinding; failing to fix it is itself cause for rescission. When the decision to rescind is made, the Administrator notifies the submitter by publishing a notice of rescission in the Federal Register.

Part (b) gives a path to challenge that decision. The person who submitted the accepted declaration, or any person adversely affected by the rescission, may petition for reconsideration. The request goes to the FAA in a form and manner acceptable to the Administrator, within 60 calendar days of the date the notice of rescission was published in the Federal Register. The petition must show the petitioner is an interested party who has been adversely affected, and must also demonstrate at least one of three things: a significant additional fact not previously presented to the FAA; a material error of fact by the Administrator in the rescission decision; or that the Administrator did not correctly interpret a law, regulation, or precedent. After reviewing what the petitioner submits, the Administrator notifies the petitioner — and the declaration's submitter, if that is a different person — of the decision on whether to reinstate acceptance.

Part (c) states that part 13, subpart D, of this chapter does not apply to the procedures in paragraphs (a) and (b).

Illustration: A remote pilot buys a drone sold as a standard remote identification unmanned aircraft. Months later, the manufacturer tells customers the FAA has published a notice of rescission in the Federal Register withdrawing acceptance of the declaration of compliance for that model, because the FAA rescinded acceptance of the means of compliance the declaration relied on. The pilot is not the one who submitted the declaration, so the petition process in paragraph (b) is the manufacturer's to use — though paragraph (b)(1) also lets any person adversely affected by the rescission petition, and the pilot would have the same 60-calendar-day window running from the Federal Register publication date. For the pilot's own operating decisions, this section does not say what to do next; it only governs the rescission and reconsideration process, so the pilot looks to the operating and equipment sections of this part.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, the remediation period is not a right. Paragraph (a)(3) says the Administrator *may* provide a reasonable period of time, and only if the Administrator determines it is in the public interest — so a producer should not assume it will get a grace period to fix a problem. Second, the 60 calendar days in paragraph (b)(1) runs from the date the notice of rescission is published in the Federal Register, not from the date the submitter was first notified of a noncompliance issue under paragraph (a)(2), and not from the day the producer happens to read the notice.
