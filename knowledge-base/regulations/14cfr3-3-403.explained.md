This section bans three kinds of dishonesty in paperwork tied to the FAA provisions listed in § 3.401 (see that section for which provisions those are). First, no person may make, or cause someone else to make, a fraudulent or intentionally false statement. Second, no person may produce, reproduce, or alter a document for a fraudulent purpose. Third, no person may knowingly omit, or cause the omission of, a material fact. Each of the three prohibitions covers two categories of document, in any format: documents submitted (or, for paragraph (b), submitted or granted) under a referenced provision that consist of or relate to an acceptance, application, approval, authorization, certificate, rating, declaration, designation, qualification, record, report, request for reconsideration, or similar; and any document kept, made, or used to show compliance with a requirement under those provisions. Doing any of these things is a basis for two consequences: the Administrator may deny, suspend, modify, revoke, rescind, remove, or withdraw any acceptance, application, approval, authorization, certificate, rating, declaration, designation, qualification, request for reconsideration, or similar that the Administrator issued or granted and that the person holds; or a civil penalty may be imposed. Note the breadth here. It reaches the person who causes the false statement, not only the one who writes it. It covers any format, so a digital form, a photo, a spreadsheet, or a paper logbook all count. And an omission counts, not just an affirmative lie, when the fact left out is material.

Illustration: A remote pilot is filling out an online application for an FAA authorization and comes to a question about prior enforcement history. She has one prior action against her. She leaves the field blank, reasoning that a blank is not a lie. Under paragraph (c), knowingly leaving out a material fact in a document submitted under a referenced provision is prohibited on its own terms. The blank is the violation. She fills in the accurate answer instead. Separately, her chief pilot suggests she backdate a maintenance record kept to show compliance. That would be an alteration for fraudulent purpose under paragraph (b), and because the chief pilot asked for it, the 'cause to be made' language reaches him too. Either act could support revoking her certificate or a civil penalty under paragraph (d).

Common misreading: People read this as covering only the application for a certificate, so they assume records they keep for themselves are outside it. Each paragraph has a second subparagraph that reaches any document kept, made, or used to show compliance, which pulls in ordinary operational and maintenance records, not just submissions to the FAA. A related misreading is that only deliberate false statements count; paragraph (c) separately prohibits knowingly omitting a material fact.
