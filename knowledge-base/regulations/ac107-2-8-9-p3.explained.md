This part of the advisory circular describes what happens after the FAA decides a Declaration of Compliance (DOC) for a small unmanned aircraft should be pulled back, and what that means for remote pilots.

The normal rescission path: if a manufacturer declared an aircraft compliant with both Category 2 and Category 3 and the FAA finds it must rescind the DOC for one of those categories, the FAA first sends the applicant a notice proposing rescission. That notice states the agency's basis and gives the applicant 30 calendar-days to submit information refuting it. If the DOC holder does not show within those 30 calendar-days that the aircraft meets the applicable safety requirements, the FAA issues a notice rescinding the DOC. Besides publishing any final rescission on the FAA website, the FAA may publish notice of applicable safety defects in the Federal Register as a Notice of Availability — a notice that tells remote pilots the identified aircraft are no longer eligible to conduct operations over people.

Fixing the problem: if a DOC was rescinded over a safety issue, the aircraft can be modified so the issue is resolved, and the person may then submit a new DOC seeking acceptance of the modified aircraft. Where the FAA previously rescinded a DOC for a safety issue, the newly submitted DOC gets FAA review, and the applicant is notified once it is accepted. If the person resolved the safety issue and submitted a new or amended DOC, and the FAA determines the issue is corrected, the FAA will accept the resubmitted DOC.

Emergency rescission: normally the FAA goes through the safety issue notification process with the DOC holder before rescinding. But if the FAA determines an emergency exists and safety of persons requires immediate rescission, it may rescind without prior notification, as provided in Title 49 of the United States Code (49 U.S.C.) § 46105(c). An emergency rescission is a final agency action.

Petition for reconsideration: when a DOC is rescinded over an unresolved safety issue, the holder may petition the FAA for reconsideration by submitting a request within 60 days of the date the notice of rescission was issued. The petition must show one of three things — that information was not present in the original response, along with an explanation of why it was missing; that the FAA made a factual error in deciding to rescind; or that the FAA did not correctly interpret a law, regulation, or precedent. The FAA then issues a final decision either affirming or withdrawing the rescission. After that final agency decision, the person may appeal as provided in 49 U.S.C. § 46110. Important for pilots: a remote pilot must cease operations over people with a small unmanned aircraft listed on a rescinded DOC until a final decision has been made on the appeal.

Illustration: A real estate photographer flies a drone she has been using for Category 2 operations over pedestrians outside open houses. One morning she sees on the FAA website that the manufacturer's DOC for her model has been rescinded, and the manufacturer emails clients saying it is petitioning the FAA for reconsideration and expects to win. Her decision: stop flying that aircraft over people immediately. The chunk says a remote pilot is required to cease operations over people with an aircraft listed on a rescinded DOC until a final decision has been made on the appeal. She can still shoot the listings, but she repositions so no one is under the aircraft, and she waits for word that a new or amended DOC has been accepted, or that the rescission was withdrawn, before resuming overflight of people.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read the 30-calendar-day and 60-day windows as something a remote pilot can use — they belong to the DOC holder (usually the manufacturer), who uses 30 calendar-days to refute a proposed rescission and 60 days from issuance of the rescission notice to petition for reconsideration. A pilot gets no grace period; the duty to stop operating over people runs until the appeal is finally decided. Second, people assume a pending petition or appeal suspends the rescission. It does not. The aircraft stays ineligible for operations over people throughout, and in an emergency rescission under 49 U.S.C. § 46105(c) there is no advance notice at all.
