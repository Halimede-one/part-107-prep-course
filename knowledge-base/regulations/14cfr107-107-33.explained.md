This section says what has to happen *if* you choose to use a visual observer. Using one is optional under this rule, but once there is a visual observer on the crew, these conditions apply to the whole crew: the remote pilot in command (the person legally responsible for the flight), the person manipulating the flight controls (which may be someone other than the remote pilot in command), and the visual observer.

There are three requirements. First, all three of those people must keep effective communication with each other at all times — not just at takeoff, but continuously through the flight. Second, the remote pilot in command is responsible for making sure the visual observer can see the unmanned aircraft in the manner specified in § 107.31; see that section for what that manner of seeing requires. Third, all three must coordinate to do two things together: scan the airspace where the small unmanned aircraft is flying for any potential collision hazard, and keep track of where the aircraft is by looking at it directly.

Note the phrasing on that last item: awareness of the aircraft's position comes from direct visual observation, not from a screen or a map.

Illustration: A remote pilot in command is running a roof inspection on a commercial building. A trainee is manipulating the flight controls, and a third crew member is acting as visual observer standing at the far corner of the lot. The remote pilot in command hands out two-way radios and confirms all three can hear each other before launch. Partway through, the observer's radio battery dies. The crew has lost effective communication, which paragraph (a) requires at all times, so the correct call is to bring the aircraft down and land, swap the battery, re-establish contact with everyone, and only then continue. Pressing on with a silent observer would not meet this section.

Common misreading: People assume that adding a visual observer lets the remote pilot or the control manipulator stop watching the aircraft and focus on the camera feed. This chunk says the opposite: all three must coordinate to scan for collision hazards and must maintain awareness of the aircraft's position through direct visual observation. The observer is an addition to the crew's eyes, not a substitute for them.
