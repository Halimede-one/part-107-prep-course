This part of the advisory circular covers two things. First, it reminds remote pilots that Part 107's existing duties — conduct a preflight inspection and make sure the small unmanned aircraft is in a condition for safe operation — do not change when you fly over people. If anything, the FAA says the preflight assessment should be *more* thorough for operations over people, because those operations carry extra risk. The AC suggests the remote pilot in command (remote PIC) think about where the people are that the aircraft would fly over, the weather and other factors affecting aircraft performance, the environment and airspace of the operation, and the remote pilot operating instructions — including characteristics such as expected battery life. Second, the AC explains the role of the remote pilot operating instructions themselves, which are required for small unmanned aircraft eligible to operate in Category 2 or Category 3 of Part 107 subpart D. The FAA expects those instructions to help the remote pilot do the preflight check and confirm the aircraft is safe to fly. Beyond the items already listed in the section, the FAA does not require anything more in those instructions — but it encourages manufacturers to add more, and suggests manufacturers develop voluntary standards so instructions look consistent across products. The kinds of extra information the FAA suggests manufacturers consider are: performance, limitations and operating characteristics (operating temperature high and low limits; weather limitations including wind, precipitation, and maximum wind gusts; altitude limitations including maximum operating altitude; range limitations; power source information including endurance, power setting, and consumption levels appropriate to the propulsion system such as fuel or battery; airspeed limitations; maximum weights; prohibited maneuvers; and other limitations needed for safe operations over people); normal, abnormal, and emergency operating procedures (preflight inspection, and emergency or abnormal procedures); and weight and balance (W&B) information for the aircraft.

Illustration: You are hired to shoot footage over a crowd gathered at an outdoor market, using an aircraft the manufacturer declared eligible for Category 2 operations. Your normal preflight habit is a quick battery and propeller check. Following this guidance, you build out the assessment instead: you walk the site to see exactly where the crowd will be standing beneath your planned flight path, you check the wind forecast against what the manufacturer's remote pilot operating instructions say about wind and gusts, you confirm the airspace and the surrounding environment (buildings, trees, a nearby road), and you read the stated battery endurance so you know how long you can stay airborne before you need to land away from the crowd. The gust forecast turns out to sit above what the instructions list as a limitation, so you reschedule. Reason: the preflight duty under Part 107 still applies over people, and the extra risk of flying above a crowd is exactly why the assessment should be deeper.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read the long bulleted lists in 8.12.2.5.1 through 8.12.2.5.3 — temperature limits, wind limits, airspeed, weight and balance, and so on — as a required contents list for remote pilot operating instructions. The chunk says the opposite: the FAA does not require those additional items, it only encourages manufacturers to provide them. Second, people treat the existence of remote pilot operating instructions as a substitute for inspecting the aircraft. The instructions are meant to *assist* the remote pilot in conducting the preflight check; the obligation to inspect and to confirm the aircraft is in a condition for safe operation stays with the remote pilot.
