This is guidance from an FAA advisory circular, not a rule — it describes recommended practice, not a requirement. It walks through the rest of filling in a hazard-and-risk worksheet and then what to do before, during, and after the flight.

Finishing the worksheet: list the effect of each hazard, then do a qualitative risk assessment by estimating how likely the hazard is (probability), how bad it would be if it happened (severity), and defining the risk as the combination of those two. Then describe mitigation steps for each hazard. The AC says to develop controls that bring all risks down to an acceptable level, and if you cannot get there, the operator should not fly the small unmanned aircraft until they can. Last, write down any procedures to carry out if the hazard still occurs, including emergency and contingency procedures.

During flight (A.4.1), the AC suggests: use the assessment and inspection checklists properly, including briefing the risk assessment and mitigation steps; keep the aircraft properly configured for the category of operation; constantly re-assess risk; and have and follow procedures for changing the flight profile, including telling crewmembers.

After flight (A.4.2): do a thorough debrief, and capture lessons learned and recommendations.

Factors to weigh when assigning a risk rating (A.4.3, an initial list, not comprehensive): workload; configuration such as gross weight and center of gravity (CG); environment such as weather, air traffic control (ATC), particular airport conditions, and turbulence; the aircraft limitations the manufacturer states; and the consequence of a failure in technique, system, or structure.

Formulating mitigations (A.4.4): mitigations are actions to minimize, understand, prepare for, or respond to the causes of hazards, and they must be things the remote pilot, crewmembers, or other team members actually control. They reduce the probability of a cause, the severity of the effect, or both, and should be detailed and specific. Items to consider include setting limits on flight conditions (such as minimum weather, altitude, and minimum or maximum speed); clearly defining and briefing the criteria that would end the flight and who decides and acts; reviewing hazards and the steps that reduce their risks; and reviewing weight and balance (W&B) computations.

A.4.5 points back to writing down emergency and contingency procedures for hazards that occur despite mitigation. A.4.6 says other risk assessment tools may be used, including ones remote pilots in command (remote PICs) build themselves — what matters is that all potential hazards and risks get identified and that actions are taken to reduce risk to persons and property not involved in the operation. A.4.7 says the analysis should not stop at assigning severity and likelihood levels but should ask why those levels were chosen; that is root cause analysis, and it is the first step toward controls that actually lower risk. Simple brainstorming among crewmembers is often the most effective and affordable method, and it involves the people who will have to carry out the controls. A.4.7.1 warns against getting bogged down trying to list every hazard — focus on the hazards posing the greatest risks, and by documenting these processes a remote PIC builds a store of safety practices for future operations. A.4.8 introduces a sample hazard identification and risk assessment, which is not included in this chunk.

Illustration: A remote pilot plans a roof inspection on a windy afternoon. On the worksheet she lists the hazard 'gust-induced loss of control near the roof edge,' the effect 'aircraft strikes building or falls to the sidewalk,' and rates probability and severity to get a risk level. For mitigation she writes specific, controllable actions: fly no closer than one rotor span from the parapet, set a wind limit and abort if gusts exceed it, and station a visual observer on the sidewalk side. She also brief the crew on who calls the abort and writes a contingency procedure for a gust upset — descend to a cleared landing spot away from the building. Mid-flight the wind picks up past her stated limit, so she lands, because she pre-defined that condition as a stop-flight criterion and A.4.1 tells her to keep re-assessing risk rather than ride it out. Afterward she debriefs the crew and notes the lesson that the roofline funneled wind more than expected, so the next job gets a tighter limit.

Common misreading: Two things get misread here. First, this is advisory material — the 'should' statements are recommended practice, not regulatory requirements, so no checkride-style citation hangs on them. Second, people read 'identify all hazards and risks' as a demand for an exhaustive list; A.4.7.1 says the opposite — getting bogged down chasing every possible hazard is not the purpose, and the focus belongs on the ones posing the greatest risk. Also note that mitigations are limited to actions the crew actually controls; wishing for calmer weather is not a mitigation, but setting a wind limit is.
