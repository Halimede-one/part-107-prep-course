This section gives the remote pilot in command (the person directly responsible for and in final authority over the flight) emergency authority. If an in-flight emergency comes up that requires immediate action, the remote pilot in command may break any rule in Part 107 — but only as far as is necessary to handle that emergency. The deviation is tied to the emergency: once the situation no longer requires it, the authority to deviate no longer applies. Part (b) adds the follow-up duty. If the Administrator (the FAA, or someone acting for it) asks for one, the remote pilot in command who deviated must send a written report of that deviation to the Administrator. Note the trigger: the report is owed upon request, not automatically.

Illustration: You are flying a survey grid over a parking lot when the aircraft reports a failing battery cell and starts losing altitude faster than you can control. The only clear spot inside your line of sight is on the far side of a low hedge, where you would briefly lose visual contact with the aircraft as it settles. You take it there rather than letting it come down over the cars. That is a deviation under paragraph (a), justified because the emergency required immediate action and you went no further than needed to put the aircraft down safely. Afterward you write up what happened and keep it on file. If the FAA later asks for a written report of the deviation, you send it.

Common misreading: Two common ones. First, people read this as a blanket excuse — it is not. The deviation is permitted only 'to the extent necessary' to meet the emergency, so convenience, a tight deadline, or a bad forecast you flew into anyway will not qualify. Second, people assume a written report is always required after any emergency deviation. The chunk says the report is due upon request of the Administrator, so the obligation is triggered by that request.
