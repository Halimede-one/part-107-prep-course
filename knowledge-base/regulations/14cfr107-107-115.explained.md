This section lists what a remote pilot in command must have in order to fly a Category 2 operation. Category 2 is one of the categories of operation over human beings; this chunk does not define what Category 2 flight itself permits, so see the sections it points to.

Paragraph (a) is about the aircraft. All three things must be true of the small unmanned aircraft you use. First, it must be eligible for Category 2 operations under § 107.120(a) — see that section for what eligibility means. Second, it must be listed on a declaration of compliance that the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) has accepted, showing it as eligible for Category 2, in accordance with § 107.160. Third, it must be labeled as eligible for Category 2 operations in accordance with § 107.120(b)(1). So eligibility, the accepted declaration listing, and the label on the aircraft are three separate checks, not one.

Paragraph (b) adds a separate limit that applies regardless of category. No remote pilot in command may fly a small unmanned aircraft in sustained flight over an open-air assembly of human beings unless the operation meets the requirements of either § 89.110 or § 89.115(a). Those are remote identification sections in another part of this chapter; see them for what they require. Note the trigger words: sustained flight and open-air assemblies.

Illustration: A remote pilot is hired to shoot footage of a 5K road race. The aircraft is a model the manufacturer declared for Category 2, the FAA has accepted that declaration of compliance, and the pilot confirms the Category 2 label is present on the airframe — so paragraph (a) is satisfied. The plan calls for holding a hover above the crowd at the start corral for the duration of the countdown. That is sustained flight over an open-air assembly, so paragraph (b) applies, and the pilot checks that the operation meets § 89.110 or § 89.115(a) before flying that part of the shoot. Because it does not, the pilot reworks the shot to stay off the assembly rather than hovering over it.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people treat the aircraft's Category 2 eligibility as blanket permission to hover over crowds. It is not: paragraph (b) imposes a separate condition for sustained flight over open-air assemblies, and that condition is met through § 89.110 or § 89.115(a), not through Category 2 status. Second, people assume the manufacturer's label alone is enough. Paragraph (a) requires eligibility, the listing on an FAA-accepted declaration of compliance, and the label — all three.
