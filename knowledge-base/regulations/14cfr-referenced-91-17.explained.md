This is the alcohol and drug rule that part 107 adopts. It says no person may act, or even attempt to act, as a crewmember of a civil aircraft in four situations: within 8 hours after drinking any alcoholic beverage; while under the influence of alcohol; while using any drug that affects the person's faculties in any way contrary to safety; or while having an alcohol concentration of 0.04 or greater in a blood or breath specimen. The section defines alcohol concentration as grams of alcohol per deciliter of blood, or grams of alcohol per 210 liters of breath. Paragraph (b) says that except in an emergency, a pilot may not allow someone who appears intoxicated, or who shows by manner or physical signs that they are under the influence of drugs, to be carried in the aircraft, with an exception for a medical patient under proper care. Paragraph (c) sets out two testing duties for a crewmember. First, on request of a law enforcement officer, submit to a blood or breath alcohol test, but only when that officer is authorized under State or local law to conduct or arrange the test and is requesting it to investigate a suspected violation of a State or local law covering the same or substantially similar conduct banned by (a)(1), (a)(2), or (a)(4). Second, when the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) has a reasonable basis to believe a person violated (a)(1), (a)(2), or (a)(4), that person must, on FAA request, furnish or authorize release of the results of each test taken within 4 hours after acting or attempting to act as a crewmember that shows an alcohol concentration in blood or breath. Paragraph (d) is the drug parallel: when the Administrator has a reasonable basis to believe (a)(3) was violated, the person must, on request, furnish or authorize release of the results of each test taken within 4 hours after acting or attempting to act as a crewmember that shows the presence of any drugs in the body. Paragraph (e) says test information the Administrator obtains under (c) or (d) may be evaluated in deciding a person's qualifications for any airman certificate or possible violations of this chapter, and may be used as evidence in legal proceedings under section 602, 609, or 901 of the Federal Aviation Act of 1958. Note the three separate alcohol triggers: the 8-hour clock, being under the influence, and the 0.04 number. Each stands on its own.

Illustration: A remote pilot has two beers at a 7 p.m. client dinner on Tuesday and is scheduled to fly a roof inspection at 1 a.m. that night. Even if she feels completely clear-headed and would blow well under 0.04, she cannot act as a crewmember, because fewer than 8 hours have passed since she consumed an alcoholic beverage. She moves the flight to after 3 a.m. the next morning, or hands it to another pilot on the crew. Separately, if she had taken a prescription sedative that made her drowsy, (a)(3) would keep her on the ground regardless of the clock, because the drug affects her faculties contrary to safety.

Common misreading: Many people read the 0.04 figure as the only real test and assume that if they are below it, they are legal. The four conditions in paragraph (a) are independent. Flying within 8 hours of any drink violates (a)(1) even at 0.00, and being under the influence violates (a)(2) even below 0.04. A second misreading is that (a)(3) covers only illegal drugs; the text says any drug that affects the person's faculties in any way contrary to safety, which can include lawfully prescribed and over-the-counter medication.
