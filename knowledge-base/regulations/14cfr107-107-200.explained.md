This section sets up the waiver system for Part 107. A waiver is formal permission from the FAA (Federal Aviation Administration) Administrator to break one of the rules in this part — but only rules that are on the waivable list in § 107.205 (see that section for which rules those are). The Administrator may issue a certificate of waiver only after finding that the proposed small UAS (unmanned aircraft system) operation can be conducted safely under the terms of that certificate. The burden is on the applicant: a waiver request must include a complete description of the proposed operation plus justification showing it can be done safely under the waiver's terms. The Administrator may also add any extra limitations he or she considers necessary. Once you hold a waiver, two things are true at once. You may deviate from the regulations of this part only to the extent the certificate spells out, and you must comply with every condition and limitation written into that certificate. The waiver is not a blanket exemption; it is a narrow, conditional swap of one set of rules for another.

Illustration: A remote pilot wants to inspect a bridge at 3 a.m. so there is no traffic on the deck. Night operations and the other Part 107 rules aside, she applies for a waiver and submits a full description of the operation: the bridge location, the flight profile, the lighting on the aircraft, how she will see and avoid obstacles, who else is on the crew. The FAA grants a certificate of waiver, and the certificate says operations are authorized within a half-mile stretch of that bridge, with a visual observer stationed on the north bank. She may deviate from the waived rule only within those bounds. If she decides the next night to inspect a different bridge two miles away, or to fly without the observer, she is outside the certificate and is no longer operating under the waiver — because paragraph (d) limits the deviation to what the certificate specifies and requires compliance with its conditions.

Common misreading: People read a waiver as a general license to operate outside Part 107, or assume that because the FAA granted one waiver the same latitude applies to their other flights. It does not. The deviation reaches only as far as the certificate's own words, and the conditions attached to it are mandatory — violating a waiver condition puts you back under the original rule you thought you had escaped. A second misreading: that a thin application will do. The chunk requires a complete description and a justification that establishes safety, so an incomplete request gives the Administrator no basis to make the required finding.
