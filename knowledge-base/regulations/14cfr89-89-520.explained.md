This section sets the rules for people who build remote identification broadcast modules — the add-on devices that broadcast identification data for a drone that does not have remote ID built in. It applies to producers, not to remote pilots. After April 21, 2021, no person may produce these modules unless two things are true. First, under paragraph (a), the module must be designed and produced to meet the minimum performance requirements for broadcast modules in § 89.320 (see that section), using a means of compliance that the FAA has accepted. Second, under paragraph (b), four ongoing conditions must all be met: (1) the producer must, on request, let the Administrator inspect its facilities, its technical data, and any modules it produces, and witness any tests needed to check compliance with this subpart; (2) the producer must have independent audits done on a recurring basis, and also any time the FAA gives notice of noncompliance or potential noncompliance, to show that the modules listed under a declaration of compliance meet this subpart's requirements, and must hand over the results of all such audits to the FAA on request; (3) the producer must keep product support and notification procedures in place, and must notify both the public and the FAA of any defect or condition that makes the module stop meeting this subpart's requirements, within 15 calendar days of becoming aware of it; and (4) the producer must make installation and operating instructions available to any person operating an unmanned aircraft with that module.

Illustration: A remote pilot buys a broadcast module to attach to an older drone that has no built-in remote ID. The module arrives with no instruction sheet in the box. Because paragraph (b)(4) requires the producer to make installing and operating instructions available to any person operating an unmanned aircraft with the module, the pilot contacts the manufacturer and asks for them. The manufacturer is obligated to make those instructions available. Two months later the manufacturer posts a public notice that a firmware fault causes the module to broadcast an incorrect takeoff location — a defect that keeps the module from meeting this subpart. That notice is the paragraph (b)(3) obligation at work: the producer had to tell the public and the FAA within 15 calendar days of learning of the condition.

Common misreading: People read this as something a remote pilot has to do or verify. It is not. The inspections, audits, defect notices, and instruction duties all fall on the person responsible for producing the module. The only part that touches a pilot directly is the benefit in paragraph (b)(4) — the producer owes you the installation and operating instructions. Also note this section covers production of the modules only; it is not the rule about operating with one.
