This part of Chapter 10 covers the thinking tools behind good decisions rather than any numbered regulation. Four principles of risk management: accept no unnecessary risk (risk without a matching return); make risk decisions at the appropriate level, meaning by the person who can actually develop and put controls in place; accept risk when the benefits outweigh the dangers or costs; and integrate risk management into planning at all levels, not just preflight but through every stage of the flight. The chunk notes aviation's margin for error is thin, so all pilots should learn and use Aeronautical Decision-Making (ADM). Crew Resource Management (CRM) was built for crews, but its ideas carried over into Single-Pilot Resource Management (SRM), defined as the art and science of managing all resources available to a single pilot before and during flight to get a successful outcome. SRM bundles ADM, risk management (RM), task management (TM), automation management (AM), controlled flight into terrain (CFIT) awareness, and situational awareness (SA); its purpose is to help a pilot gather information, analyze it, and decide. Two defining elements of ADM are hazard and risk. A hazard is a real or perceived condition, event, or circumstance the pilot encounters. Risk is the pilot's assessment of the potential impact of that hazard, single or cumulative — and different pilots see hazards differently. Fitness to fly also depends on attitude, described here as a motivational predisposition to respond to people, situations, or events in a given manner. Five hazardous attitudes have been identified: anti-authority, impulsivity, invulnerability, macho, and resignation. Each has an antidote. The sequence is: recognize the thought, label it as hazardous, then state the antidote — and the antidotes should be memorized so they surface automatically. On assessing risk, the single pilot is his or her own quality control. A fatigued pilot who has flown 16 hours, asked if he or she is too tired, may answer 'no.' Goal-oriented pilots tend to deny personal limitations while adding weight to issues not germane to the mission. Helicopter emergency medical services (EMS) pilots have been known, more than other groups, to weight the patient's welfare heavily while failing to properly quantify real hazards like fatigue or weather. Without another crew member to consult, the single pilot is more vulnerable to being drawn into a hazardous position. Illustration: A remote pilot is hired to film a roof inspection and has been awake since 4 a.m. on an earlier job. Standing at the launch site at dusk, the thought arrives: 'I've flown tired plenty of times, nothing has ever happened to me.' Using the sequence in the chunk, he recognizes the thought, labels it as the invulnerability attitude, and states the antidote. Then he applies the risk principles: the benefit is finishing today instead of tomorrow, the danger is a fatigued pilot losing situational awareness over an occupied building. The benefit does not outweigh the danger, and the risk is unnecessary — the client has no deadline. He also notes he is the person who can implement the control, so the decision is his to make at this level. He reschedules for morning.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, 'accept risk when benefits outweigh dangers' gets read as permission to fly whenever the job seems important enough — but the chunk's whole point about single-pilot assessment is that goal-oriented pilots inflate intangible benefits and under-count real hazards like fatigue and weather, so the scale is easy to tilt by accident. Second, people read 'integrate risk management into planning' as a preflight-only task; the chunk says explicitly it belongs in all stages of the flight, not just the preflight planning stage.
