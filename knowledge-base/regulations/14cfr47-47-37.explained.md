This section covers the extra paperwork needed when an aircraft's most recent registration was under a foreign country's law and the owner now wants it on the United States registry under Part 47. Three things are required. First, the owner must meet the general registration requirements in §§ 47.3, 47.7, 47.8, 47.9, 47.11, 47.13, 47.15, and 47.17, as applicable — see those sections for what each one asks. Second, along with the Aircraft Registration Application, AC Form 8050-1, the owner must submit a bill of sale from the foreign seller, or other evidence the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) finds satisfactory that the owner owns the aircraft. Third, the owner must prove the old foreign registration is out of the way, and what counts as proof depends on which treaties the foreign country has ratified. If the country has ratified neither the Geneva Convention (the Convention on the International Recognition of Rights in Aircraft, 4 U.S.T. 1830) nor the Cape Town Treaty (the Convention on International Interests in Mobile Equipment as modified by the aircraft equipment Protocol), the owner only needs to show the foreign registration has ended or is invalid. If the country ratified the Geneva Convention but not the Cape Town Treaty, the owner must show the registration ended or is invalid and that every holder of a recorded right against the aircraft has been satisfied or consented to the transfer — or that ownership in the exporting country ended through a sale in execution under the Geneva Convention. If the country ratified the Cape Town Treaty and the aircraft falls under that treaty, the owner must show the registration ended or is invalid and that all interests ranking in priority have been discharged, or that the holders of those interests consented to deregistration and export. Paragraph (a)(3)(iv) adds that the Cape Town branch does not affect rights established before the treaty took effect for that country. Paragraph (b) then says what the FAA will accept as evidence that the foreign registration is terminated: either a statement from the official who has jurisdiction over that country's national aircraft registry saying the registration has ended or is invalid, giving the official's name and title and describing the aircraft by make, model, and serial number; or a final judgment or decree from a court of competent jurisdiction in that country holding that the registration has become invalid under that country's law.

Illustration: A survey company buys a large unmanned aircraft from a seller in another country, where the aircraft had been on that country's civil registry. The company wants it on the U.S. registry. It fills out AC Form 8050-1 and attaches the bill of sale from the foreign seller. That is not enough on its own. The company also has to show the foreign registration is finished. It contacts the foreign registry office and obtains a letter from the official in charge stating the registration has ended, with that official's name and title and the aircraft's make, model, and serial number — exactly what paragraph (b)(1) describes. Because the seller's country has ratified the Cape Town Treaty and the aircraft is subject to it, the company also gathers written consent to deregistration and export from the finance company holding a priority interest. With the ownership evidence and the termination evidence together, the application can go forward. If the company had sent only the bill of sale, the FAA would still be missing the proof the old registration is no longer live.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read the bill of sale as the whole job — proof of ownership and proof that the foreign registration has ended are separate requirements, and both are needed. Second, people pick whichever evidence branch looks easiest. The branch is not a choice: which of (a)(3)(i), (ii), or (iii) applies is set by which treaties the country where the aircraft was registered has ratified, and for the Cape Town branch, whether the aircraft is actually subject to that treaty.
