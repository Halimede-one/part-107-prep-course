This passage explains the Declaration of Compliance (DOC), the paperwork a manufacturer files so that a small unmanned aircraft can be used for Category 2 or Category 3 operations over people. The duty falls on the person who designs, produces, or modifies the aircraft, not on the remote pilot. That person declares compliance with the performance-based safety requirements using an FAA-accepted Means of Compliance (MOC), and submits the declaration on an electronic form at https://uasdoc.faa.gov.

In submitting a DOC the applicant declares four things: that the aircraft meets the performance-based safety requirements for the category or categories of operation through an FAA-accepted MOC; that the applicant keeps a process for notifying aircraft owners and the FAA of unsafe conditions that make those aircraft noncompliant with part 107 subpart D; that the aircraft contains no safety defects; and that the FAA may have access to its facilities and technical documents, records, or reports, or witness any test needed to determine compliance with the DOC.

Paragraph 8.9.1 lists what a completed DOC contains, which under § 107.160 (see that section) includes: the FAA-accepted MOC used; the applicant's name; physical address; email address for FAA correspondence; the aircraft make and model, and series if applicable; the serial number or range of serial numbers covered, where open-ended ranges are permitted; whether the DOC is initial or amended, and if amended the reason for resubmittal; the declaration itself (that the applicant has demonstrated the aircraft meets the injury severity limits of Category 2, Category 3, or both, and the prohibition on exposed rotating parts that would cause lacerations; has verified no safety defects; has satisfied the product support and notification process requirement; and will on request allow the Administrator to inspect its facilities and technical data); and any other information the Administrator requires.

Paragraph 8.9.2 covers resubmittal. An amended DOC must state the reason for the amendment — examples given are adding serial numbers, documenting correction of a safety defect, or fixing a misspelled applicant name or wrong address. The FAA keeps a list of FAA-accepted DOCs and publishes it on its website, so the FAA and the public can see which makes, models, and series are eligible for Category 2 and Category 3 operations over people. Paragraph 8.9.3 adds that after declaring, the applicant should make sure the aircraft keeps complying, and that submitting the DOC is an attestation that it meets part 107 subpart D requirements. Illustration: A remote pilot is asked to shoot video over a crowd gathered at a street festival and wants to fly Category 3. Before accepting, she looks up her aircraft's make, model, and series on the FAA's published list of accepted DOCs at the FAA website. Her model appears, with a serial number range that covers her aircraft. She proceeds under the Category 3 rules. Had the model not appeared on the list, she would decline the flight over people, because the manufacturer, not she, is the one who can declare the aircraft compliant through an FAA-accepted MOC — and without an accepted DOC the aircraft is not eligible for Category 2 or 3.

Common misreading: Pilots sometimes think they can file a DOC themselves to make their aircraft eligible, or that modifying an aircraft is harmless. The chunk puts the declaring duty on the person who designs, produces, or modifies the aircraft — so a pilot who modifies an aircraft steps into that role, and a modified aircraft is not covered by someone else's DOC. A second misreading is treating the DOC as a one-time filing: the applicant also has to maintain the notification process, keep the aircraft complying, and submit an amended DOC with the reason when something changes.
