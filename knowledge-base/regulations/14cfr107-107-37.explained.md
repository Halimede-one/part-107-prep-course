This section puts the small unmanned aircraft at the bottom of the right-of-way order. Paragraph (a) says your small unmanned aircraft must yield to all aircraft, airborne vehicles, and launch and reentry vehicles — every one of them, with no exceptions listed. The chunk defines what yielding means: you give way, and you may not pass over, under, or ahead of the other aircraft or vehicle unless you are well clear. Note that passing behind is not in the prohibited list, and that the restriction on passing over, under, or ahead lifts once you are well clear. Paragraph (b) is a separate prohibition: no person may operate a small unmanned aircraft so close to another aircraft as to create a collision hazard. That applies whether or not a right-of-way question ever comes up — you can be in compliance with (a) and still violate (b) by crowding another aircraft.

Illustration: You are flying a survey grid at 300 feet over a rural parcel when you hear and then see a crop duster working the next field, turning toward your area at low altitude. Under (a) you yield: you descend and hold your aircraft well off to the side, and you do not try to cut across in front of the airplane's path or slip underneath it to finish your line. The airplane has the right of way, and until you are well clear you may not pass over, under, or ahead of it. Once it has worked past and you are well clear, you resume the grid. The reason is simple: the manned aircraft gives way to nobody here, and (b) independently forbids you from operating close enough to create a collision hazard.

Common misreading: People read 'yield the right of way' as merely 'don't hit it' or 'move once it's obviously a problem.' The chunk is stricter and more specific: yielding means giving way, and it bars passing over, under, or ahead of the other aircraft unless you are well clear. Another common slip is treating (b) as just a restatement of (a). It is a separate rule — creating a collision hazard by flying too close to another aircraft is prohibited on its own terms, even in situations where no right-of-way conflict was in play.
