One person, one unmanned aircraft at a time. This applies to all three jobs on a crew: manipulating the flight controls, acting as remote pilot in command (the person responsible for the flight), and acting as visual observer (the person who watches the aircraft for the pilot). You may not hold any of those roles for more than one unmanned aircraft simultaneously. The rule is about the person, not the controller — so a single transmitter that can switch between two aircraft does not change the answer, and neither does splitting roles across different aircraft (for example, flying one while serving as visual observer for another).

Illustration: A real estate shoot calls for a high wide shot and a low orbit at the same time. The remote pilot thinks about launching a second aircraft and letting it hold a hover while she flies the first, since only one is actually moving. Under this section she cannot: she would be acting as remote pilot in command of two unmanned aircraft at the same time. The fix is a second certificated remote pilot running the second aircraft as its own operation, or flying the two shots one after the other.

Common misreading: People read it as a ban only on "flying two at once" and assume the parked or hovering one doesn't count, or that being a visual observer for a neighboring crew's aircraft while flying their own is fine. The chunk bars acting as remote pilot in command or visual observer for more than one aircraft at the same time, not just hands-on control of two.
