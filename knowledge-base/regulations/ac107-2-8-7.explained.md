This part of the advisory circular explains how an applicant can show that a small unmanned aircraft's rotating parts meet the no-laceration requirement for Categories 2 and 3. A voluntary consensus standards body is a group that writes standards using openness, balance, due process, an appeals process, and consensus, so its standards get peer reviewed; these bodies draw from a wide selection of industry participants, often including the FAA (Federal Aviation Administration). The FAA encourages industry to create more test methods and analyses so applicants have multiple ways to comply, and it offers two sample approaches. First, an aircraft can have protective safety features such as propeller guards, with testing to show those guards actually keep rotating parts from touching a person during impact and stay intact. Second, an aircraft can have fully exposed rotating parts, and testing plus analysis of the parts' size, shape, rotational speed, material, and orientation, together with the severity of injuries under impact scenarios, can show they would not lacerate human skin. The depth of the analysis should match the design: flexible propellers likely need little sophisticated testing, while sharp-edged rigid propellers (for example carbon fiber composite) driven by high torque motors likely need much more. Other compliance routes mentioned are rotors without enough momentum to lacerate normal skin, technology that stops the exposed rotating part before it would lacerate skin, and materials or designs that break away or flex on impact instead of using guards or shrouds. For Categories 2 and 3, whatever method the applicant picks must be demonstrated through an FAA-accepted means of compliance (MOC). The FAA defines a laceration as a cut that goes all the way through the skin and an abrasion as a superficial skin injury, and it uses "typical human encounter" to mean normal impacts such as an aircraft hitting a person after a loss of control, a small unmanned aircraft failure, or remote pilot error. Because the requirement is to show the aircraft would not cause lacerations, the FAA expects testing across typical human encounters and operational scenarios, not every possible encounter.

Illustration: A remote pilot runs a small roof-inspection business and also assembles his own airframes. He wants his machine eligible for Category 2 so he can plan operations over people, and his design uses rigid carbon fiber propellers with sharp leading edges on high torque motors. Reading this guidance, he sees that this is exactly the case the FAA says would likely require more sophisticated analysis or testing, and that for Categories 2 and 3 his chosen method has to be demonstrated through an FAA-accepted means of compliance. He has two realistic paths described here: fit protective propeller guards and test that they stay intact and keep the blades from contacting a person on impact, or switch to propellers made of flexible material, which the guidance says would likely not need sophisticated testing. He chooses the flexible propellers, because the lighter test burden gets him to a demonstrable no-laceration claim faster.

Common misreading: People read "propeller guards" and conclude that bolting guards onto any drone makes it Category 2 or 3 eligible. The chunk does not say that. Guards are only one possible approach, and the guidance is addressed to an applicant who must support the claim with testing showing the guards accomplish their intended function, and for Categories 2 and 3 must demonstrate the method through an FAA-accepted means of compliance. A second misreading is treating the no-laceration showing as covering every conceivable impact; the chunk says the FAA expects testing for typical human encounters, not every human encounter. A third is blurring laceration and abrasion, which this chunk separates: a laceration cuts all the way through the skin, an abrasion is superficial.
