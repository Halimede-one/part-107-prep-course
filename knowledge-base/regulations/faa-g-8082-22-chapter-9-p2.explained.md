This part of Chapter 9 covers three body-condition hazards: fatigue, dehydration, and heatstroke.

Fatigue comes in two forms. Acute fatigue is short-term. A special kind of acute fatigue is skill fatigue, which hurts performance in two ways. Timing disruption means you still go through the motions of a task, but the timing of each piece is slightly off, so the whole operation stops feeling smooth — you handle each step as a separate act instead of one flowing activity. Disruption of the perceptual field means your attention narrows to whatever is in the center of your vision, and you stop noticing things at the edges; control movements lose accuracy and smoothness at the same time. The study guide lists the most important causes of acute fatigue for a pilot: mild hypoxia (oxygen deficiency), physical stress, psychological stress, depletion of physical energy resulting from psychological stress, and sustained psychological stress. Acute fatigue can be prevented by proper diet and adequate rest and sleep — a well-balanced diet keeps the body from consuming its own tissues for energy, and rest maintains the body's store of vital energy.

Chronic fatigue extends over a long period and usually has psychological roots, though an underlying disease is sometimes responsible. Continuous high stress produces it. It is not relieved by diet and rest and usually needs treatment by a physician. It can show up as weakness, tiredness, heart palpitations, breathlessness, headaches, or irritability, and sometimes as stomach or intestinal problems and generalized aches and pains. If serious enough, it leads to emotional illness.

The operational bottom line: if you are suffering from acute fatigue, do not operate a small unmanned aircraft (UA). If fatigue sets in during a flight, no amount of training or experience can overcome its effects. Adequate rest is the only way to prevent fatigue. Avoid flying a small UA without a full night's rest, after working excessive hours, or after an especially exhausting or stressful day. If you suspect chronic fatigue, see a physician.

Dehydration is a critical loss of water from the body. Causes are hot temperatures, wind, humidity, and diuretic drinks — coffee, tea, alcohol, and caffeinated soft drinks. Common signs are headache, fatigue, cramps, sleepiness, and dizziness. The first noticeable effect is fatigue, which makes top physical and mental performance difficult if not impossible. Flying a small UA for long periods in hot summer temperatures or at high altitudes raises the risk because those conditions speed up water loss. To help prevent it, drink two to four quarts of water every 24 hours, keeping in mind that each person is physiologically different so this is only a guide. The familiar eight-glasses-a-day guide works out to 64 ounces if each glass is eight ounces, which is two quarts. If fluid is not replaced, fatigue progresses to dizziness, weakness, nausea, tingling of hands and feet, abdominal cramps, and extreme thirst.

The guide warns that thirst is an unreliable alarm. Most people become thirsty at a 1.5 quart deficit, or a loss of 2 percent of total body weight — that is when the "thirst mechanism" triggers. It arrives too late and shuts off too easily: a small amount of fluid in the mouth turns it off, which delays replacing the fluid the body actually needs. Other prevention steps are carrying a container so you can measure daily water intake, staying ahead instead of waiting for thirst (adding sport drink flavoring if plain water is unappealing), and limiting daily caffeine and alcohol since both are diuretics that increase urine production.

Heatstroke is a condition caused by any inability of the body to control its temperature. Its onset may be recognized by the symptoms of dehydration, but it has also been known to be recognized only upon complete collapse.

Illustration: A remote pilot has a July afternoon job photographing a solar farm, with six battery cycles planned over three hours in open sun. He worked a double shift the night before and got about four hours of sleep. On the drive out he has two large coffees and no water. By the third battery he notices he is flying the mission as a string of separate steps — check altitude, then check heading, then check battery — instead of one smooth scan, and he nearly misses a pickup truck entering the access road at the edge of his vision because his attention has locked onto the aircraft in the center of his view. That is skill fatigue: timing disruption plus disruption of the perceptual field. He also has a headache and feels sleepy, and he realizes the coffee was a diuretic and he has had no water in the heat. He lands and ends the operation. The reason: the study guide says a remote pilot suffering from acute fatigue should not operate a small UA, and that once fatigue occurs during a flight, no amount of training or experience can overcome it. He reschedules for a morning slot after a full night's rest, and this time he brings a container of water so he can measure his intake and stay ahead of thirst rather than waiting for it.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people assume thirst tells you when to drink. The guide says the opposite: the thirst mechanism does not trigger until you are already down about 1.5 quarts or 2 percent of body weight, and a swallow or two of fluid switches it off before you have replaced what you lost. Second, people assume a nap or a good meal fixes any fatigue. That applies to acute fatigue, which proper diet and adequate rest and sleep can prevent. Chronic fatigue is not relieved by diet and rest and usually requires treatment by a physician — so 'I'll just sleep it off' is the wrong answer for a tiredness that has lasted a long time. Also note that heatstroke may give you no warning you can act on: the guide says it has been known to be recognized only upon complete collapse, so you cannot rely on spotting symptoms in time.
