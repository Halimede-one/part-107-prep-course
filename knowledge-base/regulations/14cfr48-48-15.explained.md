This section says you may not operate a small unmanned aircraft that is eligible for registration under 49 U.S.C. 44101-44103 unless at least one of three things is true. First, the owner has registered the aircraft and marked it as this part requires. Second, the aircraft is flown exclusively in compliance with 49 U.S.C. 44809 (see that statute for what it covers) and weighs 0.55 pounds or less at takeoff, counting everything on board or otherwise attached to it. Third, the aircraft belongs to the Armed Forces of the United States. The rule is written as a prohibition on the person operating the aircraft, so the operator bears the duty to confirm one of these criteria is met before flight. Note that the second criterion has two parts joined by 'and' — the flight must meet the 44809 condition and the aircraft must be at or under 0.55 pounds at takeoff. Meeting only one of those two parts does not satisfy paragraph (b).

Illustration: You buy a 0.4-pound micro quadcopter to shoot interior photos for a real estate client, a paid job you intend to fly under your remote pilot certificate. You remember hearing that drones under 0.55 pounds don't need to be registered, so you skip registration. That is a mistake. The weight exception in paragraph (b) only applies to an aircraft operated exclusively in compliance with 49 U.S.C. 44809, and your paid client work is not being flown under that statute. Because paragraph (b) does not apply and the aircraft is not an aircraft of the Armed Forces, the only remaining path is paragraph (a): register and mark the aircraft before you fly it.

Common misreading: The most common error is reading paragraph (b) as a pure weight cutoff — 'anything 0.55 pounds or less is exempt from registration.' It is not. Paragraph (b) requires both that the aircraft be operated exclusively in compliance with 49 U.S.C. 44809 and that it weigh 0.55 pounds or less on takeoff. A second error is forgetting what counts toward that weight: the chunk says it includes everything on board or otherwise attached to the aircraft, so a clip-on camera, prop guards, or a payload all count.
