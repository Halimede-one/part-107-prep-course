This is the federal statute that carves out a narrow exception for recreational flying. It says a person may operate a small unmanned aircraft without specific certification or operating authority from the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) — meaning without a Part 107 remote pilot certificate — but only if the operation meets all eight conditions in subsection (a), and only except as provided in subsection (e) (see that subsection). All eight apply together; this is not a menu. The conditions are: (1) the flight is strictly for recreational purposes; (2) it follows the safety guidelines of a community-based organization, guidelines developed in coordination with the FAA; (3) the aircraft stays within visual line of sight of the operator or of a visual observer who is co-located with and in direct communication with the operator; (4) it does not interfere with and gives way to any manned aircraft; (5) in Class B, Class C, or Class D airspace, or within the lateral boundaries of the surface area of Class E airspace designated for an airport, the operator gets prior authorization from the Administrator or designee before flying and complies with all airspace restrictions and prohibitions; (6) in Class G airspace, the aircraft is flown from the surface to not more than 400 feet above ground level and complies with all airspace and flight restrictions and prohibitions established under this subtitle, such as special use airspace and temporary flight restrictions — except where the Administrator sets alternative altitude ceilings or where subsection (c) authorizes otherwise; (7) the operator has passed the aeronautical knowledge and safety test described in subsection (g) and keeps proof of passage available to the Administrator or law enforcement on request; and (8) the aircraft is registered and marked under chapter 441 of this title, with proof of registration available to the Administrator, a designee, or law enforcement on request. Subsection (b) is the hinge for Part 107 students: any unmanned aircraft operation that does not conform to all of subsection (a) must comply with all statutes and regulations generally applicable to unmanned aircraft and unmanned aircraft systems. Subsection (c) directs the Administrator to set up a process to approve and publicly publish the locations of fixed sites for recreational operations. For fixed sites inside Class B, C, or D airspace, or within the lateral boundaries of the surface area of Class E airspace designated for an airport, the people flying there — or the community-based organization sponsoring them — must make the site location known to the Administrator and establish a mutually agreed upon operating procedure with the air traffic control facility. The Administrator, working with sponsoring community-based organizations, must also develop a process to approve fixed-site requests that exceed the maximum altitude shown in a UAS Facility Map published by the FAA. The chunk ends mid-sentence in subsection (c)(2)(C), which begins to describe Class G operations from an approved fixed site subject to all airspace and flight restrictions and prohibitions, including special use airspace designations and temporary flight restrictions; the rest of that provision is not in this chunk.

Illustration: You hold a Part 107 remote pilot certificate and fly a drone on weekends for fun, following your model aircraft club's FAA-coordinated safety guidelines, registered and marked, within visual line of sight, under 400 feet above ground level in Class G. One Saturday a neighbor asks you to photograph their house for a real estate listing, and offers nothing in return but a thank-you. The flight is identical in every physical respect to your usual weekend flight. But it is no longer strictly for recreational purposes under condition (1), so the subsection (a) exception does not cover it. Under subsection (b), the flight must instead comply with the regulations generally applicable to unmanned aircraft — for you, that means flying it as a Part 107 operation, with the preflight, airspace authorization, and operating rules that come with it. The decision: fly it under Part 107, not under 44809, because the purpose of the flight failed one of the eight conditions.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read the 400 feet above ground level figure as a blanket recreational ceiling. In this chunk that number is stated for Class G airspace, and even there it is subject to alternative altitude ceilings set by the Administrator or to what subsection (c) authorizes; in Class B, C, D, or the surface area of Class E designated for an airport, the chunk's requirement is prior authorization plus compliance with all airspace restrictions and prohibitions. Second, people treat the eight conditions as a loose set of best practices, so that breaking one still leaves them 'a recreational flyer.' Subsection (b) says otherwise: fall outside any part of subsection (a) and the operation must meet the rules generally applicable to unmanned aircraft. Passing the knowledge and safety test in subsection (g) is one of the eight conditions, not a substitute for the other seven, and it is not a pilot certificate.
