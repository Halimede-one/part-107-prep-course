This part of the advisory circular explains how a "means of compliance" (MOC) gets approved. An MOC is a method — a test, an analysis, an inspection, or some combination — that shows a small unmanned aircraft meets the performance-based safety requirements of § 107.120(a) or § 107.130(a) (the Category 2 and Category 3 requirements for operations over people; see those sections for what they say). Any person may propose an MOC, not just the aircraft maker. The showing an MOC has to make is that the aircraft would not exceed the applicable injury severity limit upon impact with a human being, has no exposed rotating parts that would lacerate human skin, and has no safety defects. The FAA must accept the MOC first; only then can an applicant rely on it to declare compliance. Proposals go to https://uasdoc.faa.gov and must include a detailed description of the MOC plus an explanation of how applying it fulfills the safety requirements of § 107.120(a) or § 107.130(a), or both. The FAA reviews testing procedures and substantiating documents case by case. Its review criteria include, but are not limited to: whether the methods show the severity of human injury has been properly mitigated to an acceptable safety level for that aircraft category; whether tests or analyses follow accepted methods used by the medical industry, consumer safety groups, or other peer-reviewed test methods; whether the proposed MOC demands unreasonable skill from the remote pilot or unreasonable mitigations to meet the requirements; and whether the MOC addresses design features such as deployable devices (for example, parachutes) or other features independent of the aircraft that help fulfill the safety requirements. Acceptance is announced by a Notice of Availability published in the Federal Register, and the applicant is notified. An accepted MOC counts the same whether it came from a consensus standards body or from an individual person. If the FAA does not accept a proposed MOC, it tells the applicant what the issues were. The AC also warns applicants to budget extra time: coordination may involve FAA technical specialists, Chief Scientific Technical Advisors, or other government agencies, so using an MOC the FAA has already accepted is faster. Not everyone who develops an MOC also submits a declaration of compliance (DOC) — the two can come from different parties.

Illustration: A small company has designed a 1.4-pound quadcopter with a parachute that fires when the motors stop, and it wants to sell the aircraft for Category 2 operations over people. An engineer there develops a drop-test procedure and writes it up as a proposed MOC, submitting it at https://uasdoc.faa.gov with a detailed description and an explanation of how passing the test shows the aircraft meets § 107.120(a). Because the parachute is a deployable device, the submission explains how the chute contributes to limiting impact severity and shows the test method is drawn from peer-reviewed injury research rather than invented in-house. The company cannot start declaring compliance the day it mails the package in — it has to wait for the FAA to accept the MOC, which the agency signals through a Notice of Availability in the Federal Register and by notifying the company. A rival firm, wanting to move faster, instead looks for an MOC the FAA has already accepted and uses that one, since the review is already done.

Common misreading: Two things get mixed up here. First, people read "any person may propose an MOC" as meaning a remote pilot or operator can write up their own test and start flying over people. The chunk is explicit that the FAA must accept the MOC before anyone can rely on it to declare compliance. Second, an MOC is not the same thing as a declaration of compliance. The MOC is the approved method of showing the aircraft is safe enough; the DOC is the separate submission an applicant makes about a particular aircraft. The chunk notes the developer of an MOC may not be the same party that submits a DOC.
