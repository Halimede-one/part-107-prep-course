This part of Chapter 4 covers load factor, stalling speed, and the start of weight and balance. Load factor is the total load on the aircraft's structure compared with its weight: a load factor of 3 means the structure is carrying three times the aircraft's weight. Because load factors are expressed in Gs, a load factor of 3 can be called 3 Gs and a load factor of 4 can be called 4 Gs. The study guide says pilots need to understand load factors for two reasons: a pilot can impose a dangerous overload on the structure, and an increased load factor raises the stalling speed so that stalls become possible at speeds that seem safe.

In a coordinated turn at constant altitude, load factor comes from two forces: centrifugal force and weight. For any given bank angle, the rate-of-turn (ROT) varies with airspeed — the higher the speed, the slower the rate-of-turn — which compensates for the added centrifugal force so the load factor stays the same. Load factor climbs very rapidly once bank passes 45 degrees or 50 degrees. In a coordinated level turn, a 60-degree bank produces 2 Gs and an 80-degree bank produces 5.76 Gs, and the wing must produce lift equal to those load factors to hold altitude. A 90-degree banked, constant-altitude turn is not mathematically possible, so the load factor line never quite reaches the 90-degree bank line. An aircraft can be banked to 90 degrees in a coordinated turn if it is not trying to hold altitude, and an aircraft that can be held in a 90-degree banked slipping turn is capable of straight knife-edged flight. At slightly more than 80 degrees of bank, the load factor exceeds 6 Gs, which is the limit load factor of an acrobatic aircraft.

On stalls: any aircraft, within the limits of its structure, can be stalled at any airspeed. When a high enough angle of attack (AOA) is imposed, the smooth airflow over the airfoil breaks up and separates, giving an abrupt change in flight characteristics and a sudden loss of lift — a stall. Stalling speed increases in proportion to the square root of the load factor. So an aircraft with a normal unaccelerated stalling speed of 50 knots can be stalled at 100 knots by inducing 4 Gs, and if it could withstand a load factor of nine it could be stalled at 150 knots. Banking more than 72 degrees in a steep turn produces a load factor of 3; in an aircraft with a normal unaccelerated stalling speed of 45 knots, airspeed must be kept greater than 75 knots to avoid a stall. The same effect comes from a quick pull up or any maneuver producing load factors above 1 G, and this sudden loss of control — especially in a steep turn or an abrupt pull of back elevator near the ground — has caused many accidents. Because the load factor is squared as the stalling speed doubles, stalling at relatively high airspeeds can impose tremendous loads on the structure.

The chapter then turns to weight and balance. Staying within the weight and balance limits of any aircraft is critical to flight safety. Flying above the maximum weight limitation compromises structural integrity and hurts performance, and operating with the center of gravity (CG) outside the approved limits causes control difficulty. Weight and balance data must be reevaluated frequently. Weight is the force of gravity pulling a body toward the center of the Earth, the product of the body's mass and the acceleration acting on it. Lift is the only force that counteracts weight and keeps an aircraft flying, and the lift an airfoil produces is limited by airfoil design, angle of attack, airspeed, and air density. If weight exceeds the lift generated, the aircraft may not be able to fly, so loading beyond the manufacturer's recommended weight must be avoided. Any item aboard that adds total weight hurts performance, which is why manufacturers build aircraft as light as they can without giving up strength or safety. Illustration: A remote pilot is flying a fixed-wing small unmanned aircraft on a survey line and wants to reverse course quickly at the end of the run without losing altitude. The temptation is to roll into a very steep bank. Reading the numbers above, the pilot works it out: past 45 to 50 degrees the load factor climbs fast, 60 degrees of bank means 2 Gs, and more than 72 degrees means a load factor of 3. The aircraft's normal unaccelerated stall speed is 45 knots, so at a load factor of 3 the pilot must hold more than 75 knots to avoid stalling. Flying at cruise speed near 55 knots, a 72-degree bank would stall the aircraft mid-turn, low over the field, with no chance to recover. Decision: use a shallower bank and accept a wider, slower turn, because the stalling speed rises with the square root of the load factor and the aircraft does not have the speed margin for a steep one.

Common misreading: The common error is thinking a stall is only something that happens when you are flying slowly. The chunk says the opposite: an aircraft can be stalled at any airspeed within the limits of its structure, because what causes the stall is a high enough angle of attack, not a low number on the airspeed indicator. A 50-knot stall speed becomes a 100-knot stall speed at 4 Gs. A second error is reading 'load factor of 3' as 'three times the load,' when what the chunk says is that the total load on the structure is three times the aircraft's weight.
