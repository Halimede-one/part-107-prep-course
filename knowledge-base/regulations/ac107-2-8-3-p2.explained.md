This part of the advisory circular does two things: it summarizes the rules for flying over moving vehicles in two tables, and it starts walking through the four categories of operations over people.

The two tables split by location. Table 8-1 covers flights over or within a closed or restricted access site. Table 8-2 covers flights that are not over or within such a site. In both tables, flight over people who are directly participating in the operation is allowed for all four categories. The difference shows up for people who are not directly participating. Inside a closed or restricted access site, those people must be on notice for Categories 1, 2, and 3. Outside such a site, flight over non-participants in Categories 1, 2, and 3 is transit only, with no sustained flight. Category 4 is handled differently in both tables: a Category 4 eligible small unmanned aircraft may operate over human beings and moving vehicles if that is not prohibited by the operating limitations in the FAA-approved Flight Manual, or as otherwise prescribed by the Administrator.

Category 1 is defined by the aircraft itself, not by paperwork. The small unmanned aircraft must weigh 0.55 pounds (250 grams) or less at takeoff and for the whole flight, counting everything on board or attached to it. It must also have no exposed rotating parts that would lacerate human skin on impact. The remote pilot is the one responsible for confirming both of those things, on top of the existing part 107 duties such as the preflight responsibilities in Section 107.49. There are no applicant requirements for Category 1. Category 1 operations may not be flown in sustained flight over open-air assemblies unless the operation meets the requirements of Section 89.110 or Section 89.115(a) — see those sections. That prohibition can be waived.

Category 2 depends on the aircraft meeting the requirements of Section 107.120 (see that section) and being listed on an FAA-accepted declaration of compliance, or DOC. The remote pilot must confirm both that the aircraft is listed on a valid FAA-accepted DOC as Category 2 eligible and that a Category 2 label is affixed to the aircraft. The AC points to https://uasdoc.faa.gov for checking the DOC and to a visual inspection for the label. The aircraft must also have current remote pilot operating instructions covering its operation and be subject to a product support and notification process. The manufacturer side of this — submitting the DOC through an FAA-accepted means of compliance, or MOC — is described in paragraph 8.9. Category 2 carries the same open-air assembly restriction as Category 1, also waivable.

Category 3 requires the aircraft to meet the safety requirements of Section 107.130 (see that section) and, again, to be listed on an FAA-accepted DOC. The chunk cuts off at that point, so the rest of the Category 3 discussion is not included here.

Illustration: You are flying a 249-gram quadcopter with shrouded propellers over a city street to photograph a storefront. Cars are moving on the street below. The street is open to the public, so this is not a closed or restricted access site, and the drivers are not directly participating in your operation. Table 8-2 tells you the answer: as a Category 1 operation, you may only transit over those vehicles, with no sustained flight. So you plan a single crossing of the street rather than hovering above the traffic lane to frame the shot, and you reposition to a spot over the sidewalk where no non-participants are present for the hover. You also confirm for yourself that the aircraft is at or under 0.55 pounds with its battery and camera attached, and that no exposed rotating part could lacerate skin — nobody else does that check for you.

Common misreading: The most common error is treating Category 1 as a free pass because it has no paperwork. It is true that there are no applicant requirements for Category 1 and no DOC to look up, but the pilot still carries the full weight and rotating-parts determination, still owes the normal part 107 preflight duties under Section 107.49, and still faces the transit-only limit over non-participating moving vehicles outside a closed site plus the sustained-flight prohibition over open-air assemblies. A second error is reading the Category 4 column as unrestricted. The asterisk points the other way: Category 4 permission is bounded by whatever the FAA-approved Flight Manual's operating limitations say.
