This part of AC 107-2A splits the work of operations over people between the remote pilot and the person who holds the FAA-accepted Declaration of Compliance (DOC) — usually the manufacturer or a modifier.

The remote pilot's job is verification. Before flying over people, the remote pilot must determine that the aircraft is listed on an FAA-accepted DOC appropriate to the category of operations planned for that flight. The chunk says the pilot may do this by visiting the FAA's website. The FAA maintains that website listing eligible small unmanned aircraft by make, model, series (if applicable), and the category declared. The same website also flags aircraft by make, model, series (if applicable), and category that have been found noncompliant through the DOC rescission process, and the FAA publishes the final rescission there with the rescinded category named. If a DOC is rescinded over an unresolved safety issue, the applicant may petition for reconsideration or fix the aircraft and submit a new DOC that the FAA may accept.

The DOC holder's job is safety reporting and follow-through. The holder can notify the FAA electronically of a safety issue through the FAA's website. The FAA documents receipt and may inform the public, possibly sharing what the applicant said about the nature of the issue. The notification should describe the nature of the safety issue and how it may be corrected; if no corrective action has been determined yet, the holder should tell the public the nature of the issue and a plan for correcting it. The holder may advise owners whether operation over people should continue with their aircraft, given the nature of the issue. Once a fix is found, the holder can push corrective action information to owners and operators through its product support and notification system, and the FAA works with the holder to decide whether the corrective action is acceptable. After addressing the issue, the holder must run the tests, analysis, or inspections needed to satisfy the performance-based safety requirements through an FAA-accepted Means of Compliance (MOC), verify there are no safety defects, and submit a new DOC. When the FAA receives and accepts that new DOC, it appears on the FAA's website.

Recordkeeping: DOC-related records must be kept for a minimum of 2 years after the small unmanned aircraft has ceased being manufactured; alternatively, an applicant who designs or modifies a small unmanned aircraft must retain the records for 2 years after submitting the DOC. The detailed description of the MOC and the justification showing how it meets the Category 2 or 3 safety requirements must be retained for as long as the MOC remains accepted. A person who submits a DOC after modifying an aircraft must retain all supporting information used to show the aircraft meets the Category 2 or 3 safety requirements. The FAA explains why: if a safety defect appears or the FAA begins an enforcement action, this information is critical to determining the cause, scope, and severity of the defect or noncompliance, and the FAA will require all substantiating data, including data covering any modifications made. Resubmitted DOCs carry the same recordkeeping requirements.

Illustration: A remote pilot is hired to shoot overhead footage of a downtown street festival, which means flying directly over attendees. Two weeks earlier she had flown the same drone over a small crowd under Category 2. Before this flight she checks the FAA website again and finds that her exact make, model, and series now appears in the rescission listing for the category she was relying on. Decision: she does not fly over the festival crowd with that aircraft. Her responsibility under 8.10.3.2 is to confirm the aircraft is listed on an FAA-accepted DOC appropriate to the category for the intended flight, and the website — the source the AC points her to — now shows that category rescinded for her aircraft. She reworks the shot so the flight path stays clear of people, and watches for the manufacturer to correct the issue and get a new DOC accepted.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, pilots assume a box label, spec sheet, or marketing claim of "Category 2 ready" settles the question. The AC points the remote pilot to the FAA's website, because that list reflects which aircraft are currently covered and which have been rescinded — a label printed at the factory cannot. Second, pilots read the recordkeeping paragraphs as homework for themselves. Those duties fall on the person who holds the FAA-accepted DOC and on applicants who design or modify aircraft, not on the operator flying the drone. The remote pilot's duty in this chunk is verification before the flight.
