Part 107 lets you ask the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) for permission to fly outside certain rules. That permission is a Certificate of Waiver (CoW). The FAA will grant it only if the Administrator finds the proposed operation can be safely conducted under the terms of that certificate (§ 107.200). Only specific sections can be waived, and this chunk lists them: 107.25 (operation from a moving vehicle or aircraft), 107.29(a)(2) and (b) (anti-collision light for night and civil twilight operations), 107.31 (visual line of sight), 107.33 (visual observer), 107.35 (operation of multiple small unmanned aircraft systems), 107.37(a) (yielding right-of-way), 107.39 (operation over people), 107.41 (operation in certain airspace), 107.51 (operating limitations for small unmanned aircraft), and 107.145 (operations over moving vehicles). One carve-out: no waiver of 107.31 will be issued to allow carrying another person's property by aircraft for compensation or hire. You apply through the instructions and application at https://www.faa.gov/uas/. The application must fully describe the proposed operation and justify it with supporting data and documentation as needed, showing the operation can be done safely under the waiver's terms. The FAA posts Waiver Safety Explanation Guidelines to help applicants build that justification. Part 107 does not require it, but the FAA encourages applying at least 60 days before the operation starts, and the FAA will strive to finish review and adjudication within 60 days — though actual time varies with how complex the request is. How much data and analysis you need is proportional to the relief you are asking for: waiving several sections for a congested metropolitan area with heavy air traffic will likely demand far more than waiving a single section in a sparsely populated area with minimal air traffic. A granted CoW may carry specific special provisions meant to keep the operation as safe as one flown under part 107, and the FAA's website lists standard special provisions for part 107 waivers.

Illustration: A remote pilot wants to inspect a 12-mile stretch of pipeline without keeping the aircraft within visual line of sight. That is § 107.31, which appears on the waivable list, and the flight is a self-inspection rather than carrying someone else's property for compensation, so the carve-out does not block it. The pilot submits an application at faa.gov/uas describing the route, the terrain, the detect-and-avoid method, and the crew setup, using the Waiver Safety Explanation Guidelines to structure the justification. Because the corridor is sparsely populated with minimal air traffic, the supporting analysis is lighter than it would be over a city. The pilot files roughly 75 days out rather than two weeks out, since the FAA encourages at least 60 days and only strives to decide within 60 days. If granted, the pilot must then fly inside whatever special provisions the certificate attaches.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people assume any part 107 rule can be waived — only the sections listed here are subject to waiver, so there is no waiver for, say, the remote pilot certificate requirement. Second, the 60 days is widely read as a guarantee: the chunk says part 107 does not require early filing and the FAA only strives to decide within 60 days, with the real timeline varying by complexity. Plan for a decision that may take longer, and do not schedule the operation as though approval is certain.
