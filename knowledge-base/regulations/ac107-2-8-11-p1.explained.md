This part of the advisory circular explains how labeling works for the three categories of operations over people.

For Category 1, the FAA does not require any label on the aircraft. The guidance says it would be helpful for a manufacturer to mark the retail packaging with the aircraft's weight, or with a general statement that it weighs 0.55 pounds or less, and to provide information helping the pilot tell that the aircraft has no exposed rotating parts that would lacerate human skin upon impact. The FAA expects applicants to supply that kind of packaging information for easy identification, but it is not required. The burden stays with the pilot: before conducting Category 1 operations, the remote pilot must determine that the small unmanned aircraft weighs 0.55 pounds or less, including everything on board or otherwise attached to the aircraft at takeoff and throughout the whole operation, and must determine that the aircraft has no exposed rotating parts that would lacerate human skin upon impact.

For Category 2 and Category 3, a label is required. To be eligible for operations over people under either of those categories, the small unmanned aircraft must display a label stating the category or categories it is eligible for. Because Category 3 carries operating limitations, a Category 3 label also serves to remind the remote pilot of the limitations he or she must observe (the chunk cites §§ 107.120(b)(1) and 107.130(b)(1) — see those sections for the limitations themselves).

The FAA deliberately does not dictate font size, exact wording, or placement, because small unmanned aircraft vary so much in design. The label may be applied by any means as long as it is in English, legible, prominent, and permanently affixed to the aircraft before any operations over people. Examples the FAA offers are “Category 2,” “Category 3,” “Cat. 2,” or “Cat. 3.” It can be painted, etched, or permanently affixed some other way. It should be located externally where it is easily seen, and putting it on a non-critical surface will likely keep it from wearing off during normal operations.

If a Category 2 or Category 3 label is damaged, destroyed, or missing, the remote pilot in command (remote PIC) must label the aircraft before conducting operations over human beings. That replacement label must be in English, legible, prominent, and must remain on the aircraft for the duration of the operation, and it must correctly identify the category or categories the aircraft is actually eligible for.

Illustration: A remote pilot is hired to film a charity 5K and plans to fly directly over the runners. She owns two aircraft. The first is a 0.4-pound micro drone with fully enclosed prop guards; the box it came in says nothing about weight or category. She checks the spec sheet, weighs it on a kitchen scale with the battery and the small camera attached — 0.44 pounds — and confirms the rotors are shrouded so no exposed blade could cut skin. No label is needed, and the absence of one does not stop her from flying Category 1. The second aircraft is factory-labeled “Cat. 2” on its underside, but the sticker was scraped off in a hard landing last month. Before flying that one over the runners, she must apply a new label herself — in English, legible, prominent, and good for the whole flight — reading “Category 2,” because that is the category the aircraft is genuinely eligible for. A paint pen marking on a non-critical part of the shell satisfies the requirement; flying it over people with no label at all does not.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people treat the packaging statement as the authority for Category 1 — if the box says “under 0.55 lbs,” they assume they are covered. The guidance says the opposite: packaging is optional and advisory, and it is the remote pilot's responsibility to determine the weight, including everything on board or attached at takeoff and throughout the operation, and to determine there are no exposed rotating parts that would lacerate skin. Adding a heavier battery, a camera, or a payload after the box was printed can put the aircraft over the limit. Second, people read the replacement-label provision as permission to self-declare a category. It is not. The pilot may re-create a missing label, but it must correctly identify the category the aircraft is already eligible for — writing “Cat. 2” on an aircraft that was never eligible does not make it eligible.
