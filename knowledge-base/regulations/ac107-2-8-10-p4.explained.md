A Declaration of Compliance (DOC) is the manufacturer's filed statement that a small unmanned aircraft meets a category's requirements for operations over people. This paragraph answers what happens when the maker stops making or supporting that model. The answer: the DOC stays valid. Discontinuing a model does not cancel the declaration, so the aircraft may still appear on the FAA website as eligible to operate over people under the original DOC. There is one way the DOC can go away — if an unsafe condition is identified, the FAA may rescind it. After a rescission, any applicant (not only the original manufacturer) could submit a new DOC covering design changes that fix the unsafe condition, as long as that applicant can satisfy the requirements for submitting a DOC under § 107.160 (see that section for what those requirements are).

Illustration: You own a small drone that the manufacturer stopped producing two years ago and no longer supports. You want to fly it over a crowd at a park gathering. You check the FAA's website list of aircraft eligible to operate over people, and your model is still there under its original DOC. You may proceed on that basis — the model being discontinued does not strip the declaration. If instead you find the model has been removed because the FAA rescinded the DOC over an unsafe condition, you stop planning the over-people flight; that eligibility is gone until someone files a new DOC for a corrected design.

Common misreading: Two opposite errors. One is assuming a discontinued or unsupported model loses its over-people eligibility automatically — it does not; the DOC remains valid. The other is assuming a DOC is permanent — the FAA may rescind it if an unsafe condition is identified, so the FAA website listing, not the age of the purchase, is what you check.
