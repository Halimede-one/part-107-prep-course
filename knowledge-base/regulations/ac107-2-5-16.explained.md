This part of the advisory circular covers flying while impaired. Part 107 does not allow a small unmanned aircraft system (small UAS) to be operated if the remote pilot in command (remote PIC), the person manipulating the controls, or a visual observer (VO) cannot safely carry out his or her responsibilities (section 107.27). The remote PIC is the one responsible for making sure every crewmember is unimpaired before and during the operation — not just himself or herself. The guidance points out that impairment is broader than drugs and alcohol: some over-the-counter medications and some medical conditions can also reduce your ability to fly safely, and it gives antihistamines and decongestants as examples of medications that may cause drowsiness. Beyond that general safety duty, part 107 flatly prohibits a person from serving as remote PIC, VO, or other crewmember, or from manipulating the controls, if that person has consumed any alcoholic beverage within the preceding 8 hours, is under the influence of alcohol, has a blood alcohol concentration of .04 percent or greater, or is using a drug that affects his or her mental or physical capabilities (section 91.17). Paragraph 5.16.1 adds that certain medical conditions, such as epilepsy, may also create a risk, and it is the remote PIC's responsibility to determine that a medical condition is under control and that the operation can be conducted safely.

Illustration: You are the remote PIC on a morning roof inspection and you have brought along a visual observer. When the VO arrives he mentions he took an allergy pill about an hour ago and is feeling a little foggy. Nothing about alcohol applies here, but drowsiness from an antihistamine is exactly the kind of impairment this guidance describes, and as remote PIC it is your job to ensure crewmembers are not impaired. You either find a different VO or restructure the flight so it does not depend on him, because a VO who cannot reliably scan for traffic cannot carry out his responsibilities safely.

Common misreading: A common misreading is that the 8-hour window and the .04 percent figure are the whole rule, so anyone who is stone sober is automatically cleared to fly. The chunk makes clear there are two separate hurdles: the specific alcohol and drug prohibitions, and the broader requirement that nobody in the crew be unable to carry out responsibilities safely. Over-the-counter medication, fatigue-inducing cold remedies, or an uncontrolled medical condition can ground you even with zero alcohol involved. A second misreading is that each crewmember polices himself — the chunk puts the responsibility for the whole crew on the remote PIC.
