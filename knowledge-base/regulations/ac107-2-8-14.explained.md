This passage explains how the rules for flying a small unmanned aircraft system (small UAS, or drone) over people interact with night flying. The short answer: nothing about the over-people categories changes just because it is dark. The categories and their restrictions in part 107 subpart D are the same at night as in daylight. The test methods, analyses, or inspections a manufacturer or applicant uses to show the aircraft meets the performance-based safety requirements are described as "time-of-day neutral" — the same showing works for day or night. The risk mitigation measures apply equally day and night, and they place requirements on both the manufacturer of the aircraft and the remote pilot.

So if the aircraft being flown is eligible to operate in any of the over-people categories listed in part 107 subpart D, the remote pilot may fly it over human beings at night — but only by following the requirements of §§ 107.29 and 107.39 (see those sections for what they require).

The passage also adds a practical note aimed at manufacturers: when declaring an aircraft eligible for Category 2 or Category 3, a manufacturer whose aircraft is meant to fly over people at night will most likely need to account for the mass of an anti-collision light when declaring that the aircraft meets the safety requirements in § 107.120(a) or § 107.130(a). Adding the light adds weight, and that weight is part of what the safety declaration has to cover.

Illustration: A real estate photographer wants to shoot a twilight exterior of a hotel, and the shot requires a brief pass above a small group of guests on the entry drive. Her aircraft already carries a manufacturer's declaration making it eligible in one of the subpart D over-people categories. She does not go looking for a separate "night over people" category, because this guidance tells her none exists — the category and its restrictions are the same after dark. What she does do is work through the requirements of §§ 107.29 and 107.39 before flying, since the passage conditions the night-over-people flight on those sections. She also checks that the anti-collision light fitted to the aircraft is the configuration the manufacturer's eligibility declaration was based on, rather than a heavier aftermarket light she bought separately, because the declared mass matters to the safety showing.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read "the categories do not change due to conditions of night" as meaning night flight over people has no extra conditions at all. It does — the passage expressly ties the privilege to the requirements of §§ 107.29 and 107.39. Second, the anti-collision light mass sentence is often read as a rule the remote pilot must calculate. It is written as a consideration for the manufacturer when declaring Category 2 or 3 eligibility under § 107.120(a) or § 107.130(a); the pilot's concern is simply that the aircraft as flown matches what the manufacturer declared.
