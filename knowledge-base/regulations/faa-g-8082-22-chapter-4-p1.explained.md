This chapter opening explains why the remote pilot in command (PIC) must check how the aircraft is loaded before every flight. Two things matter: how much the aircraft weighs, and where that weight sits.

On weight: the manufacturer or builder sets weight and balance limits, and the study guide says those limits should be closely followed because compliance is critical to flight safety. A published maximum gross takeoff weight is not a promise the aircraft will take off safely in every condition. High elevation, high air temperature, and high humidity — together called high density altitude — can all cut takeoff and climb performance, so the load may need to be lighter. So can the launch area itself: its length, surface, slope, the surface wind, and nearby obstacles. The guide also notes weight can change during flight. In manned aircraft that is usually fuel burn, which makes the aircraft lighter and improves performance but can upset balance. In small unmanned aircraft (UA) operations, the change comes from expendable items used on board, such as a jettisonable load.

On balance: the center of gravity (CG) is the point where all the aircraft's weight can be considered concentrated. It is not a fixed mark on the airframe — it moves as weight is distributed, shifted, or expended. The manufacturer may set limits on where the CG may be. The remote PIC should work out how the CG will shift in flight and what that does to the aircraft. If the CG is not within allowable limits after loading, or will not stay within them, weight must be moved or removed before flight. Bad balance can hurt flight characteristics much the same way excess weight does.

The aerodynamic reason: weight acts at the CG, while lift acts at the center of pressure (CP). CG forward of the CP gives a nose-down pitching tendency; CP forward of the CG gives a nose-up pitching moment. Designers therefore fix the aft CG limit forward of the CP for the corresponding flight speed to keep the aircraft in equilibrium.

Lift opposes weight. In stabilized level flight, lift equal to weight means equilibrium, with no upward or downward acceleration. Less lift than weight means vertical speed decreases; more lift than weight means it increases.

Stability is the aircraft's built-in tendency to correct a disturbance and return to or continue on its original flight path — mostly a design characteristic. It significantly affects maneuverability (how easily the aircraft can be maneuvered and how well it withstands maneuvering stresses, governed by weight, inertia, size and location of flight controls, structural strength, and powerplant) and controllability (how well the aircraft responds to the pilot's control inputs for flight path and attitude, regardless of its stability).

Load factor is the proportion between lift and weight at a given bank angle, a trigonometric relationship measured in Gs. One G is the force gravity exerts on a body at rest. Any force that bends the aircraft's flight away from a straight line stresses the structure, and that force is the load factor. The guide says a course in aerodynamics is not required to get a remote pilot certificate, but a competent pilot should understand the forces acting on the aircraft, how to use them, and the aircraft's operating limitations.

Illustration: You fly a multirotor whose manufacturer lists a maximum gross takeoff weight that your airframe, battery, and a new heavier camera just barely meet. The job is in mountain country on a hot, humid August afternoon, launching from a short sloping patch of grass with trees at one end. On paper you are legal against the manufacturer's number. But elevation, temperature, and humidity together mean high density altitude, and the guide warns that the published maximum may not take off safely under all conditions. The launch area's length, slope, surface, and obstacles point the same way. You swap back to the lighter camera to cut weight before attempting the flight, because a marginal climb with trees ahead leaves you nothing in reserve. You also check the manufacturer's CG limits after the swap, since moving the camera changed where the weight sits — the CG is not a fixed point on the aircraft.

Common misreading: The common mistake is treating maximum gross takeoff weight as a guarantee: if the aircraft is at or under the number, it must be safe to fly. The chunk says the opposite — the aircraft may not always safely take off with that load under all conditions, and hot, humid, high-elevation days or a short, sloping, obstacle-lined launch area may require reducing weight below the published maximum. A second misreading is thinking weight and balance only matter at takeoff. Weight can change in flight when expendable items are used, which shifts the CG, so the remote PIC should determine in advance how the CG will move and what effect that has.
