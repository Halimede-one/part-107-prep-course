This is the opening section of Advisory Circular (AC) 107-2A, and it explains what the document is and is not. The AC gives guidance in four areas: airman (remote pilot) certification, aircraft registration and marking, aircraft airworthiness, and operating small Unmanned Aircraft Systems (UAS) in the National Airspace System (NAS). Its purpose is to help people comply with Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations (14 CFR) part 107.

Three limits on the document matter. First, the AC does not have the force and effect of law and does not bind the public. It only clarifies requirements that already exist in law or agency policy, and it is not a legal interpretation. When the AC uses a mandatory word like "must," that word is describing a statutory or regulatory requirement that already exists somewhere else — the AC itself cannot change, add to, or delete a regulatory requirement, and it cannot authorize a deviation from one.

Second, the AC does not cover every provision of part 107. It covers only the provisions where extra explanation seemed useful. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) stresses that anyone subject to part 107 must comply with every applicable provision, whether or not this AC discusses it.

Third, on privacy: part 107 operators should be aware that State and local authorities may pass privacy laws specific to UAS operations, and the FAA encourages operators to review those laws before flying. The National Telecommunications and Information Administration (NTIA) published "Voluntary Best Practices for UAS Privacy, Transparency, and Accountability," dated May 18, 2016, which describes voluntary practices for private and commercial UAS use. As the title says, these are voluntary.

Illustration: A remote pilot is hired to photograph a residential property in a city park district. He reads AC 107-2A cover to cover, finds nothing about filming near private homes, and concludes he is clear to fly. That conclusion does not follow from the AC. The AC says plainly that it does not cover every provision of part 107, so its silence is not permission — he still has to check the regulation itself for anything applicable. Separately, the AC tells him State and local authorities may have enacted privacy laws covering UAS operations, so he looks up the laws for his state and city before the flight. He also reviews the NTIA voluntary best practices document, understanding that it is voluntary guidance rather than a legal requirement.

Common misreading: Two traps. The first is treating the AC as the rule. Because the AC uses the word "must" in places, readers assume the AC is itself enforceable. It is not — the "must" is pointing at a requirement that lives in the statute or regulation, and the AC cannot create, remove, or waive any requirement. The second is reading the AC as a complete checklist for part 107. If a topic is not in the AC, that does not mean no rule applies; compliance with every applicable part 107 provision is required regardless of whether the AC discusses it.
