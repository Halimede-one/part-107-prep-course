This part of the chapter sets up the vocabulary behind aeronautical decision-making (ADM). It says human factors is a multidisciplinary field drawing on psychology, engineering, industrial design, statistics, operations research, and anthropometry. It covers three things: understanding what humans are and are not capable of, applying that understanding to how systems and services are designed and deployed, and making sure human factors principles actually get used across aviation — pilots, air traffic control (ATC), and aviation maintenance. The chunk warns that people often treat human factors as a synonym for CRM or maintenance resource management (MRM), but says human factors is broader in both knowledge base and scope. The work consists of gathering research about human abilities, limitations, and other characteristics in specific situations (flight, maintenance, stress levels, knowledge) and applying it to tool design, machines, systems, tasks, jobs, and environments so that human use is safe, comfortable, and effective.

On decision-making: understanding the decision-making process gives a pilot the foundation for building ADM and single-pilot resource management (SRM) skills. Some situations, like engine failure, demand an immediate response using established procedures, but the chunk says there is usually time during a flight to analyze changes, gather information, and assess risks before deciding. Risk management and risk intervention are described as decision-making processes that systematically identify hazards, assess the degree of risk, and determine the best course of action. The chunk lists the parts of that process: identify hazards, assess the risks, analyze the controls, make control decisions, use the controls, and monitor the results.

Three structured models are named: the 5P, the 3P using PAVE, CARE and TEAM, and the DECIDE model. All three are described as helpful to the single pilot in organizing critical decisions. The chunk only details the 3P model here. SRM is about gathering information, analyzing it, and making decisions — and the chunk stresses that learning to judge a situation and 'how to think' is harder than learning specific maneuvers. It states plainly that there is no one right answer in ADM; each pilot analyzes the situation against their own experience level, personal minimums, and current physical and mental readiness, and makes their own decision.

The 3P model is Perceive the given set of circumstances for a flight, Process by evaluating their impact on flight safety, and Perform by implementing the best course of action. The chunk then adds Evaluate, telling you to use Perceive, Process, Perform, and Evaluate as a continuous model for every aeronautical decision. It notes that risk management processing can happen in any of three timeframes, that most flight training activities fall in the 'time-critical' timeframe, and that the six risk management steps can be folded into 3P paired with three checklists: PAVE for perceiving hazards (Pilot, Aircraft, enVironment, External pressures), CARE for processing them (Consequences, Alternatives, Reality, External factors), and TEAM for performing risk management (Transfer, Eliminate, Accept, or Mitigate).

Illustration: A remote pilot is scheduled to fly a roof inspection on a commercial building at 4 p.m. She runs PAVE first. Pilot: she slept badly and has been at work since 6 a.m. Aircraft: fine, fresh batteries. enVironment: gusty wind picking up, sun low and directly behind the roofline she needs to photograph. External pressures: the client is standing in the parking lot waiting for the imagery today. That is the Perceive step. She then runs CARE. Consequences: fatigue plus gusts plus sun glare near a structure raises the chance of a control or orientation error. Alternatives: fly tomorrow at 9 a.m. with better light, or fly only the windward side today. Reality: she is tired, and wanting the job done does not change the wind. External factors: the client's schedule is real but not a safety input. Finally TEAM. She eliminates the glare problem by not flying the sunward side today, mitigates fatigue and gusts by shortening the mission, and tells the client the remaining elevation will be captured in the morning. The reason: the chunk says there is usually time to gather information and assess risk before deciding, and that each pilot decides against their own readiness level — so a legal flight can still be the wrong flight for her, today. She repeats the loop (Evaluate) once airborne, because conditions keep changing.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read 'human factors' as just another name for crew resource management or maintenance resource management; the chunk explicitly says human factors is much broader in knowledge base and scope than either. Second, people treat 3P, PAVE, CARE, and TEAM as a one-time preflight checklist to tick off before launch. The chunk calls for Perceive, Process, Perform, and Evaluate as a continuous model for every aeronautical decision you make, including decisions made mid-flight. A related trap is expecting these models to produce a single correct answer — the chunk says there is no one right answer in ADM; the models organize your thinking, they do not decide for you.
