This section gives two alternatives for drones that were not built with remote identification (remote ID) inside them. Remote ID is the requirement in § 89.105 — see that section. If your aircraft is not a standard remote identification unmanned aircraft, you can still satisfy § 89.105 by meeting everything in paragraph (a) or everything in paragraph (b). You pick one path and meet all of its conditions; you do not mix them.

Path (a) is a remote identification broadcast module — an add-on device. Four groups of conditions apply, unless the Administrator authorizes otherwise. First, equipage: the module must meet § 89.320 (see that section) and its serial number must be listed on an FAA-accepted declaration of compliance. Also, either the aircraft's Certificate of Aircraft Registration must include the module's serial number, under the applicable requirements of parts 47 and 48, or the aircraft's serial number must be given to the FAA in a notice of identification under § 89.130 before the flight. Second, operating requirements: from takeoff to shutdown the module must broadcast the § 89.315 message elements directly from the aircraft, and the person manipulating the flight controls must be able to see the aircraft at all times throughout the operation. Third, pre-flight: before takeoff that person must make sure the module is working as this subpart requires. Fourth, if the aircraft stops broadcasting the § 89.315 message elements in flight, that person must land it as soon as practicable.

Path (b) is flying with no remote identification equipment at all, which is allowed only inside an FAA-recognized identification area (FRIA). Both the aircraft and the person manipulating the flight controls must stay within the FRIA boundaries for the whole operation, and that person must be able to see the aircraft at all times throughout the operation.

Illustration: You own an older drone with no built-in remote ID. You buy a broadcast module, confirm its serial number appears on an FAA-accepted declaration of compliance, and add that serial number to the aircraft's Certificate of Aircraft Registration. Before a mapping flight you power up and verify the module is broadcasting. Ten minutes into the flight your ground display shows the module has dropped off and is no longer broadcasting. Under paragraph (a)(4) you bring the aircraft down as soon as practicable rather than finishing the last two grid lines — the rule ties your continued flight to an active broadcast, and you cannot keep flying while it is dark.

Common misreading: Two traps. First, people read the broadcast-module path as just "bolt on a module," and skip the paperwork half — the module's serial number must be on an FAA-accepted declaration of compliance, and either that serial goes on the aircraft registration or the aircraft's serial goes to the FAA under § 89.130 before you fly. Second, people assume a broadcast module lets them fly beyond visual line of sight the same way a standard remote ID aircraft might. It does not: under both paragraph (a) and paragraph (b), the person manipulating the flight controls must be able to see the aircraft at all times throughout the operation.
