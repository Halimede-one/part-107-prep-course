Objective: know the impact kinetic energy limits for Categories 2 and 3, and how the FAA-provided means of compliance applies them.

The limits are stated as injury severity. Under 107.120(a)(1), a Category 2 aircraft must not cause an injury to a person equivalent to or greater than the severity of injury caused by a transfer of 11 foot-pounds of kinetic energy upon impact from a rigid object. Under 107.130(a)(1), the Category 3 figure is 25 foot-pounds. The figures are energy limits, not weight limits: a heavier aircraft that cannot go fast may qualify, and a light aircraft that is fast may not.

AC 107-2A describes the FAA-provided means of compliance for these limits. The applicant first determines the maximum forward airspeed the aircraft can reach at full power in level flight, using a reliable method such as a GPS groundspeed indicator, a radar gun, or a tape measure and stopwatch, and then the ground impact speed from an unpowered free fall from the highest altitude the aircraft can reach, by a drop test or by a lower drop that establishes the aircraft's drag and terminal velocity. From the highest combination of these speeds that a typical failure could produce, the maximum impact kinetic energy is calculated as KE = 0.0155 × w × v², where KE is in foot-pounds, w is the aircraft's weight in pounds, and v is the maximum impact speed in feet per second. The AC's examples: a 1.0-pound aircraft with a maximum impact speed of 26 feet per second has 10.5 foot-pounds, within Category 2; the same aircraft at 40 feet per second has 24.8 foot-pounds, within Category 3 but not Category 2. Its tables give the maximum impact speeds for other weights, for example about 19 feet per second for Category 2 and 28 feet per second for Category 3 at 2.0 pounds. Any speed or altitude limiter the compliance depends on must be enabled during the tests and explained in the operating instructions. This method assumes that all of the aircraft's kinetic energy transfers to the person; an applicant who wants credit for energy-absorbing structures or a parachute needs a different, FAA-accepted means of compliance.

The remote pilot does not apply the formula. The pilot relies on the declaration of compliance, which states that an accepted means of compliance showed the aircraft within the limit.

Worked example: a manufacturer's 1.0-pound aircraft reaches 26 feet per second in the worst typical failure. At 10.5 foot-pounds it can be declared for Category 2. A faster version of the same aircraft reaching 40 feet per second, at 24.8 foot-pounds, can be declared only for Category 3, with that category's operating limits.

Common misreading: reading the categories as weight classes. The thresholds are energy transferred on impact, and speed counts as much as weight.
