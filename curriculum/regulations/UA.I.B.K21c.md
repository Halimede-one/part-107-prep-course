Objective: know the minimum flight visibility, where it is measured, and what it means.

Under 107.51(c), the minimum flight visibility, as observed from the location of the control station, must be no less than 3 statute miles. The rule defines flight visibility as the average slant distance from the control station at which prominent unlighted objects may be seen and identified by day, and prominent lighted objects may be seen and identified by night. The measurement point is the control station, where the pilot stands, not the aircraft's position and not a weather station miles away.

AC 107-2A explains how to determine it. Local aviation weather reports with current and forecast conditions are one means. When more than one reporting station is nearby, choose the closest one that best represents the terrain around the operating area. If no local aviation weather report is available, the remote pilot in command cannot operate until the required visibility can be determined by other reliable means. The aircraft cannot be operated above any cloud, and there can be no obstruction to visibility, such as smoke or a cloud, between the aircraft and the remote pilot in command.

Worked example: the report from the airport 4 miles away gives 2 statute miles in mist. The pilot waits. An hour later the report gives 5 statute miles, and the pilot confirms from the control station that distant landmarks are clearly visible. The flight can go ahead.

Common misreading: measuring visibility at the aircraft or taking it from a distant station. The rule measures it from the control station.
