Objective: know what the remote pilot in command must do, and verify, before and during a flight over people.

Flying over people adds duties to the ones every Part 107 flight carries. AC 107-2A sums them up: the remote pilot is responsible for determining that the operation is in the appropriate category for the flight, for verifying that the aircraft is properly labeled and listed on an FAA-accepted declaration of compliance where the category requires one, and for performing all the usual preflight actions. Section 107.49(f) makes the category check part of the preflight: when the flight will be over people under subpart D, the remote pilot in command must ensure that the aircraft meets 107.110, 107.120(a), 107.130(a), or 107.140, as applicable.

What the check involves depends on the category. For Category 1 the pilot determines that the aircraft weighs 0.55 pounds or less with everything on board or attached, and that it has no exposed rotating parts that would lacerate skin. For Category 2 or 3 the pilot confirms the aircraft's listing on the FAA's online list of accepted declarations of compliance, inspects the label, follows the remote pilot operating instructions, and, under 107.135, relabels the aircraft if its label is damaged, destroyed, or missing. For Category 4 the pilot flies within the operating limitations in the approved flight manual. Two definitions from FAA guidance shape every one of these flights. "Over" a person means the aircraft directly over any part of a person, however briefly: over a head, a shoulder, an extended arm, or the toes of someone lying on a beach. "Sustained flight" over an open-air assembly means hovering, flying back and forth, or circling so that the aircraft stays above some part of the assembly; a brief, one-time transit incidental to a point-to-point flight is not sustained flight.

The FAA's overview of the rule adds the standard of care. The remote pilot must take into account the aircraft's course, speed, and trajectory, including the possibility of a catastrophic failure, to determine whether it would go over or strike a non-participant, and must ensure that the aircraft poses no undue hazard if control is lost (107.19) and is not operated carelessly or recklessly (107.23). If the pilot cannot comply, the flight must not take place or must be ended at once. The preflight assessment for a flight over people should be more thorough than usual: where the people are, the weather and other performance factors, the environment and airspace, and what the operating instructions say about the aircraft, such as its expected battery life.

Worked example: a pilot is hired to film a community fair with a 1.2-pound aircraft marketed as Category 2. Before the flight the pilot finds the model on the FAA's declaration list, finds the Category 2 label on the airframe, confirms that the aircraft is broadcasting remote identification so that sustained flight over the crowd is allowed, reads the operating instructions for the required propeller guards, and briefs the crew. Without the listing or the label, the aircraft could fly near the fair but not over anyone.

Common misreading: taking the manufacturer's word for the category. The remote pilot must verify the listing and the label, and stays responsible for the category decision and for the flight.
