Objective: know when a person other than the remote pilot in command may manipulate the flight controls, and what the remote pilot in command must be able to do.

Section 107.12(a) allows a person to manipulate the flight controls of a small unmanned aircraft system in two cases: the person holds a remote pilot certificate with a small UAS rating and meets the recency requirement of 107.65, or the person is under the direct supervision of a remote pilot in command who has the ability to immediately take direct control of the flight of the aircraft. The second case is how a trainee, or a certificated pilot whose recurrent training has lapsed, may fly.

AC 107-2A describes what "the ability to immediately take direct control" can look like. One method is a "buddy box" with two control stations, where the remote pilot in command's station can override the other. Another is the remote pilot in command standing close enough to physically take the control station. A third is an automation system that the remote pilot in command can engage at once to put the aircraft into a safe mode, such as a hover, a holding pattern, or return to home. Whichever method is used, the remote pilot in command stays directly responsible for the operation under 107.19, and the person on the controls is still bound by the rules that apply to that role, such as the medical and alcohol rules. Under 107.35, neither of them may be involved in operating more than one unmanned aircraft at the same time.

Worked example: a certificated pilot lets a trainee fly a mapping pattern. The pilot stands beside the trainee holding a linked second controller set to override. This is lawful. The same pilot letting the trainee fly while the pilot drives to the next site, unable to take control, is not.

Common misreading: thinking that supervision means being somewhere on site. The test is the ability to immediately take direct control of the flight.
