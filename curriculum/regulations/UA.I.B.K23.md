Objective: know what Part 107 requires of the remote pilot in command during an automated flight.

AC 107-2A describes an automated operation as one in which the remote pilot enters a flight plan into the control station, which sends it to the autopilot on board the aircraft. During the automated flight the control inputs are made by components on the aircraft, not from the control station, and if the control link is lost the aircraft continues the programmed mission or returns home to land.

Automation changes who moves the controls, not who is responsible. Under 107.19 the remote pilot in command remains directly responsible for the operation and must have the ability to direct the aircraft to ensure compliance with Part 107. The AC spells out what that means during automated flight: the remote pilot in command must be able to change the routing or altitude, or command the aircraft to land immediately. The ability may be exercised by manual control inputs or by commands through the automation, such as climb, descend, land now, proceed to a new waypoint, enter an orbit, or return to home, and any of these may be used to avoid a hazard or to yield the right of way. Automation does not allow a person to operate more than one aircraft at the same time under 107.35, and the visual line of sight requirement of 107.31 applies to an automated flight as to any other.

Worked example: a mapping mission is flying itself along programmed lines. A helicopter approaches. The pilot commands the aircraft to descend and hold, yields the right of way, and resumes the mission once the helicopter is well clear. If the app offered no way to intervene during the mission, the operation would not meet 107.19.

Common misreading: believing that because the aircraft flies itself, the rules about the pilot do not apply. The remote pilot in command remains responsible and must retain the ability to direct the aircraft at every moment of the flight.
