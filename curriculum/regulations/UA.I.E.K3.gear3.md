Objective: know the four categories of operation over people and what separates them. This is the step-by-step version.

Step 1, the question each category answers. The rule asks one thing: if this aircraft fell on a person, how badly could it hurt them, and how do we know? Each category is a different answer.

Step 2, Category 1, the featherweight. The aircraft weighs 0.55 pounds or less, about 250 grams, and its spinning parts cannot cut skin. The weight alone makes it safe enough, so nobody has to prove anything to the FAA. No declaration, no label.

Step 3, Category 2, the tested light aircraft. Heavier than Category 1, but someone has shown that an impact would transfer no more than 11 foot-pounds of energy, that no exposed rotating part can cut skin, and that the aircraft has no safety defect. The proof is a declaration of compliance the FAA has accepted, and the aircraft carries a Category 2 label.

Step 4, Category 3, the tested heavier aircraft. Same idea as Category 2, but the impact limit is 25 foot-pounds. Because the potential harm is greater, the pilot gets fewer places to use it: never over an open-air assembly, and sustained flight over people only inside a closed- or restricted-access site where everyone has been told a drone may fly over them.

Step 5, Category 4, the certified aircraft. The aircraft has an airworthiness certificate from the FAA, like a manned aircraft, and it flies within the limits in its flight manual.

Step 6, the rule that spans all four. Sustained flight over an open-air assembly of people needs remote identification on the aircraft, and Category 3 may not do it at all.

Memory handle: 1 is light, 2 and 3 are tested with 11 and 25 foot-pounds, 4 is certified. The bigger the number, the more proof, and for 3, the fewer places.

Worked example, step by step. A pilot has a 1.2-pound aircraft with a Category 2 label and remote identification. Over a crowd at a festival? Category 2 allows sustained flight over an open-air assembly with remote identification, so yes. Same aircraft without remote identification? Then no sustained flight over the crowd. A different 4-pound aircraft labeled Category 3 over the same crowd? Never: Category 3 may not fly over open-air assemblies.
