Objective: know who must stand down for a physical or mental condition, and what the standard is.

Section 107.17 says that no person may manipulate the flight controls, or act as remote pilot in command, visual observer, or direct participant in the operation, if he or she knows or has reason to know of a physical or mental condition that would interfere with the safe operation of the small unmanned aircraft system. Part 107 requires no medical certificate. The standard is self-assessment: what the person knows, or has reason to know, before and during the operation.

AC 107-2A gives examples of conditions that would interfere: temporary or permanent loss of the dexterity needed to operate the control station; blurred vision that prevents see-and-avoid vigilance; illness or medication that degrades situational awareness, such as a medication that warns against driving or operating heavy machinery; a debilitating condition such as a migraine or moderate or severe pain; and a hearing or speaking impairment that prevents effective communication with the rest of the crew, unless the remote pilot in command arranges an alternative means, such as sign language. The AC also notes that a condition such as epilepsy may create a risk, and that it is the remote pilot's responsibility to determine that a condition is under control.

Worked example: a visual observer took an antihistamine that morning. The label warns against operating machinery, and the observer feels drowsy. The observer has reason to know of an interfering condition and must not act as visual observer. The remote pilot in command may use another observer, or fly without one while keeping the aircraft in sight personally, because a visual observer is optional.

Common misreading: thinking the rule covers only the remote pilot in command, or that it requires an FAA medical certificate. It covers everyone who takes part, and it turns on what the person knows or has reason to know.
