Objective: understand how an aircraft manufactured before the operations-over-people rule can be used over people.

The operations-over-people rule took effect in 2021, but it does not require that an aircraft be newly manufactured to qualify. Eligibility is decided by what the aircraft is, not when it was made.

For Category 1, eligibility depends only on the aircraft: it weighs 0.55 pounds or less at takeoff including everything attached, and it has no exposed rotating parts that would lacerate human skin on impact. No declaration of compliance is involved, so an aircraft made years before the rule qualifies if it meets those two conditions today.

For Category 2 and Category 3, the aircraft must be listed on an FAA-accepted declaration of compliance and labeled for the category. A declaration can be submitted after manufacture, by the manufacturer or by another person who can show the aircraft meets the category's requirements through an accepted means of compliance. So a previously manufactured aircraft becomes eligible only once a declaration covering it is accepted.

Category 4 requires an airworthiness certificate, which is a different path altogether.

Worked example: a pilot owns a 2019 camera drone that weighs 1.3 pounds. It is too heavy for Category 1. It can fly over people only if the manufacturer, or someone else, has an accepted declaration of compliance listing that model for Category 2 or 3, and the aircraft carries the category label. Without that, the pilot may not fly over people except under the general exceptions: people directly participating, or people under a covered structure or inside a stationary vehicle.

Common misreading: assuming that any drone sold before 2021 is "grandfathered" for flight over people. There is no grandfathering; the eligibility conditions apply as written.
