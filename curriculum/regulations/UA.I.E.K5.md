Objective: know that Part 107 sets no stand-off distance from people, and what decides the distance a remote pilot keeps.

Part 107 does not impose a specific stand-off distance from people. The rule it does impose is 107.39: no flight over a person who is not directly participating, not under a covered structure or inside a stationary vehicle, and not covered by a category of operation over people. "Over" means directly over any part of the person, however briefly. Everything short of that is a matter of judgment, and AC 107-2A says the remote pilot may elect to observe a minimum stand-off distance to keep the operation safe.

The AC lists what should go into that distance: the aircraft's performance, meaning its course, speed, trajectory, and maneuverability; environmental conditions such as wind and gusts, precipitation, and visibility; the conditions of the operational area, such as the location and movement of people, vessels, and vehicles, and terrain features and structures; probable failures and the pilot's ability to perform emergency maneuvers, including an emergency landing; and the pilot's own familiarity with the aircraft and ability to maneuver it. The distance is not fixed for the day. The pilot should evaluate it and adjust it as conditions change.

Worked example: a pilot filming a sculpture in a plaza sets a 50-foot stand-off from passers-by in calm air. When gusts pick up, the pilot doubles the distance, because a gust could carry the aircraft toward the people in the time it takes to react. For a hovering shot above the sculpture, the pilot asks the crowd to wait under the colonnade, which is a covered structure, so that no one is exposed.

Common misreading: believing Part 107 states a number, such as 25 feet or 100 feet, for distance from people. It does not. The pilot sets the distance from the factors above and changes it as conditions change.
