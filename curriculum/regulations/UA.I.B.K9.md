Objective: know the three periods of the day that Part 107 distinguishes, and what changes at the edges of daylight.

Part 107 divides the day into three periods. Daylight runs from official sunrise to official sunset. Civil twilight, outside Alaska, is the 30 minutes before official sunrise and the 30 minutes after official sunset; in Alaska it is the period of civil twilight defined in the Air Almanac. Night, as 14 CFR 1.1 defines it, is the time between the end of evening civil twilight and the beginning of morning civil twilight, as published in the Air Almanac and converted to local time.

During daylight, 107.29 imposes no lighting requirement. AC 107-2A notes that lighting is generally not effective for avoiding collisions by day, and that a remote pilot may still choose to fly with the anti-collision light on. At the edges of daylight the requirements change. During civil twilight the aircraft must have lighted anti-collision lighting visible for at least 3 statute miles, with a flash rate sufficient to avoid a collision. At night the same lighting is required, and the remote pilot in command must also have completed the initial knowledge test or training under 107.65 after April 6, 2021. Units 2.24 and 2.25 take each of those periods in turn.

Worked example: official sunset is 7:40 pm. A pilot may fly until 7:40 pm with no lighting. From 7:40 to 8:10 pm the flight is in civil twilight, and the anti-collision light must be on and visible for 3 statute miles. From 8:10 pm it is night, and the training condition applies as well.

Common misreading: treating "daylight" as "while I can still see well enough." The boundaries are official sunrise and sunset, with the 30-minute twilight bands, not the light level the pilot perceives.
