Objective: know the rotating-parts requirement for operations over people and the ways an aircraft can meet it.

Categories 1, 2, and 3 share the same requirement: the aircraft must not contain any exposed rotating parts that would lacerate human skin upon impact with a human being. It appears in 107.110(a)(2) for Category 1, 107.120(a)(2) for Category 2, and 107.130(a)(2) for Category 3. For Category 1 the remote pilot determines that the aircraft meets it. For Categories 2 and 3 the applicant must demonstrate it through an FAA-accepted means of compliance, and the pilot relies on the declaration of compliance.

AC 107-2A describes what the requirement means and how it can be met. The FAA distinguishes a laceration, a cut that goes all the way through the skin, from an abrasion, a superficial injury. The test is a typical human encounter, such as an impact caused by a loss of control, an aircraft failure, or pilot error, not every imaginable encounter. One way to comply is to have no exposed rotating parts at all, for example propellers inside ducts, as long as a typical impact would not expose them; a design whose structure would break and expose the propellers in an impact does not comply. Another is a protective feature such as propeller guards, tested to show that they stay intact in an impact. A third is to show by test and analysis that the exposed parts would not lacerate skin, taking account of their size, shape, rotational speed, material, and orientation: flexible propellers may need little analysis, while rigid, sharp propellers driven by high-torque motors need more. Rotors with too little momentum to cut, rotor-stop technology, and propellers that break away or flex on impact are further examples.

Worked example: a pilot's Category 1 aircraft has fitted propeller guards. The pilot checks that the guards are intact before flying over people. The same aircraft with the guards removed and rigid propellers exposed no longer plainly meets the requirement, and the pilot must not fly it over people unless satisfied, on the aircraft's design, that the propellers would not lacerate skin.

Common misreading: treating propeller guards as the rule. The rule is that no exposed rotating part would lacerate skin; guards are one way to meet it, and only if they stay in place on impact.
