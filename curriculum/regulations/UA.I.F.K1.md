Objective: know the remote identification requirement and what it takes to fly a standard remote identification aircraft.

Part 89 subpart B applies to anyone operating an unmanned aircraft that is registered or required to be registered under part 47 or part 48, and to anyone operating a foreign civil unmanned aircraft in the United States. It does not apply to operations under part 91 that are transmitting ADS-B Out. Under 89.105, after September 16, 2023, no person may operate an unmanned aircraft in the airspace of the United States unless the operation meets 89.110 or 89.115, except as the Administrator authorizes or as 89.120 provides. Since every aircraft flown under Part 107 must be registered, every Part 107 flight needs remote identification. AC 107-2A makes the point for the lightest aircraft: an aircraft of 0.55 pounds or less, which need not be registered for recreation, must comply with remote identification when it is flown under a part that requires registration.

Section 89.110 is the standard route. A standard remote identification unmanned aircraft is produced with the broadcast built in. To fly one, the operator must ensure that from takeoff to shutdown it broadcasts the message elements in 89.305, and the person on the controls must land as soon as practicable if it stops broadcasting. The aircraft must have its serial number listed on an FAA-accepted declaration of compliance, or be covered by a part 21 design or production approval and meet subpart F; its remote identification equipment must be functional and compliant from takeoff to shutdown and must not have been disabled; and its serial number must be on the aircraft's Certificate of Aircraft Registration, or, for a foreign aircraft, in a notice of identification under 89.130. The performance rules in 89.310 do part of the work for the pilot: the aircraft must test its remote identification automatically before takeoff and report the result, must not be able to take off if the equipment is not functional, must monitor it continuously in flight and report a failure, and must broadcast with a non-proprietary specification on spectrum that personal wireless devices can receive.

Worked example: a Part 107 pilot buys a new aircraft, finds its make and model on the FAA's declaration of compliance list, and registers it with its remote identification serial number. Before each flight the aircraft self-tests. During a flight the controller reports that the broadcast has failed. The pilot lands as soon as practicable, and does not fly again until the equipment works.

Common misreading: thinking that an aircraft of 0.55 pounds or less needs no remote identification under Part 107. The weight exception belongs to recreational registration; a Part 107 aircraft is registered, and therefore broadcasts.
