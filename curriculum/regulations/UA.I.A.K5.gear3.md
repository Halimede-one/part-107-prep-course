Objective: Inspection, testing, and demonstration of compliance.

This unit is about one short rule with two separate halves. We'll take them one at a time, then look at who is named in each.

**The analogy to hold onto.** Think of a roadside check. An officer can ask for two different things: *papers* (your license, the car's registration) and *access* (a look at the car itself, and at you, the driver). Those are two distinct demands, and refusing either one is its own problem. Part 107 works the same way when the FAA asks.

**Half one: make documents available.** A remote pilot in command, the owner, or the person manipulating the flight controls must, on request, make available to the FAA the remote pilot certificate with a small unmanned aircraft system (small UAS) rating, and any other document, record, or report that the regulations require to be kept. Registration is one such record. ([14 CFR 107.7](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-107.7))

Notice the second part of that list. It is not just the certificate. It is *anything the rules require you to keep*. So the question to ask yourself is never "do I have my certificate?" but "can I produce every record the rules make me keep?"

**Half two: allow testing and inspection.** The remote pilot in command, visual observer, owner, operator, or person manipulating the controls must, on request, allow the FAA to test or inspect the small unmanned aircraft system, the remote pilot in command, the person manipulating the controls, and the visual observer, to determine compliance with Part 107. ([14 CFR 107.7](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-107.7))

Read that list of what can be inspected slowly. It includes the *aircraft system* and it includes *people*: the remote pilot in command, whoever is on the controls, and the visual observer. The FAA is checking compliance, and compliance lives in both the equipment and the crew.

**Who is on the hook.** The most common misreading is that only the remote pilot in command carries these duties. Go back and count the names. Documents: remote pilot in command, owner, person manipulating the flight controls. Testing and inspection: remote pilot in command, visual observer, owner, operator, person manipulating the controls. The visual observer standing beside you is named too. ([14 CFR 107.7](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-107.7))

**A parallel set of duties for declaration holders.** Since the operations-over-people rule, a person who holds an FAA-accepted declaration of compliance has duties that mirror the two halves above: make the declaration and its supporting records available on request, and allow inspection of the facilities, technical data, and aircraft the declaration covers. Same shape, different subject matter. ([14 CFR 107.7](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-107.7))

**Walk through it.** An FAA inspector approaches a remote pilot at a construction site.

1. The inspector asks to see the pilot's certificate and the aircraft's registration. That is half one. The pilot must produce both.
2. The inspector then asks to examine the aircraft. That is half two. The pilot must allow it.
3. Suppose the pilot says no. Refusing is itself a violation, separate from anything the inspection would have found. ([14 CFR 107.7](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/section-107.7), [AC 107-2A](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_107-2A.pdf))

That last step is the one worth carrying away. The penalty for shutting the door is not avoided by having nothing to hide. The refusal stands on its own.
