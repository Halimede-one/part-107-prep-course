Objective: know the four categories of operation over people and what separates them.

A remote pilot may operate a small unmanned aircraft over people only under one of four categories, or under the general exceptions: the people are directly participating in the operation, or they are under a covered structure or inside a stationary vehicle that gives reasonable protection from a falling aircraft.

The categories are arranged by how much harm the aircraft could do on impact. Category 1 is for very light aircraft, 0.55 pounds or less, with no exposed rotating parts that could lacerate skin; it needs no declaration. Category 2 is for aircraft that would not cause injury worse than a transfer of 11 foot-pounds of kinetic energy; they need an accepted declaration of compliance and a label. Category 3 raises that limit to 25 foot-pounds, also with a declaration and label, but adds operating restrictions: no flight over open-air assemblies, and sustained flight over people only within a closed- or restricted-access site where everyone is on notice, or only over people who are participating or protected. Category 4 is for aircraft holding an airworthiness certificate, flown within the operating limits in the flight manual.

Across all categories, sustained flight over an open-air assembly of people is allowed only if the aircraft meets the remote identification requirement, and Category 3 may not fly over open-air assemblies at all.

Worked example: a news crew wants to fly over a crowd at a street festival. That is an open-air assembly. Category 3 is out. Category 1, 2, or 4 is possible only if the aircraft meets the category's eligibility and has remote identification.

Common misreading: assuming a higher category number means more permission. Category 3 allows a heavier impact but gives the pilot fewer places to fly over people.
