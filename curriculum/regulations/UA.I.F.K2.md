Objective: know the two alternatives to a standard remote identification aircraft and the conditions attached to each.

Section 89.115 gives a person operating an aircraft that is not a standard remote identification aircraft two ways to comply with 89.105. The first is a remote identification broadcast module. The module must meet the performance requirements of 89.320, its serial number must be listed on an FAA-accepted declaration of compliance, and its serial number must be on the aircraft's Certificate of Aircraft Registration, or in a notice of identification for a foreign aircraft. From takeoff to shutdown the operator must ensure that the module broadcasts the message elements of 89.315 directly from the aircraft. Before takeoff the person on the controls must ensure that the module is functioning, and if the broadcast is lost in flight that person must land as soon as practicable. And throughout the operation the person manipulating the flight controls must be able to see the aircraft at all times.

The second is an FAA-recognized identification area, a FRIA. An aircraft without any remote identification equipment may be flown only if the aircraft and the person manipulating the controls remain within the boundaries of the area throughout the operation, and the person can see the aircraft at all times. Only a community-based organization recognized by the Administrator or an educational institution may apply to establish one. AC 107-2A notes that a module may be built in by the manufacturer or be a standalone unit secured to the aircraft before takeoff, and that a module may move from one Part 107 aircraft to another, provided its serial number is moved to the second aircraft's registration first. The FAA's guidance adds that a Part 107 pilot registers each device, standard aircraft or module, separately, and that a module broadcasts the aircraft's takeoff location rather than the control station location.

Worked example: a pilot's older mapping aircraft has no built-in remote identification. The pilot buys a broadcast module listed on the FAA's declaration list, adds the module's serial number to the aircraft's registration, tests it before takeoff, and keeps the aircraft in sight for the whole flight. The same pilot could instead fly the aircraft with no equipment at all at a FRIA, staying inside its boundary.

Common misreading: treating a broadcast module as the equal of standard remote identification. The module carries a condition the standard aircraft does not: the person on the controls must be able to see the aircraft at all times.
