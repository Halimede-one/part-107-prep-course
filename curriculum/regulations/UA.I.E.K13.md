Objective: know what a declaration of compliance is, what it contains, and what happens when the FAA rescinds one.

A declaration of compliance is the record by which an applicant declares to the FAA that a small unmanned aircraft meets the requirements for Category 2 or Category 3. Under 107.160(a) it includes the applicant's name, physical address, and email address; the aircraft make, model, and series; the serial number or range of serial numbers covered; whether it is an initial or an amended declaration, and if amended, why; the FAA-accepted means of compliance used; and the applicant's declaration that the aircraft, or specific configurations of it, satisfies 107.120(a) or 107.130(a) or both through that means of compliance, has no safety defects, is covered by a product support and notification process, and may be inspected by the FAA, with any tests witnessed. When the FAA determines that the applicant has demonstrated compliance, it notifies the applicant that the declaration is accepted, and it publishes the list of accepted declarations online. For the remote pilot, the declaration is what makes the aircraft eligible: under 107.115 and 107.125 the aircraft must be listed on an FAA-accepted declaration for the category and labeled accordingly.

The FAA may rescind a declaration. Grounds under 107.160(d)(2) are that the aircraft no longer complies with 107.120(a) or 107.130(a), that the declaration was false, or that the Administrator finds an emergency related to safety. Before rescinding for an unresolved safety issue, the FAA issues a notice proposing to rescind, and the holder has 30 calendar days to submit information showing that the aircraft meets the requirements; if none comes, the FAA rescinds. In an emergency the FAA may rescind at once. The holder may petition for reconsideration within 60 days by showing a new material fact, a material factual error, or a misreading of the law. While a declaration is rescinded, no person may operate an aircraft listed on it under that category. AC 107-2A adds that a remote pilot must stop operations over people with such an aircraft until a final decision is made, and that a rescission does not ground the aircraft: it only makes the aircraft ineligible for operations over people.

Worked example: before a job, a pilot checks the FAA's list and finds that the declaration for the pilot's aircraft model was rescinded last month because of a safety defect. The pilot may still fly the aircraft under Part 107, but not over anyone, until the manufacturer corrects the defect and a new declaration is accepted.

Common misreading: thinking a rescinded declaration grounds the aircraft. It ends eligibility for operations over people and nothing more.
