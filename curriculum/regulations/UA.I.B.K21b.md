Objective: know the altitude limit, its structure exception, and how altitude is determined.

Under 107.51(b), the altitude of a small unmanned aircraft cannot be higher than 400 feet above ground level, with one exception. The aircraft may go higher if it is flown within a 400-foot radius of a structure and does not fly higher than 400 feet above the structure's immediate uppermost limit. Both conditions must hold at the same time: the lateral limit is a 400-foot radius, and the vertical limit is 400 feet above the top of the structure. The moment the aircraft leaves the 400-foot radius, the limit is 400 feet above ground level again.

Above ground level means above the ground beneath the aircraft, not above the takeoff point. AC 107-2A lists ways to determine altitude: a calibrated device that reports the aircraft's altitude above mean sea level, from which the pilot subtracts the elevation of the control station; a GPS device that reports altitude; pacing off 400 feet on the ground so that the pilot and observer recognize the distance when the aircraft is in flight; or using the known heights of terrain and structures as references. In controlled airspace, an authorization under 107.41 may set a ceiling lower than 400 feet, and that ceiling then governs.

Worked example: a pilot inspects a 1,000-foot broadcast tower. Within 400 feet of the tower, the aircraft may climb to 1,400 feet above ground level. To check a guy-wire anchor 600 feet from the tower, the pilot must descend to 400 feet above ground level before leaving the 400-foot radius.

Common misreading: thinking the structure exception allows 400 feet above the structure anywhere near it. The exception applies only inside a 400-foot radius.
