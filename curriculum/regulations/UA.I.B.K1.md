Objective: know which small unmanned aircraft must be registered, how registration works, and what it requires of the owner afterward.

Section 107.13 requires anyone operating a civil small unmanned aircraft system to comply with the registration rule in 91.203(a)(2): the aircraft must be registered. Part 48 is the streamlined online registration for small unmanned aircraft flown only inside the United States, through the FAA DroneZone website. An owner may instead register under Part 47 in the same way as a manned aircraft.

Under 48.15, no person may operate a small unmanned aircraft eligible for registration unless one of three things is true: the owner has registered and marked it under Part 48; the aircraft is flown exclusively under the recreational exception in 49 U.S.C. 44809 and weighs 0.55 pounds or less on takeoff, including everything on board or attached; or it is an aircraft of the Armed Forces. The weight exception belongs to recreational flying only. An aircraft flown under Part 107 must be registered whatever it weighs.

Registration under 48.100 covers one specific aircraft. The aircraft is registered when the applicant receives the Certificate of Aircraft Registration, and the certificate expires 3 years after its date of issue unless renewed. The fee is $5.00 per aircraft. The application asks for the owner's name, physical address, email address, and telephone number, the aircraft manufacturer and model, and, for an aircraft with standard remote identification or a remote identification broadcast module, the serial number issued by the manufacturer under Part 89.

The owner registers the aircraft in the owner's legal name. If the owner is under 13 years of age, a person who is at least 13 must register it. The holder must keep the information accurate and must update it through the online system within 14 calendar days of a change, or when the registration must be cancelled, for example on sale, destruction, or export.

A registered aircraft must display its unique identifier, normally the registration number, legibly on an external surface, affixed so that it stays on for the whole of each operation. A certificate issued under 48.105 for exclusively recreational flying covers all the owner's recreational aircraft, but the FAA's guidance is plain: an aircraft registered that way cannot be flown under Part 107.

Worked example: a photographer buys a 0.5-pound camera drone to shoot real-estate listings. It is lighter than 0.55 pounds, but the weight exception applies only to recreational flying, so the drone must be registered before its first Part 107 flight. The photographer registers it on FAA DroneZone for $5.00, receives a certificate valid for 3 years, and writes the registration number on the outside of the aircraft.

Common misreading: treating 0.55 pounds as a Part 107 threshold. It is the recreational threshold. Under Part 107 every aircraft is registered, and a recreational registration does not cover a Part 107 flight.
