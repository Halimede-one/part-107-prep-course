Objective: know how to choose an operational area so that the flight does not go over people it may not go over.

AC 107-2A names three ways a remote pilot can comply with the prohibition on operating over people. The first is to select a location where there are no people and none are expected to be present for the duration of the operation. If people are present, the pilot should have a plan to keep them clear of the operating area, for example by directing them indoors or under safe cover until the flight ends; safe cover is a structure or a stationary vehicle that protects a person if the aircraft strikes it. The second is to maintain a safe distance from people who are not directly participating. The third is to ensure that the aircraft will not be operated over any moving vehicle. In each case the pilot must consider the aircraft's course, speed, and trajectory, including the possibility of a failure, to decide whether it could go over or strike a non-participant.

Two further judgments belong to the choice of area. The pilot must assess before the flight whether the area is an open-air assembly, which the AC describes as a dense gathering of non-participants in the open, judged case by case: sporting events, concerts, parades, protests, rallies, festivals, and parks or beaches during certain events are likely examples, while families leaving a shopping center, casual sports with no spectators, small groups in a park, or people on a bike path are less likely to be. And if the operation needs a closed- or restricted-access site, the pilot is responsible for ensuring that no one enters it inadvertently or without authorization, with physical barriers such as fencing and barricades, monitoring personnel, or geographic boundaries such as rivers, canals, cliffs, and heavily wooded areas. The preflight assessment under 107.49 covers the same ground: the location of persons and property on the surface, other ground hazards, and, in the AC's expansion, people and moving vehicles not taking part and the chance that they enter the area during the flight.

Worked example: a surveyor maps a construction site that borders a public park. The surveyor schedules the flight for early morning when the park is empty, keeps the flight lines inside the site fence, plans the turns so that the aircraft never crosses the sidewalk, and briefs the site workers so that they are on notice. If a group gathers in the park during the flight, the pilot moves the flight lines or lands.

Common misreading: judging the flight by where it launches. The flight is judged by everywhere the aircraft goes and could fall, for the whole flight, including a failure.
