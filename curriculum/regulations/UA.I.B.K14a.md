Objective: know how to see and avoid other aircraft, and the other hazards a remote pilot in command must watch for.

See and avoid rests on two rules. Under 107.31 the people flying the aircraft must be able to see it throughout the flight, in part so they can observe the airspace for other air traffic or hazards. Under 107.37 the aircraft must yield to all other aircraft and must never be flown so close to one as to create a collision hazard. AC 107-2A says that to satisfy this responsibility the remote pilot in command must know the aircraft's location and flight path at all times, be aware of other aircraft, persons, and property near the operating area, maneuver to avoid a collision, and act so that other aircraft never need to maneuver around the small unmanned aircraft.

The FAA study guide describes the scanning technique. Look from right to left or from left to right. Begin at the greatest distance at which an object can be seen and move inward toward the aircraft's position. Scan an area about 30 degrees wide at each stop, hold no stop longer than 2 to 3 seconds, and overlap each field of view with the previous one by 10 degrees. The Pilot's Handbook puts the same idea in terms of eye movement: effective scanning is a series of short, regularly spaced eye movements that bring successive areas of the sky into the central visual field, each movement no more than 10 degrees and each area observed for at least 1 second. The common point is that the eyes detect while they are still, not while they sweep.

Other hazards belong to the same vigilance. The preflight assessment under 107.49 covers persons and property on the surface and other ground hazards, and AC 107-2A names weather near structures, trees, and rolling terrain, and aircraft moving on the surface of an airport.

Worked example: between glances at the controller, a pilot scans the sky in short stops of 2 to 3 seconds, overlapping each one. The pilot hears an engine, finds a light aircraft on the next stop, descends, and holds position until it has passed well clear.

Common misreading: sweeping the eyes continuously across the sky. Detection happens during the pauses. A continuous sweep misses traffic.
