Objective: understand that one aircraft can qualify for more than one category of operation over people, and what that means for the remote pilot.

The categories of operation over people (Categories 1 through 4) are eligibility classes for the aircraft, not labels tied to one aircraft forever. A single small unmanned aircraft system can be eligible for more than one category at the same time. For example, an aircraft could be light enough for Category 1 on one configuration and listed on a declaration of compliance for Category 2 in another, or be eligible for both Category 2 and Category 3 on one declaration.

What matters to the remote pilot in command is the category the flight is conducted under. The pilot chooses the category for the operation and must comply with all of that category's requirements, including the aircraft eligibility requirements and the operating limits that come with it. Being eligible for a stricter category does not let the pilot borrow the looser category's operating rules.

Worked example: an aircraft is listed for both Category 2 and Category 3. The pilot plans sustained flight over a crowd at a fair, which is an open-air assembly. Category 3 prohibits operations over open-air assemblies, so the pilot must conduct the flight under Category 2, which allows sustained flight over open-air assemblies only if the aircraft meets the remote identification requirement. The pilot chooses the category and lives with that category's limits.

Common misreading: assuming a Category 3 aircraft can do everything a Category 2 aircraft can. Category 3 allows a heavier impact but imposes stricter operating limits.
