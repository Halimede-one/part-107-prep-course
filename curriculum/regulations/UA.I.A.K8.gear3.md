Objective: Previously manufactured sUAS.

Let's take this one piece at a time. The question is narrow: you already own a small unmanned aircraft system (sUAS) that was built before the operations-over-people rule took effect in 2021. Can you fly it over people?

**Piece one: the date the aircraft was built is not part of the test.** The operations-over-people rule took effect in 2021, but it does not require that an aircraft be newly manufactured to qualify. Eligibility is decided by what the aircraft **is**, not when it was made.

**A mental picture to hold onto.** Think of flight over people as a door with four different locks on it, one for each category. Category 1's lock opens with the aircraft itself, like a key you either have in your pocket or you don't. Category 2 and Category 3 open with paperwork, like a badge someone has to issue in your name. Category 4 opens with a certificate, which is a different door entirely. The year the key was cut does not matter to any of these locks. What matters is whether the key fits, or whether the badge exists.

**Piece two: Category 1, the key in your pocket.** Category 1 eligibility depends only on the aircraft. Two conditions, both about the aircraft as it sits on the ground in front of you today:

1. It weighs **0.55 pounds or less** at takeoff, including everything attached to it.
2. It has **no exposed rotating parts** that would lacerate human skin on impact.

That is the whole test. No declaration of compliance is involved. So an aircraft made years before the rule qualifies for Category 1 if it meets those two conditions today.

**Piece three: Categories 2 and 3, the badge someone issues.** For Category 2 and Category 3, the aircraft must be listed on an FAA-accepted **declaration of compliance** and **labeled** for the category. Both parts, the listing and the label.

Here is the part that matters for an older aircraft: a declaration can be submitted *after* manufacture. It can come from the manufacturer, or from another person who can show the aircraft meets the category's requirements through an accepted means of compliance. So a previously manufactured aircraft is not locked out forever. It becomes eligible only once a declaration covering it is accepted.

**Piece four: Category 4.** Category 4 requires an airworthiness certificate. That is a different path altogether, not something an older aircraft picks up by meeting a weight or a label requirement.

**Walk one through, step by step.** A pilot owns a 2019 camera drone weighing 1.3 pounds.

- Step 1, try Category 1. Is it 0.55 pounds or less? No, it is 1.3 pounds. Category 1 is out. The 2019 build date played no role in that conclusion; the weight did.
- Step 2, try Category 2 or 3. Is that model listed on an accepted declaration of compliance, submitted by the manufacturer or someone else? And does the aircraft carry the category label? If yes to both, it may fly over people under that category.
- Step 3, if there is no accepted declaration and no label, the pilot may not fly over people, except under the general exceptions: people directly participating in the operation, or people under a covered structure or inside a stationary vehicle.

**The misreading to guard against.** Many pilots assume any drone sold before 2021 is "grandfathered" for flight over people. There is no grandfathering. The eligibility conditions apply as written, to every aircraft, whatever year it left the factory.
