Objective: Registration requirements for sUAS.

We are going to take registration apart into small pieces and set each one down on its own. By the end you will have a short checklist you can run in your head.

**Piece 1: the rule that starts it all.**

Section 107.13 says that anyone operating a civil small unmanned aircraft system must comply with the registration rule in 91.203(a)(2): the aircraft must be registered. That is the whole of the connection. Part 107 does not write its own registration rule; it reaches over and borrows the one that applies to aircraft generally.

A mental picture that helps: think of a car. Before you drive it on a public road, the car itself has a registration and a plate on the outside, and that is separate from your own driver's licence. A drone works the same way. **Your remote pilot certificate is the driver's licence. Registration belongs to the aircraft.** Two different things, each needed.

**Piece 2: two doors into registration.**

Part 48 is the streamlined online registration for small unmanned aircraft flown only inside the United States, done through the FAA DroneZone website. An owner may instead register under Part 47, in the same way as a manned aircraft. Most Part 107 pilots walk through the Part 48 door, and the rest of this lesson follows that door.

**Piece 3: who is allowed to fly without registering.**

Under 48.15, no person may operate a small unmanned aircraft eligible for registration unless one of exactly three things is true:

1. The owner has registered and marked it under Part 48; or
2. the aircraft is flown **exclusively** under the recreational exception in 49 U.S.C. 44809 **and** weighs 0.55 pounds or less on takeoff, including everything on board or attached; or
3. it is an aircraft of the Armed Forces.

Read item 2 slowly, because it has two conditions joined by *and*. Recreational flying only, and 0.55 pounds or less on takeoff with everything on board or attached. Fail either half and the exception does not apply.

**Piece 4: the trap in that 0.55 pounds.**

This is the single most common misreading, so let us reason it out step by step.

- Step 1. The weight figure sits inside the recreational item of the list.
- Step 2. A Part 107 flight is not a flight under the recreational exception.
- Step 3. So the weight figure never reaches a Part 107 flight at all.
- Step 4. Therefore an aircraft flown under Part 107 must be registered **whatever it weighs**.

**Piece 5: what one registration buys you.**

Registration under 48.100 covers one specific aircraft. The aircraft is registered at the moment the applicant receives the Certificate of Aircraft Registration, and that certificate expires **3 years** after its date of issue unless it is renewed. The fee is **$5.00 per aircraft**.

**Piece 6: what the application asks for.**

Four things about you, two about the machine, and one more if it broadcasts its identity:

- your name, physical address, email address, and telephone number;
- the aircraft manufacturer and model;
- for an aircraft with standard remote identification or a remote identification broadcast module, the serial number issued by the manufacturer under Part 89.

**Piece 7: whose name goes on it.**

The aircraft is registered in the owner's legal name. If the owner is under 13 years of age, a person who is at least **13** must register it.

**Piece 8: registration is not finished when the certificate arrives.**

The holder must keep the information accurate. A change must be updated through the online system within **14 calendar days**, and the same applies when the registration has to be cancelled, for example on sale, destruction, or export.

**Piece 9: the mark on the outside.**

A registered aircraft must display its unique identifier, normally the registration number, legibly on an external surface, affixed so that it stays on for the whole of each operation. Back to the car picture: the plate goes on the outside, readable, and it must not fall off partway through the trip.

**Piece 10: a recreational registration is not a Part 107 registration.**

A registration issued for exclusively recreational flying covers all of that owner's recreational aircraft at once. FAA guidance is plain, though: an aircraft registered that way cannot be flown under Part 107.

**Put it together with a worked example.**

A photographer buys a 0.5-pound camera drone to shoot real-estate listings.

- Is it under 0.55 pounds? Yes. Tempting, but keep going.
- Is the flight exclusively recreational? No, it is commercial work under Part 107. So the weight exception does not apply.
- Conclusion: it must be registered before its first Part 107 flight. The photographer registers it on FAA DroneZone for $5.00, receives a certificate valid for 3 years, and writes the registration number on the outside of the aircraft.

**The checklist to carry.**

1. Flying under Part 107? Register it, any weight.
2. One aircraft per registration, $5.00, good for 3 years.
3. Register in the owner's legal name; someone at least 13 does it.
4. Mark the number legibly on an external surface, firmly attached.
5. Anything changes? Update within 14 calendar days.
