Objective: know when night begins and ends, and the two conditions for flying at night.

Night is defined in 14 CFR 1.1 as the time between the end of evening civil twilight and the beginning of morning civil twilight, as published in the Air Almanac and converted to local time. Outside Alaska, evening civil twilight ends 30 minutes after official sunset and morning civil twilight begins 30 minutes before official sunrise, so night is the period between those two points.

Section 107.29(a) allows operation at night only when two conditions are met. First, the remote pilot in command has completed an initial knowledge test or training, as applicable, under 107.65 after April 6, 2021. AC 107-2A explains that the test and the recurrent training updated on that date include night physiology and night visual illusions. Second, the aircraft has lighted anti-collision lighting visible for at least 3 statute miles with a flash rate sufficient to avoid a collision. The remote pilot in command may reduce the intensity, but not extinguish the light, on determining that operating conditions make that in the interest of safety; the AC gives the example of a bright strobe close to the pilot that makes the aircraft's position and attitude harder to judge. The pilot may rely on the manufacturer's statement that the light is visible for 3 statute miles, but remains responsible for verifying that it works, is visible for 3 statute miles, and flashes at a sufficient rate at the operating location. Under 107.29(d), night waivers issued under the old rule ended, and no night operation may rely on a certificate of waiver issued before April 21, 2021. The lighting requirement can be waived under 107.205.

The AC adds a warning about line of sight at night. Reduced light and contrast make it harder to judge the aircraft's location, attitude, altitude, and direction, so the pilot may need to restrict the operating area, and may not rely on the anti-collision light or telemetry displays alone to satisfy 107.31.

Worked example: a pilot certificated in 2019 who has done no recurrent training since may not fly at night. After completing recurrent training, the pilot may fly at night with an anti-collision light visible for 3 statute miles, and may dim it if its glare makes the aircraft harder to see.

Common misreading: thinking a waiver is still needed to fly at night. Since 2021 no waiver is needed when the two conditions are met, and the old night waivers have ended.
